# Every Greeting Used to Be a Real Test. The Zen Word "Ichi Ai Issatsu" (一挨一拶).

The Japanese word for an everyday greeting, *aisatsu* (挨拶), gets used dozens of times a day without anyone thinking about it. It comes from a phrase that meant something much closer to an exam.

## Contact, not conversation

*Ichi ai issatsu* (一挨一拶) is where the word comes from. The two characters inside it, 挨 and 拶, are usually glossed as "to push" and "to press in on" — accounts of the exact nuance differ, but the shared idea is forceful, physical contact, not a polite exchange. Chan writing used the phrase for what happened when a teacher and a student, or two monks meeting each other, tested one another through a word, a gesture, a single sharp question. Nobody was being greeted. Somebody was being read.

## Accuracy, not warmth or hostility

The point of the exchange wasn't warmth and wasn't hostility either. It was accuracy. A teacher meeting a student wanted to know, immediately and without ceremony, how deep the student's understanding actually went — not how the student described it, not how confident he sounded, but what showed up the instant he was pushed. The push was diagnostic. Whatever came back either held up or it didn't, and there wasn't much room to fake it either way. A confident answer that wobbled under the second question told you as much as a hesitant one that held its ground.

## The passage the dictionaries cite

Japanese dictionaries trace the word to Yuanwu Keqin's¹ introduction to Case 23 of the *Blue Cliff Record*² (碧巌録), compiled in 1125, where he writes that among monks, "a word, a phrase, a moment, a setting, a step out, a step in, a push and a press" are all meant to reveal depth or shallowness, meant to reveal which way someone is actually facing. Whether this is the oldest surviving use of the phrase in Chan writing isn't something I can confirm — only that it's the passage dictionaries point to. It's also a general description of how testing worked, not a summary of the case itself, which is a separate exchange: one monk, out walking in the hills, points and says the summit of the Wondrous Peak is right here, and his companion answers, "True enough — and what a pity."

## How a test became a nicety

Somewhere between a twelfth-century monastery and a modern hallway, the word softened completely. The forcing, testing sense faded out over the centuries that followed, and what was left was the outer shape: two people, meeting, exchanging something. Nobody planned the change, and there's no single moment you can point to where the meaning flipped. It simply drifted, the way a word worn smooth by daily use tends to. By the time *aisatsu* settled into its current meaning, the exchange itself had gone almost entirely ceremonial. What survived was the form of contact. What disappeared was the requirement that anything real pass between the two people making it.

## A script too short to hear yourself say

Workplace greetings mostly run on a script short enough to say without listening to yourself say it: "How's it going." "Good, you?" "Good." Nobody involved learns anything about anyone, and nobody expects to. It isn't a failure of etiquette. The ritual serves people well precisely because it lets two people pass each other without either one having to actually show up. The oldest form demanded the opposite: showing up was the entire content of the exchange.

## The two seconds it would actually take

Treating an ordinary greeting the old way is a small, specific act: noticing, for the two seconds available, what actually comes back when you ask — a genuinely tired "fine," a distracted one, one that doesn't match the face saying it — rather than letting the reply pass through you unregistered on its way to whatever you were already thinking about. It doesn't require interrogating your coworkers in the elevator, and it takes no extra time. The greeting was already going to happen; the only variable is whether anyone was actually present for it. The attention this takes is available to almost everyone, and almost everyone spends it on autopilot instead, dozens of times a day, without ever deciding to.

## In closing

The word gets said so often, by so many people, so many times a day, that it stopped requiring anyone's attention a long time ago, which is exactly the opposite of what it was built for. Somewhere underneath the modern nicety is still the older instruction: when you meet someone, actually meet them, even for a second, even if nobody else in the room would notice the difference.
