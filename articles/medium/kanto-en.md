# A Monk Who'd Already Reached the Top Asked What Came Next. The Teacher Named Two Rivers. The Zen Koan "Hyakushaku Kanto" (百尺竿頭進一歩).

A monk who has already done the hard work — reached what the tradition calls the top of a hundred-foot pole, the summit of formal attainment — asks his teacher the obvious next question: how do you go any further from here? There's nowhere left to climb. The teacher doesn't offer a technique, a stage beyond this one, or a warning about complacency. He names two rivers in two towns the monk could walk to by evening.

## Already at the top, and asking what's next

The teacher was Changsha Jingcen¹, a disciple of Nanquan Puyuan and, by that lineage, an elder dharma-brother of Zhaozhou — one of the major teaching figures of Tang-dynasty Hunan. The image behind the exchange is deliberately impossible: a hundred-foot pole has no rung above its top, and no ordinary step forward from there is a step at all — it's a fall. That's the whole difficulty the monk is naming. He hasn't failed to arrive. He's arrived, and arrival turns out not to be where the problem ends.

## One verse, two different signatures

The record on this one splits in a way that's worth following closely. The earliest source, the *Jingde Chuandenglu* (景徳伝灯録, compiled 1004), records a verse directly inside Changsha's own biographical entry: "The one who sits unmoving at the top of a hundred-foot pole has entered, but it isn't yet real. From the top of the pole, you must take a step — and the ten directions become your whole body." Case 46 of the *Mumonkan* (無門関), compiled more than two centuries later in 1228 by Wumen Huikai, quotes a version of the same verse — with the wording shifted slightly — but attributes it only to "an ancient worthy," alongside a separate question from a different teacher, Shishuang Qingzhu²: "At the top of a hundred-foot pole, how do you take a step forward?" By the time the koan reached its most-cited form, the words had outlived the name attached to them.

## Two rivers instead of an answer

The fuller scene — someone actually asking Changsha this, in person, and getting an answer — survives separately, as Case 79 of the *Congronglu* (従容録, compiled 1223–24). A monk puts the question to him directly: from the top of the pole, how do you take a step forward? Changsha replies: "The mountains of Langzhou, the waters of Lizhou." The monk is baffled — two real places, named as if answering a geography question. Changsha adds one more line: "The four seas, the five lakes, all inside the reach of one governance." He isn't describing a higher rung of the pole. He's describing the ground the monk was standing on before he ever started climbing, still there, still real, waiting on the other side of the question.

## The height you stop climbing at work

- **The expert who's out of moves.** Someone masters a domain completely enough that every remaining problem starts to feel like a variation on ones they've already solved — and the growth quietly stops, not from laziness, but from having run out of the kind of problem that got them here.
- **The leader promoted past their own feedback loop.** Early in a career, mistakes get corrected fast and visibly. At a certain height, the correction slows down or stops arriving at all, and "how do I get better" stops having an obvious next answer.
- **The title mistaken for the destination.** Reaching a long-sought role or milestone and discovering the actual question — what do I do with this — was never covered by the plan that got you there.

Changsha's answer works because it refuses to treat "further" as more of the same climb. The next move, for the person who's genuinely run out of rungs, usually isn't a harder version of the skill that got them up there. It's turning around and noticing there's an entire, ordinary world at the bottom of the pole that the climbing never had time to include.

## The step past your own hundred feet

The same shape shows up privately, in whatever a person has spent years becoming good at — a craft, a reputation, a way of being reliably right. Once you're actually at the top of that particular pole, "improve" stops meaning what it used to mean, and the honest next move rarely looks like more discipline applied to the same climb. It looks more like Changsha's answer: naming something plain and real that has nothing to do with the pole at all, and finding out the world was still there the whole time you were busy climbing.

## In closing

There wasn't a beyond in the shape the monk's question assumed, and Changsha didn't pretend otherwise. What he offered instead were rivers, real ones, in towns a person could actually walk to by evening. The four seas and five lakes had been within reach the entire time the monk was climbing — he just hadn't had a reason to notice, until the climbing itself ran out of room.
