# A Monk Who'd Already Reached the Top Asked What Came Next. The Teacher Named a Mountain and a River. The Zen Koan "Hyakushaku Kanto" (百尺竿頭進一歩).

A monk who has already done the hard work — reached what the tradition calls the top of a hundred-foot pole, the summit of formal attainment — asks his teacher the obvious next question: how do you go any further from here? There's nowhere left to climb. The teacher doesn't offer a technique, a stage beyond this one, or a warning about complacency. He names a mountain range and a river, real places in the region around them.

## Already at the top, and asking what's next

Changsha Jingcen¹ answered him — a dharma-brother of Zhaozhou under Nanquan Puyuan, and one of the major teaching figures of Tang-dynasty Hunan. The image behind the exchange is deliberately impossible: a hundred-foot pole has no rung above its top, and no ordinary step forward from there is a step at all — it's a fall. That's the whole difficulty the monk is naming. He hasn't failed to arrive. He's arrived, and arrival turns out not to be where the problem ends.

A simple case for climbing higher would have ended right there. This one keeps going because the climbing itself is eventually what gets questioned.

## A record that had the fuller answer all along

The record on this one is worth following closely, because the most-cited version of the koan turns out to be the thinnest one. The earliest source, the *Jingde Chuandenglu* (景徳伝灯録, compiled 1004), records both a verse and a full exchange together, inside Changsha's own biographical entry. The verse: "The one who sits unmoving at the top of a hundred-foot pole has entered, but it isn't yet real. From the top of the pole, you must take a step — and the ten directions become your whole body." Directly alongside it, in the same entry: a monk asks Changsha exactly that question in person — from the top of the pole, how do you take a step forward? — and Changsha answers.

Case 46 of the *Mumonkan* (無門関), compiled in 1228 by Wumen Huikai², takes only the verse — wording shifted slightly — strips off Changsha's name, and attributes it instead to "an ancient worthy," pairing it with a separate question from a different teacher, Shishuang Qingzhu³. The scene, and the teacher who actually answered it, both disappear in the version most people now know. Case 79 of the *Congronglu* (従容録), compiled a few years earlier still, in 1223–24 by Wansong Xingxiu⁴, keeps the attribution and gives the full exchange its own formal koan case — closer to the original record than the more famous one that followed it.

The more famous a line becomes, it turns out, the more it's worth tracing back to where it actually came from.

## A mountain and a river instead of an abstraction

Here's what he actually says, in full. Changsha replies: "The mountains of Langzhou, the waters of Lizhou." The monk doesn't follow — real, specific places, named as if answering a geography question instead of a question about enlightenment. Changsha adds one more line: "The four seas, the five lakes, all inside the reach of one governance." He isn't describing a higher rung of the pole. He's describing the ground the monk was already standing on before he ever started climbing — ordinary, regional, nameable — still there, still real, on the other side of a question that had gone looking for something more exalted.

That's what keeps this from reading as a plain case for trying harder.

## The height you stop climbing at work

- **The expert who's out of moves.** Someone masters a domain completely enough that every remaining problem starts to feel like a variation on ones they've already solved — and the growth quietly stops, not from laziness, but from having run out of the kind of problem that got them here.
- **The leader promoted past their own feedback loop.** Early in a career, mistakes get corrected fast and visibly. At a certain height, the correction slows down or stops arriving at all, and "how do I get better" stops having an obvious next answer.
- **The title mistaken for the destination.** Reaching a long-sought role or milestone and discovering the actual question — what do I do with this — was never covered by the plan that got you there.

Changsha's answer works because it refuses to treat "further" as more of the same climb. The next move, for the person who's genuinely run out of rungs, usually isn't a harder version of the skill that got them up there. It's turning around and noticing there's an entire, ordinary world at the bottom of the pole that the climbing never had time to include.

## The step past your own hundred feet

Whatever you've spent years becoming good at — a craft, a reputation, a way of being reliably right — eventually puts you at the top of your own particular pole. Once you're actually there, "improve" stops meaning what it used to mean, and the honest next move rarely looks like more discipline applied to the same climb. It looks more like Changsha's answer: naming something plain and real that has nothing to do with the pole at all, and finding out the world was still there the whole time you were busy climbing.

"Further" carries real force while you're still growing. The trouble starts only once it becomes the only direction you know how to look.

## In closing

There wasn't a beyond in the shape the monk's question assumed, and Changsha didn't pretend otherwise. What he offered instead was a mountain and a river, real ones, in a region he could have pointed to. The four seas and five lakes had been within reach the entire time the monk was climbing — he just hadn't had a reason to notice, until the climbing itself ran out of room.
