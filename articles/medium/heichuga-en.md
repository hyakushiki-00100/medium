# A Man Asked How to Free a Goose Without Breaking Its Bottle. The Teacher Just Called His Name. The Zen Koan "Heichuga" (瓶中鵝).

Lu Gen¹, a government official, put a classic riddle to Nanquan²: someone raised a goose inside a bottle, and it grew too large to get back out. Without breaking the bottle and without harming the goose, how would you free it? Nanquan sat with the question a moment. Then he called out sharply: "Grand Master Lu!" Lu Gen answered, startled: "Yes?" Nanquan said: "There. It's out."

## The trap was never the bottle

Every version of this riddle assumes the goose has to be extracted from inside a sealed container, which is exactly the frame that makes it unsolvable — you can't shrink a goose, and the two allowed exits are both forbidden. Lu Gen had spent who knows how long turning that impossible shape over in his mind before he brought it to Nanquan, and by the time he asked it out loud, his own attention was doing the same thing the goose was doing: sealed inside a container it had built for itself, too large now to fit back through the terms of its own question. Nanquan didn't answer the riddle. He said a name, and something in Lu Gen answered before it could check whether answering was allowed. That response was already outside every wall the puzzle had built. It always had been.

## An eighty-year-old teacher, a newly arrived student

Nanquan³ was in his eighties by the time this exchange is usually placed, not long after Lu Gen — a provincial governor said to have arrived with an armed escort and installed himself as a lay student — first sought him out, around 827. Both men appear together repeatedly in the surviving record; the earliest source that plausibly preserves this particular exchange is Nanquan's chapter in the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁴, compiled in 952, which records several of their conversations, though the exact wording of this exchange there isn't independently confirmed. It's also preserved in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁵, compiled in 1004, and became one of the most widely retold exchanges attributed to Nanquan in the centuries after.

## A knot handled the same way, half a world away

Centuries before this exchange and thousands of miles west, a wagon in Phrygia was said to be bound to its post by a knot so elaborate that no one could find either end of the rope, and a prophecy held that whoever undid it would rule all of Asia. Alexander is supposed to have looked at it once, drawn his sword, and cut straight through. The trick in both stories is identical: the knot and the goose were only impossible under the assumption that the correct move had to happen inside the terms the puzzle set. Refuse the terms, and there's nothing left to solve.

## The constraint nobody agreed to accept

A team gets handed a mandate that sounds, on inspection, a lot like the goose: grow the number without adding headcount, without changing the product, without touching the price. Weeks get spent hunting for the clever configuration that satisfies all three at once, because the constraints arrived bundled together and nobody thought to ask whether they actually had to be solved as a set. Sometimes they do. Often the more useful move is checking which of the three walls was ever actually load-bearing, instead of continuing to measure a goose that was never going to fit through any of the openings it was offered.

## Called back to yourself by name

People carry a private form of the same puzzle: how to become genuinely confident without ever risking the failure that confidence is usually built out of, how to be fully known by someone without exposing the parts that might not be liked. The mind can spend years turning that shape over, adding detail to the bottle instead of questioning whether the goose needs to stay inside it. What breaks the loop is rarely a clever answer produced from within it. More often it's something ordinary — someone saying your name, a task that needs your hands right now — that gets a response before the part of you still solving the puzzle has time to object. The response was never trapped. Only the question believed it was.

## In closing

Lu Gen came with a riddle built to have no exit, and Nanquan didn't find one. He asked for something the sealed-in version of Lu Gen couldn't refuse to give — an answer to his own name — and the moment it came, there was nothing left inside any bottle to explain.
