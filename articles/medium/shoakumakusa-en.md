# A Monk Sat in a Tree. A Governor Called It Dangerous. The Monk Disagreed. The Zen Teaching "Shoaku Makusa" (諸悪莫作).

Bai Juyi¹, the newly appointed governor of Hangzhou, went to see a monk who lived in a pine tree. He found him sitting on a branch, high enough that a fall would have killed him. "Master," Bai Juyi said, "your position looks extremely dangerous." The monk, known to everyone as Bird's Nest², wasn't impressed. "Governor," he said, "yours is far more dangerous than mine." Bai Juyi laughed him off: he governed the rivers and mountains of an entire region — what danger could there possibly be in that?

## The fire nobody else could see

Bird's Nest answered with an old pairing: firewood and flame, entangled the instant they touch; the mind's habitual churning, which never once stops. That, he said, was the real danger. Bai Juyi, newly powerful and surrounded by ambition he'd long since stopped noticing as risk, had no branch under him at all — he simply couldn't see the fire he was already standing in.

He wasn't finished yet. He asked the monk directly: what is the great meaning of the Buddhist teaching? Bird's Nest answered with two lines already old by then³: do no evil, do all good. Bai Juyi, one of the most celebrated poets of his age, laughed again. "Even a three-year-old knows that." Bird's Nest didn't argue the point. "A three-year-old can say it," he answered. "An eighty-year-old man still can't do it."

## A story two sources tell two different ways

This exchange, in the form most people know it, comes from the *Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp*⁴ (景徳伝灯録), compiled around 1004. An earlier surviving source, the *Patriarch's Hall Anthology*⁵ (祖堂集, c. 952), tells a plainer version: no governor, no tree-top danger exchange, just a direct question about how to practice, the same two-line answer, and the retort aimed at a hundred-year-old man instead of an eighty-year-old one. The livelier, more quotable version — the one with a title, a cliff-edge, and a specific number of years — is the one later compilers built on top of it.

Worth adding: the *Jingde Record* itself places this meeting in the Yuanhe era (806–820) — years when Bai Juyi wasn't yet governor of Hangzhou at all; that post came later, in 822–824. Even the source telling the fuller story can't get its own timeline straight, which is usually a sign that a good story has been assembled around two real names rather than recorded as it happened.

## Two ways of naming the same gap

Both of Bird's Nest's answers are aimed at the same blind spot from different sides. The danger Bai Juyi could see — a monk on a branch — was obvious and irrelevant. The danger he couldn't see — appetite, status, the ordinary churn of wanting — was the one actually capable of doing damage, and it felt too familiar to register as risk at all. The doctrine he dismissed as trivial had the identical shape: not too hard to understand, so easy nobody bothers to check whether they're actually living it.

## The three-year-old's answer, decades later

Most professional wisdom clears this same low bar. Give feedback while it's still useful instead of saving it for a review months later. Listen instead of drafting your reply while the other person is still talking. Decline the ninth commitment before it lands, instead of the first one that would have made the other eight sustainable.

Nobody in the room would argue with any of it, and that's exactly the tell. The moment a piece of guidance stops sounding insightful and starts sounding obvious is the moment worth pausing on — not to doubt the advice, but to check whether it's actually been done this week, or only nodded at again.

## A private ledger nobody would ever contest

There's a quieter version of the same habit. Sleep enough. Move the body daily. Say the hard thing to the person who needs to hear it instead of to everyone else first. None of it is contested. All of it was old news by the time anyone was out of their twenties. The knowing arrived decades ago and has been sitting there ever since, mistaken for the doing, because revisiting it feels like nothing new to learn.

## In closing

Bird's Nest didn't say the teaching was hard to grasp. He said it was hard to carry out, and offered a lifetime as his evidence. The branch under him was never really the question. The real one hasn't changed since: what does a person keep mistaking for solid ground, decades after first hearing exactly how not to.
