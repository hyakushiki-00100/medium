# His Answer Was the Wealth He Said He Lacked. The Zen Case "Seizei Kohin" (清税孤貧).

A monk named Qingshui told Caoshan¹, "I'm destitute. Please help me." Caoshan said, "Qingshui." "Yes?" Qingshui answered, at once. Caoshan said, "You've already drunk three cups of the finest wine in Quanzhou, and you're telling me your lips are still dry."

## A complaint answered before it was finished

Qingshui didn't get a lecture about wealth or poverty, and he didn't get turned away. He got called by his own name, and he answered — immediately, completely, without checking first whether he felt ready or worthy. That response was the whole transaction. Nothing about it required preparation, credentials, or a rehearsed opening line. Caoshan wasn't being coy about serving the wine later. He was pointing at the cup Qingshui had just finished, the one Qingshui had emptied without noticing he'd been handed it.

## Not a request for money

"I'm destitute, help me" is a familiar opening in this kind of exchange, and it isn't really a request for money. It's a request for instruction — for something to fill a gap the speaker believed was there. The gap was the whole premise. Caoshan's answer doesn't dispute that such a gap can exist in general. It just refuses to let this particular claim stand unexamined, because the proof of lack — a hesitant, uncertain response — never showed up. What showed up instead, seconds earlier, was full presence, on the first syllable of his own name.

## Recorded as a formal case before it was widely known

This exchange is preserved as Case 10 of the *Gateless Gate*² (無門関), compiled in 1228. Caoshan's own biography appears earlier, in fascicle 17 of the *Transmission of the Lamp*³ (景徳伝灯録, 1004), and this exchange very likely appears there too — though I wasn't able to check the primary Chinese text directly to confirm it, so I'm citing the *Gateless Gate* as the version I could actually verify.

## A poverty with too much pride in it

Wumen's verse on this case pairs two figures from Chinese history: poor as Fan Dan⁴, proud as Xiang Yu⁵. Fan Dan was a byword for scraping by without complaint; Xiang Yu was a general remembered for a pride so fierce it refused to bend even in defeat, right up to his final battle. Putting the two together isn't a compliment and isn't quite an insult either. Wumen isn't praising Qingshui's poverty. He's naming the contradiction in it: a complaint about lack, delivered with the bearing of someone who has never once doubted his own standing enough to actually sound poor. Real poverty, the verse implies, doesn't usually announce itself this confidently.

## The gap and the competence arrive together

People say things like "I don't really know what I'm doing here" while asking a question phrased with more precision than most of the answers they'll get back. The gap they're naming and the competence they're demonstrating arrive in the same sentence, and almost nobody catches the second half. Managers do this constantly with new responsibilities: "I've never run a team this size" said in a tone that already sounds exactly like someone running one — reading the room correctly, deciding what to raise and what to let go, adjusting mid-sentence when a face in the meeting changes. That's competent handling of a real situation, mislabeled as beginner's luck only because the label showed up before the evidence did.

## Alone, the same math gets worse

Most people carry a running account of what they haven't earned yet — the confidence they're still waiting to feel, the competence they assume real experts have and they don't. Meanwhile the actual evidence, the steady, immediate, unhesitating way they already handle most of what's in front of them, gets filed separately, as if it doesn't count toward the total. A difficult conversation handled calmly this morning, a decision made without agonizing over it, a friend's crisis met with the right words on the first try — none of it gets credited toward the account it's actually paying into. It counts. It was never a separate ledger to begin with.

## In closing

Qingshui asked to be given something, and the giving had already happened before he finished the sentence. Caoshan didn't correct his math. He just refused to pretend the cup was still empty when it so obviously wasn't. Most complaints about not having enough survive on the same arithmetic error — counting only the wanting, and forgetting to count the wine that's already been drunk.

¹ Caoshan Benji (曹山本寂): 840–901, a dharma heir of Dongshan Liangjie and, with him, a founder of the Caodong (Soto) school.
² The *Gateless Gate* (無門関, Ch. *Wumenguan*), Case 10, compiled by Wumen Huikai (無門慧開, 1183–1260) in 1228.
³ The *Transmission of the Lamp* (景徳伝灯録), compiled by Daoyuan in 1004; Caoshan Benji's biography appears in fascicle 17.
⁴ Fan Dan (范丹, also 范冉): a Later Han recluse proverbial for living in extreme poverty without complaint.
⁵ Xiang Yu (項羽): the Chu general and rival of the Han founder Liu Bang, remembered for a fierce, unyielding pride even in defeat.
