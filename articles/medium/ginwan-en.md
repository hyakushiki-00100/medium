# A Monk Asked About Kanadeva's Teaching. The Teacher Answered: Snow, Piled Inside a Silver Bowl. The Zen Phrase "Ginwanri ni Yuki o Moru" (銀椀裏盛雪).

A monk asked Baling¹, "What is the teaching of Kanadeva²?" Baling answered: "Snow, piled inside a silver bowl."

## Close enough to blur, not close enough to merge

Snow in a silver bowl isn't a puzzle with a hidden mechanism. There's no trick to uncover, no seam where the metal ends and the snow begins — silver and snow sit close enough in brightness that the eye has trouble finding the line between them. Nothing is hidden. The bowl is right there, holding the snow in plain sight, and the answer still doesn't resolve into something you can point at. Tradition reads this pairing both ways: alike enough to blur together, and still, in principle, distinguishable to someone looking closely enough. What draws attention here is the harder half of that — resemblance so complete that finding the seam becomes the whole task. Kanadeva was known as a debater so exact that his distinctions left no gap for a counterargument to enter, and one tradition holds he gave up an eye out of compassion, to someone who needed one and asked. A teaching that precise doesn't announce itself with contrast. It sits inside its own description so completely that nothing is left standing outside it for comparison.

## A pair of earlier answers, and a teacher who needed nothing else

Baling³ was a student of Yunmen Wenyan⁴, active in the tenth century; no birth or death dates are recorded. Along with the silver bowl, he gave two more answers, on separate occasions, that became known together as his three turning phrases: asked what the Way is, he said, "A clear-eyed man falls into a well." Asked about a sword sharp enough to sever a hair blown against its edge, he said, "Every branch of the coral holds up the moon." Yunmen was reportedly so pleased with all three that he told Baling reciting them alone, on the anniversary of his death, would be enough. The earliest confirmable form of the silver-bowl exchange is Xuedou Chongxian's verse collection (c. 1038); Yuanwu Keqin's added commentary, compiled as the Blue Cliff Record⁵ in 1125, is the more widely read version, as Case 13. Baling has his own chapter in the earlier Jingde Chuandenglu (1004), though whether this exchange appears there wasn't something this piece could confirm. The image itself may not have started with Baling: a closely related line also appears in the Baojing Sanmei, a verse traditionally attributed to the earlier teacher Dongshan Liangjie — though the earliest surviving text of that verse postdates the Blue Cliff Record, so which came first isn't something the record settles.

## Disguised, not hidden

In one of Edgar Allan Poe's detective stories, a government minister has stolen a compromising letter from a member of the royal family and is using it to hold power over her. The police search his residence for months — carpets pulled up, floorboards inspected under a magnifying glass — because they assume he's hidden it somewhere clever. The detective finds it on a single visit: refolded, re-sealed, re-addressed in a different hand, hanging openly in a card rack on the mantelpiece. It hadn't been hidden. It had been disguised, made to look like something nobody in the room was searching for. The silver bowl works on the same principle: whoever goes looking for the teaching expects it to stand out from its surroundings, and it doesn't, because standing out was never the point.

## Work with the seams sanded off

A heavily revised document, done well, doesn't read like a revised document. The clumsy transition, the paragraph that used to justify its own existence, the sentence doing three jobs at once — all of it gets replaced, and what's left doesn't show where the replacing happened. Someone reading the final version has no way to tell it apart from a draft that came out right the first time, which is exactly the outcome the work was aiming for. The effort is real, and the evidence of it is designed to disappear. That isn't the work going unnoticed. It's the work succeeding at the one thing it was actually trying to do.

## A change with no seam left to show

Real change in how someone reacts — less defensive, less quick to take the bait, steadier under the kind of remark that used to land — doesn't usually announce itself with a visible turning point. There's no scene where the old reaction and the new one appear side by side so the difference can be admired. There's just the new way of responding, arriving so completely that a year later it's hard to recall the exact shape of the thing it replaced. That isn't evidence the change cost nothing. It's what the finished version of that kind of work actually looks like from the inside: nothing left over to point to.

## In closing

Nothing about a silver bowl of snow explains itself when you first hear it. That was never the failure in the exchange. The teaching Baling was pointing to doesn't stand apart from its container to prove it's there — it fills the shape completely, the way the finest work fills its own occasion so exactly that afterward there's nothing left over to call attention to it.

¹ Baling Haojian (巴陵顥鑑): a dharma heir of Yunmen Wenyan, active in the tenth century (Five Dynasties period); no birth or death dates are recorded.
² Kanadeva (迦那提婆, Aryadeva): a third-century disciple of Nagarjuna, patriarch of the Madhyamaka lineage, and traditionally counted as the fifteenth patriarch in Chan's own list of Indian ancestors — sometimes confused with the unrelated Devadatta (提婆達多), a rival of the Buddha.
³ See note 1.
⁴ Yunmen Wenyan (雲門文偃): 864–949; founder of the Yunmen school of Chan, discussed elsewhere in this series.
⁵ Blue Cliff Record (碧巌録): based on Xuedou Chongxian's verse collection (c. 1038), with Yuanwu Keqin's commentary added and compiled as a single work in 1125.

*Sources: the exchange between an unnamed monk and Baling Haojian (巴陵顥鑑) over the teaching of Kanadeva (迦那提婆), first confirmable in Xuedou Chongxian's verse collection (c. 1038) and more widely known as Case 13 of the Blue Cliff Record (碧巌録), compiled with Yuanwu Keqin's commentary in 1125; Baling's other two "turning phrases" and Yunmen Wenyan's (雲門文偃, 864–949) praise of them, recorded in the same collection; a closely related line in the Baojing Sanmei, traditionally attributed to Dongshan Liangjie (洞山良价, 807–869), whose relative dating to the Baling exchange can't be established from the surviving texts; biographical notes distinguishing Kanadeva (Aryadeva), a Madhyamaka patriarch, from Devadatta, a rival of the Buddha, given the two names' frequent confusion; Edgar Allan Poe's short story "The Purloined Letter," in which a stolen letter goes undetected by being disguised and left in open view, as a structurally similar account from the Western tradition.*
