# Not Arguing Back Is the Easy Half. The Zen Koan "Tokusan Takuhatsu" (徳山托鉢).

Deshan Xuanjian was one of the roughest teachers of his generation — half of the phrase "Deshan's stick, Linji's shout" belongs to him, earned by striking students whether or not they got the answer right. One day he walked down to the dining hall carrying his bowls, ahead of the bell. A young monk stopped him with a single question. Deshan turned around and went back to his room without saying a word.

## A question that needed no answer

Xuefeng Yicun¹ was, at the time, still a student under Deshan Xuanjian², one of the sharpest and most demanding teachers in Tang-dynasty Chan. That day, Deshan came down toward the hall with his begging bowls before the bell had rung or the drum had sounded. Xuefeng asked him plainly: the signal for the meal hasn't gone out yet — where are you going with those? Deshan didn't argue, didn't explain himself, and didn't wait to be asked twice. He turned around and walked back to his room.

## An unexpected verdict

Xuefeng mentioned the exchange to a fellow student, Yantou Quanhuo³. Yantou's response wasn't approval of Deshan's grace under correction. It was a sharper judgment: as great as Deshan is, he said, the old man still hasn't grasped "the last word."⁴

## Deshan sends for him

Word of Yantou's comment reached Deshan. He sent for him and asked directly: don't you approve of this old monk? Yantou told him privately what he meant — the text never records what was actually said. The next day, when Deshan gave his lecture to the assembly, it was, just as Yantou had implied it would be, unlike his usual teaching. Yantou walked to the front of the monks' hall, clapped his hands, and laughed. Wonderful, he said. The old man has finally gotten hold of the last word. From now on, nobody in the world can do a thing with him.

## The applause Wumen refused to join

That would make a tidy story on its own — a hard old master takes correction well and comes back sharper for it. Where the exchange was first written down isn't clear; the earliest source that survives in a form anyone can point to is Wumen Huikai⁵'s *Gateless Gate*⁶, compiled well over three centuries after the events it describes. And Wumen, setting the case down, wasn't willing to let it stay tidy. If that really was the last word, he wrote, then neither Yantou nor Deshan has even dreamed of it — examined closely, the whole scene is nothing more than a shelf of puppets. Some readings take that line as Wumen rejecting the whole exchange outright. At minimum, though, he's refusing to let a reader nodding along at Yantou's applause treat a satisfying ending as proof that something real happened underneath it.

## Two kinds of correction

The koan actually stages two different tests, back to back. The first is whether Deshan can be told he's out of step, by a junior, and simply absorb it — no defending the bowls, the timing, or his own seniority. He passes that one instantly, wordlessly. The second test is harder, and Xuefeng isn't the one administering it: it's Yantou's verdict, delivered behind Deshan's back to someone else entirely, that wordless composure alone doesn't settle anything. Not reacting badly to being corrected is table stakes. Whether the correction actually landed anywhere is a separate question, answered later, if at all, by what someone does with it rather than by how gracefully they took it in the moment.

## The gap between not arguing and actually hearing it

Someone flags a real problem with a plan, a piece of work, a decision already made. The healthiest-looking response is the one Deshan gave: no defensiveness, no explaining it away, correction taken on the spot. That response is necessary, and on its own it still proves nothing. The harder version of the same moment happens later, mostly out of view: going back to the person who raised it, asking what they actually meant beyond the specific complaint, and letting the answer change something about how the next piece of work gets done. Plenty of people are excellent at the first part and quietly skip the second, and the difference between them stays invisible right up until it isn't.

## When you've made the visible change

Something painful gets pointed out, a habit gets addressed, and the next version of you does look different almost right away — calmer, more careful, saying the thing you used to swallow. It's tempting to treat that visible difference as the finish line, the way Yantou's applause treats Deshan's changed lecture as proof of arrival. Wumen's refusal to join the applause is worth borrowing here too: a changed performance is evidence something might have shifted underneath it, not proof of it. The only way to find out is to keep going back to whatever actually corrected you, long after the scene has stopped looking dramatic enough to clap for.

## In closing

Deshan turned around without a word, and that wasn't the end of the story — Yantou made sure of that, and Wumen made sure the ending didn't get to feel finished either. What's left, centuries later, is a harder question than a formula for taking criticism well, aimed past the moment someone claps: what actually happened to the thing you were told, once the room went back to normal.
