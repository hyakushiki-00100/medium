# Two Monks Gave Opposite Answers. The Teacher Gave Them the Same One. The Zen Word "Kissako" (喫茶去).

A monk arrives at a temple in Tang China and is asked a single question: have you been here before? He says yes. "Go have some tea," the teacher tells him. A second monk arrives and is asked the same question. He says no, he hasn't. "Go have some tea," the teacher tells him too. The temple's administrator, listening, can't make sense of it — isn't the entire point of the question to sort one answer from the other? He asks why both men got the identical instruction. The teacher calls out his name. The administrator answers, "Yes?" "Go have some tea," the teacher tells him as well.

## Forty years in one temple

The teacher was Zhaozhou Congshen (778–897), known in Japan as Jōshū, one of the most quoted figures in the Chan record. He didn't settle into a temple of his own until he was eighty, after decades testing himself against other teachers, then taught for roughly forty years at Guanyin Temple, on the edge of what's now a county town in Hebei province — plain, flat farm country, not the mountain retreat the setting might suggest. The exchange appears in the *Record of Zhaozhou* and later in the *Wudeng Huiyuan* (五灯会元), a Song-dynasty compilation finished in 1252 — more than three centuries after he died.

Worth being honest: exchanges like this were written down generations later, in a genre built for compression — a few lines standing in for a longer, messier conversation. Even the final character carries a live dispute. The popular reading — the one carried into tea-ceremony practice — takes *qu* (去) as a bare emphatic particle, so the line lands as a plain "have some tea." More recent philological work argues the phrase originally meant something closer to "go drink tea and wake yourself up" — a rebuke of dullness, not an invitation. Either way, the core of the exchange doesn't move: welcome or wake-up call, Zhaozhou gave the same one to both monks and to the man questioning him.

## What the question was actually testing

The natural assumption is that Zhaozhou's opening question sorted the two monks — the returning visitor gets one kind of welcome, the newcomer another. That's what a question like that usually does: gather information, then calibrate. His answer refuses the calibration. Whatever story each monk had just told about himself, the actual offer — sit down, be here, have some tea — stayed identical. The category they'd supplied turned out to be irrelevant to what they actually needed from him.

## A teacher other teachers got measured against

Zhaozhou's contemporaries taught through dramatic interruption. Linji used the shout this series covers separately as *katsu* — itself a more precise instrument than a raised voice, but still a sudden, physical jolt; Deshan hit students with a stick hard enough that it became a formal teaching method. Zhaozhou used neither. He answered almost everything in plain speech — tea, a washed bowl, a cypress tree in the courtyard — and later generations nicknamed his style *kǒuchúnpí chán* (口唇皮禅), "mouth-and-lip Chan": teaching carried entirely in ordinary talk, nothing dramatic behind it. "Go have some tea" isn't an evasion. It's the whole method.

## Where the category stops mattering at work

- **The onboarding script versus the actual person.** New hires get one script, veterans get another, and the script often survives contact with someone it doesn't fit — a ten-year veteran doing this specific task for the first time, a new hire who's done it elsewhere for a decade.
- **Sorting people by their answer to one question.** "Have you used this system before?" quietly decides how much attention someone gets, when what they actually need right now may not track their history at all.
- **Treating repeat customers and new ones by formula.** Loyalty tiers and years of service are real signals, but they aren't the same thing as what the specific person in front of you actually needs.

None of this argues for treating everyone identically in every respect. It argues for checking whether the category you just sorted someone into is doing any real work, or just letting you skip the harder task of meeting them as they are.

## The tea you'd make yourself

There's a version of this aimed inward. Most people run a quiet sorting operation on their own days — a good one earns rest, a bad one earns punishment, a productive one earns permission to enjoy it. Turned on yourself, Zhaozhou's answer is less generous than it sounds and more demanding: the same basic hospitality toward your own state, regardless of which story you'd tell about how the day went. Not because the story doesn't matter. Because it isn't what determines whether you're allowed to sit down.

## In closing

Zhaozhou never explained the trick to the administrator. He didn't have to. The administrator's own question — *why* the same answer, no matter what came before it — was itself the last thing to get sorted that day, and it got the same reply as everyone else had: sit down, be here, have some tea.
