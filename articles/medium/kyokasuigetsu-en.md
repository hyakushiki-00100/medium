# Your Like Count Is a Flower in a Mirror. The Zen Phrase "Kyōka-Suigetsu."

Look into a mirror and a flower appears in it. Look at the surface of a pond and the full moon floats there.

Both are, unmistakably, visible. Color, shape, all clearly there to the eye. But reach for either one and your hand closes on nothing. You cannot pick the flower out of the mirror. You cannot scoop the moon out of the water. That image gives us the four-character phrase *kyōka-suigetsu* (鏡花水月) — "mirror-flower, water-moon."

## The source is literary criticism, not scripture

Surprisingly, the phrase's earliest clear source isn't a Zen text at all. It comes from the Ming-dynasty poet Xie Zhen's treatise on poetry, the *Shijia Zhishuo*:

"Poetry has what can be explained and what cannot — like the flower in the mirror, the moon on the water. Don't get stuck chasing after its traces."

Trace it back further and you find an earlier form, "water-moon, mirror-image," already appearing in a Tang-dynasty stele inscription by Pei Xiu. From there, through the Ming and into the Qing dynasty, the phrase widened out from a comment on poetry into a broader metaphor for the fleetingness of life itself. My own reading — and this part is interpretation, not established fact — is that Zen adopted the phrase so readily because its core stance, *don't get stuck chasing after what you can only see, never hold*, resonates directly with Zen's own teaching on non-attachment.

## Why Kinkaku-ji's pond is named "the mirror"

There's a place where you can actually stand inside this phrase. Kyōko-chi (鏡湖池) — "mirror lake pond" — is the pond in the garden of Kinkaku-ji, Kyoto's Golden Pavilion.

On a still, clear day, the golden pavilion appears whole and unbroken on the water's surface — the famous "upside-down Kinkaku." The pond is said to take its name from exactly this mirroring quality. To be clear, there's no record that the pond's name was drawn directly from the phrase *kyōka-suigetsu* itself — that connection is my own observation, not a documented etymology. But the underlying structure is identical: something beautiful, visible on the water, and completely unreachable. The perfection only shows up on windless mornings, and never lasts.

## We keep reaching for the flower in the mirror

This phrase lands hard in an age of screens.

Like counts, follower numbers, the curated slice of someone else's life scrolling past — all of it is a flower reflected in a mirror. Genuinely visible. Sometimes genuinely beautiful. None of it can be reached in and taken hold of.

Here's the tricky part: the mirror-flower isn't the problem. There's nothing wrong with finding it beautiful. The trouble starts the moment you try to grab it — reaching in, even willing to break the mirror to get at it. That's when exhaustion sets in, and eventually resentment toward the mirror itself.

The same logic applies to recognition at work. Praise, performance ratings, other people's approval — these are real, in the sense that they visibly exist. But none of them can be permanently pocketed and fixed in place. Like the moon on water, they shift with every ripple. And here's what makes it trickier still: the very effort of chasing something ungraspable often gets praised from the outside as "hustle." Which means letting go of the reach isn't something that happens automatically — it takes a real act of will to stop.

## Enjoying the reflection without grabbing for it

*Kyōka-suigetsu* isn't telling you to want nothing. It's closer to an invitation to a specific posture toward things:

- **Let what's visible be visible — nothing more.** The flower in the mirror is beautiful without being picked. The less you try to grab it, the more room there is to actually look at it.
- **Don't let un-graspability read as personal failure.** You can't scoop up the moon not because you lack skill, but because that was never in the moon's nature to begin with.
- **Anchor yourself in what does have substance.** Outside the mirror, your own hands, today's actual hours, the conversation in front of you — these are things you genuinely can hold.

## The windless morning never lasts

Kinkaku-ji's "upside-down" reflection is said to be most perfect only in the briefest of windless moments. The instant a breeze picks up, the water ripples and the reflected pavilion loses its shape.

Watching the flower in the mirror without forgetting the ground under your own feet. Loving the beauty of a moving surface without demanding it hold still forever. That's the shape of a life this old phrase seems to be quietly offering.
