# He Asked One Question. The Answer Came Later. The Zen Story "Dozan Kasui" (洞山過水).

Before his teacher Yunyan¹ died, Dongshan² asked him something practical: if someone later doubted whether he'd truly captured the man's understanding, how should he answer? Yunyan said, "Just say — this, right here, is it." Dongshan said nothing for a long time. Yunyan told him to carry the matter carefully.

## A question about carrying someone forward

The question wasn't abstract. Dongshan was about to leave, his teacher was old, and he was asking, in effect, how he was supposed to vouch for a person's understanding once that person could no longer confirm or correct him. It's the succession question in its plainest form: what do you say when you're the only one left who can speak for someone, and you're not fully sure you've got it right? Most people asking that question want a credential, some phrase they can point to later as proof they were paying attention.

## An answer that wasn't finished on delivery

Yunyan's reply sounds like a dodge if you're expecting a checklist. "This, right here, is it" names nothing in particular — no doctrine, no formula, no criteria Dongshan could measure himself against later. But Yunyan wasn't declining to answer. He was handing Dongshan a sentence that couldn't be understood yet, on the correct assumption that understanding it was a different kind of work than being told something.

## Recorded before the verse that grew out of it

This exchange survives in the *Transmission of the Lamp*³ (景徳伝灯録), compiled in 1004, which records that Dongshan later "awakened greatly upon seeing his reflection while crossing water." Earlier compilations, including the *Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall*⁴ (祖堂集, 952), confirm that Dongshan trained under Yunyan and inherited his teaching, but I wasn't able to confirm whether this specific river episode appears there too. Dongshan's own recorded sayings likely preserve a fuller account, but I couldn't establish when that collection was compiled relative to the *Transmission of the Lamp*, so I'm citing the earlier source I could actually verify.

## The reflection that finished the sentence

Some time after leaving Yunyan, Dongshan was wading across a river. He looked down and saw his own face in the water — his, exactly, and also not the thing itself, just an image thrown back by the surface. That was the moment the unfinished sentence completed itself. He wrote a verse afterward that's still central to Caodong (Soto) teaching today⁵: don't go looking for it in someone else, or it drifts as far from you as the sky from the ground; go on alone, and you meet it everywhere; it is exactly you, and you are not it. Seen this way, you're finally at ease with things as they actually are.

## Departing mentors run into this constantly

It shows up most clearly whenever someone senior is leaving and gets asked how their replacement should decide things once they're gone. The honest answer is often something almost as unsatisfying as Yunyan's: use your judgment, you'll know it when you see it, trust what you've already absorbed. That's not evasion dressed up as wisdom. Anything more specific would be a rule, and rules are exactly what stop working the moment the situation they were written for changes. The frustration of hearing an answer like that isn't a sign the person leaving failed to prepare you. It's usually a sign they've already watched enough people try to hand down something more detailed and seen it fail.

## The advice that clicks somewhere else entirely

Most people carry at least one piece of advice that sat unfinished for years before it suddenly resolved, usually nowhere near where they first heard it. A parent's offhand comment makes sense in a hospital waiting room. A manager's vague note about "reading the room" clicks during an unrelated argument with a friend. The gap between hearing something and understanding it isn't a sign the advice was bad. It's often the only way advice that's actually true can survive being handed to someone who isn't ready for it yet.

## In closing

Dongshan didn't get his answer from more explanation. He got it from a river doing what rivers do, showing him a face that was his and wasn't, at a moment nobody had scheduled. What Yunyan gave him beforehand wasn't incomplete. It was exactly as finished as a sentence like that can be — the rest of it was never going to arrive in words, from him or anyone else.
