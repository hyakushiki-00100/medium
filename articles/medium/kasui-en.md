# He Asked One Question. The Answer Came Later. The Zen Story "Dozan Kasui" (洞山過水).

Before his teacher Yunyan¹ died, Dongshan² asked him something practical: if someone later doubted whether he'd truly captured the man's understanding, how should he answer? Yunyan said, "Just say — this, right here, is it." Dongshan said nothing for a long time. Yunyan told him he'd need to examine the matter with real care.

## A question about carrying someone forward

The question wasn't abstract. Dongshan was about to leave, and he was asking what happens once his teacher is no longer alive to confirm or correct him — how he's supposed to vouch for a person's understanding after that person is gone. It's the succession question in its plainest form: what do you say when you're the only one left who can speak for someone, and you're not fully sure you've got it right? Most people asking that question want a credential, some phrase they can point to later as proof they were paying attention.

## An answer that wasn't finished on delivery

Yunyan's reply sounds like a dodge if you're expecting a checklist. "This, right here, is it" names nothing in particular — no doctrine, no formula, no criteria Dongshan could measure himself against later. But Yunyan wasn't declining to answer. He was handing Dongshan a sentence that couldn't be understood yet, on the correct assumption that understanding it was a different kind of work than being told something.

## Recorded before the verse became famous

This exchange and the verse it produced both survive in the *Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall*³ (祖堂集), compiled in 952 — the earliest source I could confirm. The fuller, more widely cited version appears half a century later in the *Transmission of the Lamp*⁴ (景徳伝灯録, 1004), in the fascicle covering Yunyan's dharma heirs (Yunyan's own biography sits one fascicle earlier). The two accounts don't agree word for word — a handful of characters in the verse differ between them — which is itself a small reminder that even a text this settled kept being retold slightly differently by different hands. Dongshan's own recorded sayings, where a fuller version of the exchange also survives, weren't compiled until centuries later still: one surviving edition dates to Ming-dynasty China, another to a Japanese redaction from later still.

## A river supplied the rest of it

Some time after leaving Yunyan, Dongshan was crossing water and saw his own face in it — his, exactly, and also not the thing itself, just an image thrown back by the surface. The record says only that he "greatly awakened to the earlier point." Which earlier point is genuinely unclear: it could mean this exchange, or an older, unresolved conversation the two of them had about whether something with no mind of its own — a wall, a stone — could be said to teach anything. Either way, what's consistent is where the resolution didn't come from: not from more talk, and not from anywhere near where the question was first asked. He wrote a verse afterward that's still read alongside Caodong (Soto) teaching today⁵: don't go looking for it in someone else, or it only grows more distant from you; go on alone, and you meet it everywhere; it is exactly you, and you are not it. Seen this way, you're finally at ease with things as they actually are.

## Departing mentors run into this constantly

Someone senior on their way out gets asked the same thing stripped down to its essentials: how should my replacement decide things once I'm not here to ask? The honest answer is often something almost as unsatisfying as Yunyan's: use your judgment, you'll know it when you see it, trust what you've already absorbed. That's not evasion dressed up as wisdom. Anything more specific would be a rule, and rules are exactly what stop working the moment the situation they were written for changes. The frustration of hearing an answer like that isn't a sign the person leaving failed to prepare you. It's usually a sign they've already watched enough people try to hand down something more detailed and seen it fail.

## The advice that clicks somewhere else entirely

Most people carry at least one piece of advice that sat unfinished for years before it suddenly resolved, usually nowhere near where they first heard it. A parent's offhand comment makes sense in a hospital waiting room. A manager's vague note about "reading the room" clicks during an unrelated argument with a friend. The gap between hearing something and understanding it isn't a sign the advice was bad. It's often the only way advice that's actually true can survive being handed to someone who isn't ready for it yet.

## In closing

No further explanation ever arrived for Dongshan. A river did what rivers do, and showed him a face that was his and wasn't, on an afternoon nobody had scheduled. What Yunyan had given him beforehand was already complete, in the only way a sentence like that can be — the rest of it was never going to reach him in words, from Yunyan or from anyone else.
