# A Teacher Pointed at the Blinds, and Two Monks Went and Rolled Them Up. Both Did Exactly the Same Thing; the Teacher's Verdict Was Four Words Long. The Zen Koan "Niso Kenren" (二僧巻簾).

Before the midday meal, monks came up to see Fayan¹. He pointed at the bamboo blinds. Two monks went over together and rolled them up. Fayan said: "One gains, one loses."

## One pointing finger, two monks

That's the whole case. Two people get the same signal, make the same move at the same moment, and are told they came out with opposite results. Fayan doesn't say which one gained. He doesn't say what the difference was, or whether there was anything either of them could have done.

The natural reaction is to start looking for the reason. Maybe one of them understood why the teacher pointed and the other just followed. Maybe one was the person he pointed at and the other volunteered. Readers have been doing exactly this for a thousand years, and the story is told in a way that invites it.

## A note already attached in 1004

Fayan Wenyi² taught at Qingliang Monastery in what is now Nanjing; the school that grew from his teaching carries his name. The exchange appears in the Jingde-era record of the lamp³, compiled in 1004, less than fifty years after his death. Even there it comes with a note by Dongchan Daoqi⁴, a teacher two generations down in Fayan's own line, who was twenty-nine when Fayan died. The note lists the explanations people were already offering: one monk didn't understand the point and simply went to roll up the blind; or the monk who was pointed at got it, and the one who went without being pointed at lost. The note then rejects both readings and asks again: so which one gained, and which one lost?

The Essentials of the Collected Lamp⁵, in 1183, tells it as two monks who had come to see him. When Wumen⁶ included it as case 26 of the Gateless Barrier⁷ in 1228, he asked the same question, then turned it on the teacher: anyone who can see clearly here, he wrote, will also see where Fayan himself fell short. One way to hear that is that the person handing down the verdict doesn't get to stand outside it. Wumen then added a warning: whatever you do, don't work it out in terms of gain and loss.

## Barcelona, 1992

In 1995 the psychologists Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich had viewers rate the expressions of medalists at the 1992 Barcelona Olympics. The bronze medalists tended to look happier than the silver medalists, though silver is the better result. The researchers' explanation was the comparison each athlete was most likely making. For the silver medalist, the obvious alternative was gold. For the bronze medalist, it was going home with nothing. Later studies of other Games haven't always found the same pattern, but the explanation has stayed influential: how a result feels can depend a good deal on what it's being compared to.

## Praise for one of two colleagues

Two colleagues ship the same kind of work in the same quarter. One is singled out in the meeting; the other isn't mentioned. Nobody explains the difference, and there may not be a clean one: timing, who was in the room, what the manager happened to notice that week.

If you're the one who wasn't mentioned, the usual response is to treat the gap as a puzzle to solve: work out what the praised one did differently, or what you did wrong, and replay the meeting looking for clues. If you're the one who was praised, the puzzle runs the other way: did you actually deserve it? Sometimes there's a real answer, and it's worth asking for directly. Often the honest answer is that the verdict tells you more about what the person giving it happened to notice than about the two pieces of work.

## Scoring a conversation afterward

The drive home from a family gathering can turn into a replay: who came off better, who said the sharper thing, who got the warmer reception. It's the urge to know which monk gained, turned on your own life. It rarely changes anything except how the rest of the evening feels.

## In closing

Fayan pointed at a blind, watched two monks roll it up the same way, and announced a split result without a reason. A thousand years of readers have wanted to know which one was which. Wumen's advice is still the most useful: the question to drop is the one about gain and loss.
