# A Teacher Pointed at the Blinds, and Two Monks Went and Rolled Them Up. Both Did Exactly the Same Thing; the Teacher's Verdict Was Four Words Long. The Zen Koan "Niso Kenren" (二僧巻簾).

Before the midday meal, monks came up to see Fayan¹. He pointed at the bamboo blinds. Two monks went over together and rolled them up. Fayan said: "One gains, one loses."

## Same action, split verdict

That's the whole case. Two people get the same signal, make the same move at the same moment, and come out with opposite results. Fayan doesn't say which one gained. He doesn't say what the difference was, or whether there was anything either of them could have done.

The natural reaction is to start looking for the reason. Maybe one of them understood why the teacher pointed and the other just followed. Maybe one was asked and the other volunteered. Readers have been doing exactly this for a thousand years, and the story seems built to let them.

## The guesses are as old as the story

Fayan Wenyi² (885–958) taught at Qingliang Monastery in what is now Nanjing; the school that grew from his teaching carries his name. The exchange appears in the Jingde-era record of the lamp³, compiled in 1004, less than fifty years after his death. Even there it comes with a note from a later commentator, Dongchan, listing the explanations people were already offering: one monk didn't understand the point and simply went to roll up the blind; or the monk who was pointed at got it and the one who went along unasked did not. The note then rejects both readings and asks again: so which one gained, and which one lost?

The Essentials of the Collected Lamp⁴, in 1183, tells it as two visiting monks. When Wumen⁵ included it as case 26 of the Gateless Barrier⁶ in 1228, he asked the same question, then turned it on the teacher: anyone who can see clearly here, he wrote, will also see where Fayan himself fell short. The verdict-giver doesn't get to stand outside the verdict. Wumen then added a warning: whatever you do, don't work it out in terms of gain and loss.

## Bronze looks happier than silver

In 1995 the psychologists Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich had viewers rate the expressions of medalists at the 1992 Barcelona Olympics. The bronze medalists tended to look happier than the silver medalists, though silver is the better result. The researchers' explanation was the comparison each athlete was most likely making. For the silver medalist, the obvious alternative was gold. For the bronze medalist, it was going home with nothing.

How the result felt depended less on the medal than on what it was being compared to.

## Singled out in one meeting

Two colleagues ship the same kind of work in the same quarter. One is singled out in the meeting; the other isn't mentioned. Nobody explains the difference, and there may not be a clean one: timing, who was in the room, what the manager happened to notice that week.

If you're the one who wasn't mentioned, the usual response is to treat the gap as a puzzle to solve: work out what the praised one did differently, or what you did wrong, and replay the meeting looking for clues. If you're the one who was praised, there's a quieter version of the same puzzle, which is whether you deserved it. Sometimes there's a real answer, and it's worth asking for directly. Often the honest answer is that the verdict says more about the person giving it than about the two pieces of work.

## Keeping score after the conversation

After a dinner, a meeting, or a phone call with a sibling, part of the mind keeps a quiet tally of who came off better, who said the sharper thing, who was liked more. It's the same habit as wanting to know which monk gained. It rarely changes anything except how the evening ends.

## In closing

Fayan gave two monks the same small job, watched them do it the same way, and announced a split result without a reason. A thousand years of readers have wanted to know which one was which. Wumen's advice is still the most useful: the question to drop is the one about gain and loss.
