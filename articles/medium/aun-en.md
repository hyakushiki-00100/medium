# The Zen Word "A-un (阿吽)" — Living Beginnings and Endings as a Pair

## What this article covers

In Japanese, the phrase *a-un no kokyū* — "A-un breathing" — is something nearly everyone has used at some point. It describes two people perfectly in sync, understanding each other without a word. But the Buddhist term behind it, "A-un" (阿吽), carries a far larger worldview than "being in sync": **it compresses the entire span of the universe, from its beginning to its end, into two characters.**

This article looks at the original meaning of "A-un," how the expression "A-un breathing" came to be, and how the idea can be applied to relationships and work today.

One premise up front: "A-un" is a Buddhist term derived from Sanskrit — one especially valued in Esoteric Buddhism as a mantra — and is not, strictly speaking, a word born within the Zen school. It has, however, permeated Japanese Zen culture through the Niō guardian statues at Zen temple gates, Zen words in the tea room, and Noh theater, and is most often appreciated *as* a Zen word.

---

## The original meaning of "A-un" — a universe in two characters

"A-un" is a Chinese-character transcription of the Sanskrit sounds *a* and *hūṃ*.

- **A (阿):** the first sound, made with the mouth wide open. It stands at the head of the Sanskrit alphabet.
- **Un (吽, hūṃ):** the last sound, made with the mouth fully closed. It stands at the end of the alphabet.

Every sound begins with an open mouth and ends with a closed one — **all speech lives between "A" and "Un."** From this, the pair came to symbolize the beginning and end of all things.

Esoteric Buddhism went further, reading "A" as the origin from which everything arises and "Un" as the terminus to which everything returns — mapping "A" onto *bodhicitta* (the mind that seeks awakening) and "Un" onto *nirvana* (awakening completed). Two characters that span the universe from genesis to dissolution, and the spiritual path from its first step to its destination: it is an astonishingly compressed piece of language.

Incidentally, the Japanese syllabary begins with "a" and ends with "n" — an ordering said to have been shaped by the Sanskrit alphabet. Without realizing it, we speak Japanese inside the structure of "A-un" every day.

## Niō statues and komainu — the meaning of being a pair

The visible form of "A-un" stands at temple gates as the Niō guardians, and at shrines as the komainu lion-dogs.

On the right, the open-mouthed **agyō**; on the left, the closed-mouthed **ungyō**. This pairing can be read in several ways:

- Beginning and end together, **enclosing and protecting everything between them** (their role as gatekeepers)
- The power to project, and the power to receive
- Motion and stillness; release and convergence

What matters most is that agyō and ungyō are **two bodies forming one whole.** Neither works alone. Only when the one who opens and the one who closes stand together is the gate truly guarded.

This maps directly onto human pairings. The talkative salesperson and the quietly meticulous engineer. The one who expands ideas and the one who lands them by the deadline. In marriages and friendships alike, the partnerships people call "a great duo" are usually an "A" role and an "Un" role in tandem. **Two of the same type standing side by side is weaker than an opener paired with a closer** — which is exactly how the guardian statues are arranged.

---

## Where did "A-un breathing" come from?

"A" is also assigned to the out-breath, "Un" to the in-breath — and from this came the phrase *a-un no kokyū*, "A-un breathing."

In the Noh play *Ataka* (the source of the famous Kabuki play *Kanjinchō*), the dialogue between the barrier keeper Togashi and the warrior-monk Benkei contains the line "chanting the two characters A and Un upon the breath going out and coming in," showing that the link between breathing and A-un is centuries old. In time, the phrase came to describe **that wordless, simultaneous accord** — like two sumo wrestlers rising at the exact same instant at the start of a bout.

Here is the interesting part: A-un breathing is **not the product of discussion or planning.** Sumo wrestlers never count "one, two, three." They read each other's breathing, muscle tension, and gaze — and rise together. A-un breathing, in other words, is **communication that occurs before language.**

This resonates beautifully with a foundational idea of Zen: *furyū monji* (不立文字) — truth cannot be fully conveyed in letters or words, but passes directly from mind to mind. This is likely why "A-un," though a broader Buddhist term, has been cherished as a Zen word.

## Bringing it into daily life — three perspectives

### 1. A-un breathing is not talent; it is accumulation

When we say "those two have A-un breathing," we tend to treat it as innate chemistry. But just as a sumo bout's synchronized start rests on thousands of practice sessions, wordless accord only emerges **on the far side of countless words.**

Have you ever failed at work by assuming a colleague "should understand without being told"? The order is reversed. First comes "A" — a period of exhaustive, explicit communication. Only when that accumulation is saturated does "Un" arrive — a relationship in which words can be omitted. **A-un breathing is not something to aim at; it is something that visits you as the result of accumulation.**

### 2. Align the "A" and the "Un" within yourself

A-un is not only about two people. Inside every individual there is an "A" and an "Un."

- A person with only the power to start (A) drowns in half-finished things.
- A person with only the power to finish (Un) can never begin anything new.

The momentum to start a proposal, and the nerve to submit it by the deadline. The courage to start speaking, and the discretion to stop. **For everything you start, you carry the responsibility to finish it** — the two characters ask you to balance those books.

### 3. Return to the breath itself

Finally, there is the literal breath. Exhale "A"; inhale "Un." We enter the world with a first cry — an "A" — and leave it with a final breath — an "Un." In between, the one thing that never once pauses is breathing.

When you are irritated or rushed, exhale completely, then inhale. In that single cycle, you have traveled once from the beginning of the universe to its end and back. Seen that way, doesn't an ordinary deep breath begin to feel just slightly solemn?

---

## In closing

"A-un" shows, in just two characters, that **opposites become whole only as a pair**: beginning and end, the open mouth and the closed, the breath going out and the breath coming in.

For close to a thousand years, the guardian statues at temple gates have stood — one mouth open, one mouth sealed — never competing over which is correct, guarding a single gate as two. Our relationships, and our own inner lives, may be built on much the same architecture as that gate.

## Appendix: Sorting fact from interpretation

- "A-un" transcribes Sanskrit *a* and *hūṃ*; "A" is the open-mouth sound at the head of the alphabet, "Un" the closed-mouth sound at its end (fact — standard dictionary definition).
- In Esoteric Buddhism, "A" symbolizes the origin of all things and bodhicitta; "Un" the terminus and nirvana (fact — per Buddhist reference dictionaries).
- The open-mouthed agyō and closed-mouthed ungyō forms of Niō statues and komainu (fact).
- The Noh play *Ataka* contains the line about chanting A-un upon the outgoing and incoming breath, cited in dictionaries as an early attested use (fact — Nihon Kokugo Daijiten).
- The theory that the Japanese syllabary's ordering was influenced by the Sanskrit alphabet is the widely cited standard account, though scholars still debate the details — treat it as a well-supported tradition rather than a settled fact.
- Connecting "A-un" to Zen's *furyū monji*, and the claim that "A-un breathing is the result of accumulated communication," are the author's modern interpretations rather than traditional doctrine.
