# A Clever Fix and Kung Fu Share the Same Root. The Zen Word "Kufu" (工夫).

The Japanese word *kufu* (工夫) means finding a clever way to do something — a workaround, a smart little fix. It comes from the same root as kung fu, and kung fu never meant a trick. It meant years.

## A word for hired labor, borrowed by monks

*Kufu* (工夫) descends from an old Chinese word for manual labor — work counted in days and hours, not in a specific stroke of skill. By the Tang dynasty the word had already loosened into a colloquial sense of "putting in effort" or "spending the time something takes." Chan Buddhist practice borrowed that sense for something closer to home: the sustained, unglamorous effort of sitting in meditation or working a single koan, day after day, with nothing dramatic to show for any one sitting. The lineage here is debated in its finer points, but the drift from manual labor to devoted practice is the account most sources agree on. The word wasn't for a flash of insight. It was for the part before any insight arrived, the part that looked like nothing — a day where something shifted counted exactly the same as a day where nothing did.

## One word, spelled two ways, pulling apart

*Kufu* (工夫) and *gongfu*¹ (功夫) were historically two spellings of the same word — the record shows them used interchangeably for centuries, including in Dogen's own writing, where he describes "three years of *kufu*" using the 功夫 spelling. Over time the two spellings pulled apart. 工夫 drifted toward something lighter in both languages: in modern Chinese it can mean simple free time, and in Japanese it settled into a clever method or a good workaround, the kind of idea you land on and then you're done. 功夫 held onto the heavier sense — years of accumulated practice, the specific kind of skill that only shows up after you've put in more time than anyone would choose to if there were a shortcut — and that's the spelling that survived into English as *kung fu*. Nobody who has actually trained thinks kung fu is a trick you learn in an afternoon; ask anyone who's spent a decade on a single form, and they'll tell you the accumulated hours are the entire content of the skill, not an inconvenient detour on the way to it. Nobody decided any of this on purpose, either. A word used constantly, for centuries, tends to wear down to whichever sense is easiest to reach for in conversation, and "here's a clever idea" is a much easier thing to reach for than "I sat with this for years."

## A search for the trick that isn't there

A team stuck on execution usually goes looking for a *kufu* fix first: a new tool, a reorganized process, a sharper framing of the goal. Sometimes one of those genuinely is the missing piece. More often, what's missing isn't clever at all — it's the unglamorous version of the word's own original meaning, showing up and doing the same unremarkable thing correctly, for longer than feels reasonable, before any result appears. A team hunting for the one process tweak that fixes their execution problem is often avoiding the plainer diagnosis: nobody has been doing the basics consistently enough, for long enough, to find out if they even work. The tweak feels like progress because it produces a decision to make. Doing the unglamorous thing instead produces nothing to announce in a meeting.

## It shows up in personal habits too

A new diet, a new morning routine, a new personality framework that finally explains the pattern — each one gets treated as the missing piece in a habit that hasn't stuck, when the actual gap is time already spent doing the unglamorous version, repeated past the point where it stopped feeling like discovery. Switching to a better plan isn't kufu in either sense. Eating the same modest dinner on the fortieth unremarkable night, with nothing new to report, is closer to what the word used to mean. It doesn't feel like progress while you're doing it, which is exactly why it keeps getting swapped for something that does.

## In closing

Somewhere between a hired laborer and a martial artist, one branch of this word kept its weight and one lost it. The lighter version gets used constantly now, for the shortcut, the hack, the trick that saves you the years. The older version is still sitting underneath it, unglamorous and mostly invisible, doing the part of the work that was never going to be clever.

As with other word-origin pieces in this series, the finer historical details are debated among sources; the broad lineage described here is the standard account.*
