# A Visitor Asked the Buddha a Question, and the Buddha Said Nothing. The Visitor Thanked Him and Left; the Buddha's Own Attendant Had to Ask What Had Happened. The Zen Koan "Gedo Monbutsu" (外道問仏).

A follower of another teaching came to the Buddha and said, "I'm not asking about words, and I'm not asking about the absence of words." The Buddha stayed silent for a while. The visitor bowed and praised him: "In your great compassion, you've cleared away my clouds and let me in." Then he left. Ananda¹, the Buddha's attendant, asked what the man had understood that let him say he'd got in. The Buddha said: "He's like a good horse that moves at the mere shadow of the whip."

## The one who heard everything

By tradition, Ananda was the disciple who stayed at the Buddha's side for decades and could recite every sermon from memory. If anyone in the room should have understood the silence, it was him. Instead, the visitor, who belonged to a different school and owed the Buddha nothing, understood it at once and walked out satisfied, while Ananda was left asking what had just happened.

Wumen², who included the exchange in the Gateless Barrier in 1228, put it bluntly: Ananda was the Buddha's own disciple, and still his understanding didn't come close to the outsider's. Then he asked his readers how far apart the two really were.

Ananda wasn't slow. He'd simply heard so much from the Buddha that one more silence could easily look like a pause before the next sentence. The visitor came with a single question and was paying attention to nothing else.

## A simile from an older sutra

The image of the horse is older than the story. In one of the early sutras preserved in Chinese translation from the fifth century, the Saṃyukta Āgama³, the Buddha describes four kinds of good horses. The best sets off at the shadow of the whip. The next needs the whip to brush its hair and tail, the third needs it to nick the skin, and the last moves only when an iron goad pierces the flesh to the bone. The sutra matches them to four kinds of people: the one who is moved just by hearing that someone in another village has fallen ill or died; the one who has to see it; the one who has to see it happen to a teacher or someone close to him; and the one who changes only when old age, illness and death reach his own body.

The exchange itself appears in the oldest Chan collection, the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁴, compiled in 952, and in the Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp⁵ of 1004, which adds a later teacher's challenge: so where exactly did the Buddha raise the whip? It became case 65 of the Blue Cliff Record⁶. There, Yuanwu notes that some people call the Buddha's response "silence," others "sitting on his seat," others "not answering," and that none of those labels gets near it. By the time of the Gateless Barrier, the Buddha is described only as sitting on his seat.

## The fox and the lion

Aesop has a fable about a fox that had never seen a lion. The first time it met one, it nearly died of fright. The second time, it was still afraid, but less. The third time, it walked right up and started a conversation. The fable is usually read as a lesson about fear. It's also a fair description of what happens to attention: the thing that stopped you cold the first time barely registers by the third.

## The colleague who has heard it all before

In most teams there's someone who has sat through every planning meeting for years and someone who joined last month. When the leader pauses in the middle of a presentation, or leaves a point conspicuously unsaid, the newcomer often notices and asks about it afterward. The veteran, who has heard the same deck a dozen times, takes it as a pause.

The veteran is just as capable. Hearing a lot makes it easy to listen for the expected next sentence instead of to what's actually happening. Every so often, it's worth sitting through the familiar meeting the way the new person does, as if the one question you came with is the only thing that matters.

## The people you see every day

The same thing happens at home. Someone who lives with you can be visibly worn out for weeks, and it's a friend visiting for one evening who asks whether they're all right. You care as much as ever; you've just seen them tired so often that one more tired evening looks like any other.

## In closing

The visitor got an answer from the Buddha's silence, and the attendant who had heard everything the Buddha said had to ask what had happened. The Buddha didn't scold either of them. He described the visitor as a horse that moves at the shadow of the whip, and said nothing about which kind of horse Ananda was.
