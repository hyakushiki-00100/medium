# Master, Are You There? The Zen Habit of Calling Your Own Name (主人公).

Every day, several times a day, the Tang-dynasty monk Zuigan Shigen would stop whatever he was doing and call out to himself: "Master!" Then he'd answer his own call: "Yes." "Are you awake?" "Yes." "Don't ever let anyone fool you." "Yes, yes." He did this his whole life, apparently without embarrassment. The word he used for himself — *shujinkō* (主人公), "master," or more literally "the one in charge" — has become one of Zen's more famous single-word koans, and it's aimed at a question most of us have stopped asking: who, exactly, is running your day?

## A monk talking to himself

The exchange survives as Case 12 of the *Gateless Gate*, the classic thirteenth-century koan collection, where it's recorded roughly four hundred years after Zuigan is said to have lived it. It reads more like a compressed habit than a single dramatic moment — a small, repeated act of checking in, not a one-time enlightenment. (The compilers were preserving a practice, not filming it; treat the scene as the shape the tradition kept rather than a verified transcript of any particular afternoon.)

*Shujinkō* is worth sitting with. In ordinary Japanese it now just means "protagonist" — the main character of a novel or film. Zuigan's usage is sharper: not the character things happen to, but the one actually in charge of the household of your attention. His question wasn't rhetorical. He was checking whether anyone was home.

## Why call out to yourself at all

Most of a day runs without a master present. You open a laptop and let the inbox set the agenda. You react to a notification before deciding whether it deserves one. You inherit a mood from the last conversation and carry it, unexamined, into the next. None of this requires you — a sufficiently trained set of habits can drive the whole performance. Zuigan's practice interrupts exactly that: a moment, repeated on purpose, to confirm someone is actually steering.

"Don't ever let anyone fool you" lands harder once you notice it isn't really about con artists. The people most likely to talk you out of your own judgment are the ones with the most access to you — a boss's offhand comment, a family expectation you never examined, an old version of yourself whose opinions you've outgrown but kept following out of habit. Being fooled, in this reading, mostly looks like agreement you never actually reached on your own.

## Where this lands at work

- **The inbox that runs you instead of the reverse.** Before opening it, a two-second check: master, are you here? If the honest answer is no, the inbox will happily make every decision for you today.
- **The meeting you attend on autopilot.** Nodding, taking notes, agreeing to a deadline — all of it can happen with nobody actually present behind your eyes. Zuigan's habit is a way of checking who signed up for that deadline.
- **The advice you absorbed years ago and never re-examined.** A mentor's framework, an early boss's definition of ambition, a piece of feedback you internalized at twenty-six — these deserve the same "don't be fooled" scrutiny you'd give a stranger's opinion, precisely because you've stopped hearing them as opinions at all.

None of this is a call to distrust everyone around you. It's a call to notice how often the person you're agreeing with, hour to hour, is actually you.

## The daily habit, not the single insight

What makes Zuigan's practice unusual isn't the content — plenty of traditions recommend self-awareness — but the *frequency*. He didn't check in once and call it settled. He asked several times a day, treating presence the way you'd treat a habit that decays without maintenance rather than a fact you establish once. The question was never meant to have a permanently correct answer. It was meant to be asked again an hour later.

## In closing

"Master, are you there?" is a strange thing to say out loud to an empty room, which may be exactly the point — it's odd enough to actually interrupt you. The next time your attention has clearly been running on autopilot for the last hour, try the whole exchange, even silently. Master. Yes. Are you awake. Yes. Don't be fooled. The answer matters less than the fact that you were the one who had to give it.
