# Master, Are You There? The Zen Habit of Calling Your Own Name (主人公).

Several times a day, a monk named Ruiyan Shiyan — better known in English by the Japanese reading of his name, Zuigan — would stop whatever he was doing and call out to himself: "Master!" Then answer his own call: "Yes." "Are you awake?" "Yes." "Don't ever let anyone fool you." "Yes, yes." He did this for years, apparently without embarrassment. The word he used for himself — *shujinkō* (主人公), "master," or more literally "the one in charge" — has become one of Zen's more famous single-word koans, aimed at a question most of us have stopped asking: who, exactly, is running your day?

## A monk talking to himself

Zuigan's own dates were never recorded — what's known is that he trained under Yantou Quanhuo, who died in 887, placing Zuigan in the late Tang. The exchange survives as Case 12 of the *Gateless Gate*, compiled in 1228, well over three centuries after he is said to have lived it — read as the shape a practice took in the tradition's memory, not a verified transcript of any particular afternoon. It reads like a compressed habit rather than a single dramatic moment: a small act of checking in, repeated, carved out of a monastic life with bells, sitting, and communal labor but almost no room for anything as private as talking to yourself.

*Shujinkō* is worth sitting with. In ordinary Japanese it now just means "protagonist," the main character of a novel or film. Zuigan's usage is sharper: not the one things happen to, but the one in charge of the household of your attention. His question wasn't rhetorical. He was checking whether anyone was home.

## Why call out to yourself at all

Most of a day runs without a master present. You open a laptop and let the inbox set the agenda. You react to a notification before deciding whether it deserves one. You inherit a mood from the last conversation and carry it, unexamined, into the next. None of this requires you — a sufficiently trained set of habits can drive the whole performance. Zuigan's practice interrupts exactly that: a repeated moment confirming someone is actually steering.

"Don't ever let anyone fool you" lands harder once you notice it isn't about con artists. The people most likely to talk you out of your own judgment are the ones with the most access to you — a boss's offhand comment, a family expectation never examined, an old version of yourself you've outgrown but kept following out of habit. Being fooled, in this reading, mostly looks like agreement you never actually reached on your own.

## Where this lands at work

- **The inbox that runs you instead of the reverse.** Before opening it, a two-second check: master, are you here? If not, the inbox will happily make every decision for you today.
- **The meeting you attend on autopilot.** Nodding, taking notes, agreeing to a deadline can all happen with nobody actually present. Zuigan's habit checks who signed up for that deadline.
- **The advice absorbed years ago and never re-examined.** A mentor's framework, an old boss's definition of ambition, a piece of feedback internalized at twenty-six — these deserve the same "don't be fooled" scrutiny you'd give a stranger's opinion, precisely because you've stopped hearing them as opinions.

None of this is a call to distrust everyone around you. It's a call to notice how often the person you're agreeing with, hour to hour, is actually you.

## Alone, with no one to perform for

There's a version that has nothing to do with other people. Watch what you actually do the moment a task ends and no one is watching: the phone comes out, the same three apps open in the same order, the same complaint gets rehearsed to no audience. None of it is chosen so much as triggered — nobody home, running the evening on grooves worn in long ago. Zuigan's question was never really about catching liars. It was about catching the moment presence quietly clocks out, especially when there's no witness to perform it for.

## The daily habit, not the single insight

What makes Zuigan's practice unusual isn't the content — plenty of traditions recommend self-awareness — but the *frequency*. He didn't check in once and call it settled. He asked several times a day, treating presence as a habit that decays without maintenance, not a fact established once. The question had no permanently correct answer. It was meant to be asked again an hour later.

## In closing

"Master, are you there?" is a strange thing to say to an empty room, which may be the point — odd enough to actually interrupt you. Next time your attention has clearly run on autopilot for an hour, try the exchange, even silently. Master. Yes. Are you awake. Yes. Don't be fooled. The answer matters less than the fact that you were the one who had to give it.
