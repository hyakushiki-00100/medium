# A Hunter Chasing Deer Asked a Monk If He'd Seen Them Go By. The Monk Asked How Many He Could Shoot With One Arrow. The Zen Story "Shakkyo Chokyu" (石鞏張弓).

Shigong¹ was chasing a herd of deer when he ran past a hermitage and found Mazu² standing in his way. He asked whether the monk had seen deer go by. Mazu asked who he was. "A hunter." "How many can you shoot with one arrow?" "One." "Then you don't know how to shoot." "And how many can you shoot with one?" "A whole herd." "They're living things, all of them. Why would you shoot a whole herd?" "If you know that," Mazu said, "why don't you shoot yourself?" Shigong said that if he were told to shoot himself, he wouldn't know where to begin.

## A hunter arguing for the deer

The first odd thing is who ends up defending the deer. Shigong made his living killing them, and he was in the middle of a chase, yet the moment Mazu claimed he could bring down a herd with a single arrow, the hunter objected on their behalf. He knew perfectly well that the deer were alive. He had simply never had a reason to say so.

Mazu didn't argue the point. He agreed with it and turned it around: if that's what you know, aim it at yourself. Shigong had spent years getting good at one thing, looking hard at something outside himself and hitting it, and he had no idea how to point that skill the other way. He said so plainly.

That answer is the one Mazu accepted. "This man's ignorance, built up over countless ages, stopped today," he said. Shigong hadn't produced an insight. He had admitted where his skill ran out. According to the record, he broke his bow and arrows on the spot and became Mazu's student.

## Thirty years of drawing a bow on visitors

Shigong Huizang³ is known mainly through this story; his dates aren't recorded. Mazu Daoyi⁴ (709–788) was among the most influential teachers of the Tang dynasty, and the exchange is best known from the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁵, compiled in 1004.

The bow didn't stay broken in the story. Later records say that when Shigong was teaching on a mountain of his own, he greeted visiting monks by drawing a bow and aiming it at them, and kept that up for about thirty years. The versions differ on the details. In the most familiar one, a monk named Sanping⁶ bared his chest and asked whether this was the arrow that kills or the arrow that gives life. Shigong twanged the string three times, Sanping bowed, and Shigong remarked that in thirty years he had managed to hit only half a sage, then broke the bow.

## Physician, heal thyself

The Gospel of Luke has Jesus, back in his hometown of Nazareth, predicting that the people there will throw an old proverb at him: "Physician, heal thyself" (Luke 4:23). Do here, they mean, what we hear you did elsewhere. The proverb survives because it names a familiar gap. People who are good at fixing others are not automatically able to do the same work on themselves, and the skill that makes them good at the first can make the second harder to see.

## Reviewing everyone's plan but your own

Every organization has someone who can find the weak assumption in any proposal within five minutes. Ask that person to put their own plan through the same review and the speed disappears. The habits that make them sharp, standing outside the work and looking for the place where it gives, don't have anywhere to stand when the work is theirs.

The useful response is the one Shigong gave. "I don't know how to review this one" is more honest than a confident self-assessment, and it's a better place to start: it's the point at which someone else's eyes can be invited in.

## Easy to see in a friend

You can tell within a sentence when a friend is avoiding a hard conversation, overworking to stay away from something at home, or explaining away a habit. The same kind of attention, aimed at your own week, tends to slide off. Saying "I can't see my own version of this" is usually where self-awareness starts.

## In closing

Mazu didn't take Shigong's bow away. He asked a question that the bow couldn't answer, and Shigong had the good sense to say he didn't know where to begin. Of everything said that afternoon, that was the line Mazu treated as the turning point.
