# A Hunter Chasing Deer Asked a Monk If He'd Seen Them Go By. The Monk Asked How Many He Could Shoot With One Arrow. The Zen Story "Sekkyo Chokyu" (石鞏張弓).

Shigong¹ was chasing a herd of deer when he ran past a hermitage and found Mazu² standing in his way. He asked whether the monk had seen deer go by. Mazu asked who he was. "A hunter." "Do you know how to shoot?" "I do." "How many can you shoot with one arrow?" "One." "Then you don't know how to shoot." "Do you know how to shoot, then?" "I do." "How many with one arrow?" "A whole herd." "They're living things, all of them. Why would you shoot a whole herd?" "If you know that," Mazu said, "why don't you shoot yourself?" Shigong said that if he were told to shoot himself, he wouldn't know where to begin.

## A hunter arguing for the deer

The first odd thing is who ends up defending the deer. Shigong made his living killing them, and he was in the middle of a chase, yet the moment Mazu claimed he could bring down a herd with a single arrow, the hunter objected on their behalf. He knew perfectly well that the deer were alive. He may simply never have had a reason to say so.

Mazu didn't argue the point. He agreed with it and turned it around: if that's what you know, aim it at yourself. Shigong had spent years getting good at one thing, looking hard at something outside himself and hitting it, and he had no idea how to point that skill the other way. He said so plainly.

That answer is the one Mazu accepted. "This man's ignorance, built up over countless ages, stopped today, all at once," he said. What Shigong had done was admit where his skill ran out. According to the record, he broke his bow and arrows on the spot, cut off his own hair with a knife, and became a monk under Mazu.

## Thirty years of drawing a bow on visitors

Shigong Huizang³ is best known for this story; his dates aren't recorded. Mazu Daoyi⁴ (709–788) was among the most influential teachers of the Tang dynasty. The exchange first appears in the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁵, first compiled in 952, and the wording most often quoted comes from the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁶, compiled in 1004, which adds that before that day Shigong had disliked the sight of monks.

The bow came back. The same two records go on to say that once Shigong was teaching, he met visiting monks with a drawn bow and an arrow on the string. When a monk named Sanping⁷ arrived, Shigong called out, "Look at the arrow!" and Sanping bared his chest to it. In the oldest version, that is nearly all: Shigong threw the bow down and said that in thirty years there, today he had hit half a sage. Later tellings fill it in. In the commentary of the Blue Cliff Record⁸, from about 1125, Sanping asks whether this is the arrow that kills or the one that gives life, Shigong twangs the string three times, Sanping bows, and Shigong says that in thirty years, with one bow and two arrows, he has hit only half a sage, then breaks the bow. Shigong's story with the bow begins with the hunt and ends here, and the name "Shigong draws his bow" (石鞏張弓) has been attached above all to this later scene. Here it covers both.

## Physician, heal thyself

The Gospel of Luke has Jesus, back in his hometown of Nazareth, predicting that the people there will throw an old proverb at him: "Physician, heal thyself" (Luke 4:23). Do here, they mean, what we hear you did elsewhere. The proverb survives because it names a familiar gap. People who are good at fixing others are not automatically able to do the same work on themselves, and the skill that makes them good at the first can make the second harder to see.

## Reviewing everyone's plan but your own

Some people can find the weak assumption in any proposal within five minutes. Ask that person to put their own plan through the same review and the speed disappears. The habits that make them sharp, standing outside the work and looking for the place where it gives, don't have anywhere to stand when the work is theirs.

The useful response is the one Shigong gave. "I don't know how to review this one" is more honest than a confident self-assessment, and it's a better place to start: it's the point at which someone else's eyes can be invited in.

## Easy to see in a friend

You can tell within a sentence when a friend is avoiding a hard conversation, overworking to stay away from something at home, or explaining away a habit. The same kind of attention, aimed at your own week, tends to slide off. Saying "I can't see my own version of this" is usually where self-awareness starts.

## In closing

The question Mazu put to the hunter was one the bow couldn't answer, and Shigong had the good sense to say he didn't know where to begin. Of everything said that day, that was the line Mazu treated as the turning point.
