# A monk asked how to escape the cold and the heat. The teacher's answer: let it kill you. The Zen Case "Dongshan's No Cold, No Heat" (洞山無寒暑).

A monk asks his teacher how to escape the cold and the heat when they arrive. The teacher tells him: go to the place where there's no cold and no heat. Where is that place, the monk asks. The teacher answers: when it's cold, let the cold kill you. When it's hot, let the heat kill you.

## A teacher who wouldn't settle

Dongshan Liangjie¹ didn't settle easily. He studied first under Nanquan Puyuan, then under Guishan Lingyou, before finally receiving transmission from Yunyan Tansheng² and going on to found what later became known, in Japan, as the Sōtō school.

"Heat and cold" isn't really about weather here. In Chan idiom it stands for anything uncomfortable, anything you'd rather not be dealing with — the whole category of things worth escaping. The monk's question is a reasonable one. Everyone seeks warmth in winter and shade in summer; there's nothing wrong with that instinct. What he's actually asking for, though, is a place where discomfort itself doesn't exist — some safe room outside the weather altogether.

Dongshan's first answer, go where there's no heat or cold, sounds at first like he's granting the request. Then the second question lands, and the answer refuses to point anywhere. What he offers isn't a destination. It's an instruction to be killed by whichever one arrives.

## A line kept in circulation long before it was written down

This exchange survives as case forty-three of the Blue Cliff Record³, compiled in 1125 by Yuanwu Keqin — though the collection's real foundation is the verse commentary Xuedou Chongxian wrote roughly a century earlier, drawing on cases already circulating in records like the Jingde Chuandenglu. The dialogue between Dongshan and the monk likely predates Xuedou's own verse, preserved first in Dongshan's own recorded sayings before it was chosen, centuries later, for a collection meant to last.

What survives, then, isn't a transcript of one afternoon. It's a line that multiple generations of teachers judged worth keeping in circulation — which is its own kind of evidence for what it's doing.

## What "kill" doesn't mean here

"Let it kill you" sounds violent for a teaching about weather. It isn't pointing at anything close to literal death. What's being asked for is total, unreserved absorption into whatever's actually happening — no leftover self standing outside it, managing it, waiting for it to pass.

Chan monasteries had nothing resembling central heating. Winter meditation happened on stone floors that held the cold. Breath went visible in the hall; hands went numb around prayer beads. Telling a monk in that condition to let the cold kill him isn't an instruction to grit his teeth and endure. It's an instruction to stop treating the shivering as a problem happening to someone separate from the shivering. The push to hold cold at a distance is what manufactures the tension of feeling trapped by it in the first place. Take the shiver as the shiver, without the extra layer of a self trying to get away from it, and the two separate things — the cold, and the person resisting the cold — never actually form.

## The cool place that keeps moving one commitment ahead

- **The relief you keep filing for later.** Tight deadlines, a hard negotiation, work you haven't done before — a lot of people treat all of it as weather to get through until the next calm stretch. Then the next project arrives, and the same postponement gets carried forward with it. The cool place stays exactly one commitment ahead, permanently.
- **The thumb that reaches for a notification before a hard conversation.** Delivering feedback, saying no, apologizing — the discomfort of the moment right before doing it sends attention somewhere else, usually a phone screen. The discomfort hasn't gone anywhere. It's just been pushed back a few minutes.
- **A packed calendar as an excuse not to feel anything.** Filling every slot is one of the most socially acceptable ways to avoid whatever would surface the moment a person actually stopped moving.

Dongshan isn't telling anyone to take on less work. He's asking whether the cool place being searched for was ever actually out there — or whether the search itself is what's keeping the discomfort at arm's length.

## What replays once the lights go off

A remark from earlier in the day starts replaying somewhere around midnight. So does tomorrow's list of worries. Most of what's happening in that moment isn't confrontation with the feeling itself — it's an attempt to push it back out of the head. The harder the attempt not to think about something, the more persistently it returns, reshaped. The only thing that reliably grows is the number of times the clock gets checked.

Dongshan's answer, carried into that room, would mean something like: stop trying to hold the anxious feeling at a distance from the self having it, and let it sit there as a sensation instead of an opponent. The moment the pushing-away stops, the sensation stops being something to fight. It doesn't necessarily disappear — but the energy that had been spent trying to make it disappear is no longer needed either. That's most of what this case is actually offering a reader now.

## In closing

What the monk wanted was a place outside the weather altogether. What Dongshan told him was that searching for it would never end in arrival. It was also the simplest instruction available: stop pushing whatever's here away, and the searching is already over. Let the cold kill you. Let the heat kill you. It isn't a case for gritting your teeth. It's a case for noticing you were looking for an exit that was never there to begin with.
