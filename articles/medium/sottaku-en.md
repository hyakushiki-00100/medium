# A Monk Told His Teacher He Was Ready to Hatch and Asked for Help. The Teacher Asked Whether He'd Come Out Alive. The Zen Koan "Kyosei Sottaku" (鏡清啐啄).

A monk said to Jingqing¹: "I'm pecking from inside the shell. Please peck from outside."

Jingqing said: "Will you come out alive?"

The monk said: "If I don't, people will laugh at me."

Jingqing said: "Another one stuck in the weeds."

## A chick and a hen

The image is a hatching egg. The chick works at the shell from inside; the hen taps at the same spot from outside; the shell gives way when the two meet. In Chinese the two kinds of pecking have separate words, *zu* and *zhuo*, read *sotsu* and *taku* in Japanese, and Chan teachers used the pair for the moment when a student's readiness and a teacher's push arrive together.

The monk was using the image on the teacher who, by a later account, was known for it. Jingqing's reply goes straight to the only thing that matters about hatching: does the chick survive? And the monk's answer gives him away. Asked whether he would live, he talked about being laughed at. Read plainly, he was still thinking about how a failure would look to other people, which is why Jingqing said he was still in the weeds, tangled up in things that had nothing to do with getting out of the shell.

## Older than the exchange

The phrase is older than the conversation. The Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall², compiled in 952, already has a monk asking another teacher: "Leave aside the pecking inside and out happening together. What about when the chick crows inside the egg?" The Jingde record³ of 1004 has a teacher named Nanyuan complaining that teachers everywhere have the eye for simultaneous pecking but not the skill to do it.

The exchange with Jingqing doesn't appear in either book, although both have chapters on him. The earliest version found so far is in the recorded sayings of Xuedou⁴, who died in 1052. Xuedou picked it up again in the collection of old cases that later became the Blue Cliff Record⁵, where it's case 16. There Yuanwu adds that Jingqing used to teach that a traveling monk needs both the eye and the skill for this: when the hen is about to peck, the chick can't help pecking, and when the chick is about to peck, the hen can't help pecking. Yuanwu thinks well of the monk, too. He says the monk knew how to turn on a word. And still, Yuanwu notes, Jingqing was not one to go easy.

## Socrates the midwife

In Plato's *Theaetetus*⁶, Socrates describes himself as a midwife. He produces no wisdom of his own, he says, but he can tell when a young man's mind is in labor and help the idea out. Some people who came to him, he adds, didn't seem to be carrying anything at all. Those he sent on to other teachers, Prodicus among them, without pretending otherwise.

Socrates and Jingqing were doing the same check. Before helping, each asked whether there was actually something ready to be born.

## Asking for feedback

You've probably asked a colleague to "be brutal" about a draft and then spent the meeting defending every line. The request was sincere. But the questions running underneath it were about how the draft would look to the boss and whether the writer would look bad, and those questions crowded out the one that mattered: is the thing ready, and what will it take to make it work?

Before asking for help, it's worth asking yourself what you'd do if the help worked. If the honest answer is "feel relieved I wasn't laughed at," you're asking for reassurance. That's a reasonable thing to want, but it's a different request, and a good mentor will usually notice the difference.

## Letting go of the bicycle

Anyone who has taught a child to ride a bicycle knows the timing problem. Let go too early and the child falls. Hold on too long and the child never learns to balance. The moment to let go is when the child is pushing forward on their own, not when they're looking around to see who's watching.

## In closing

The monk asked to be helped out of his shell. Jingqing asked whether he would live through it, and the monk answered with a worry about being laughed at. Jingqing didn't peck. Whether the monk ever hatched, the record doesn't say.
