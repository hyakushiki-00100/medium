# A Monk Told His Teacher He Was Ready to Hatch and Asked for Help. The Teacher Asked Whether He'd Come Out Alive. The Zen Koan "Kyosei Sottaku" (鏡清啐啄).

A monk said to Jingqing¹: "I'm pecking from inside the shell. Please peck from outside."

Jingqing said: "Will you come out alive?"

The monk said: "If I don't, people will laugh at me."

Jingqing said: "Another one stuck in the weeds."

## A chick and a hen

The image is a hatching egg. The chick works at the shell from inside, the hen taps from outside, and the shell gives way when the two meet. In Chinese the two kinds of pecking have separate words, *cui* and *zhuo*, read *sotsu* and *taku* in Japanese, and Chan teachers used the pair for the moment when a student's readiness and a teacher's push arrive together.

The monk was using the image on the teacher who, by a later account, was known for it. Jingqing's reply goes straight to the only thing that matters about hatching: does the chick survive? Asked whether he would live, the monk talked about being laughed at.

Taken at face value, he was thinking about how a failure would look to other people, and "stuck in the weeds" is Jingqing's verdict on that. It's one way to read the exchange, and the one this essay leans on. The old commentators read it differently, as the next section shows.

## Earlier records of the phrase

The phrase is on record well before the exchange is. The Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall², compiled in 952, already has a monk asking another teacher: "Leave aside the pecking inside and out happening together. What about when the chick crows inside the egg?" The Jingde record³ of 1004 has a teacher known as Nanyuan⁴ complaining that teachers everywhere have the eye for simultaneous pecking but not the skill to do it.

The exchange with Jingqing doesn't appear in either book, although both have chapters on him. The earliest version found so far is in the recorded sayings of Xuedou⁵, who died in 1052. Xuedou also took it up in the collection of old cases that later became the Blue Cliff Record⁶, where it's case 16. There Yuanwu adds, in a passage found only in his commentary, that Jingqing used to teach that a traveling monk needs both the eye and the skill for this: when the hen is about to peck, the chick can't help pecking, and when the chick is about to peck, the hen can't help pecking.

Yuanwu thinks well of the monk. He says the monk knew how to move with the moment, and asks why Jingqing still called him a man in the weeds. Xuedou's verse, as Yuanwu explains it, gives the answer the commentators preferred: Jingqing's question, "Will you come out alive?", was itself the peck from outside, and "another one stuck in the weeds" was a second blow. On that reading, Jingqing didn't hold back. He pecked twice.

## Socrates the midwife

In Plato's *Theaetetus*⁷, Socrates describes himself as a midwife. He produces no wisdom of his own, he says, but he can tell when a young man's mind is in labor and help the idea out. Some people who came to him, he adds, didn't seem to be carrying anything at all. Those he sent on to other teachers, Prodicus among them.

Socrates' help came almost entirely in the form of questions, and so, on Xuedou's reading, did Jingqing's. A good question at the right moment is the tap from outside. Whether anything comes out depends on what's already moving inside.

## "Tear it apart, please"

You've probably asked a colleague to "be brutal" about a draft and then spent the meeting defending every line. The request was sincere. But the questions running underneath it were about how the draft would look to the boss and whether the writer would look bad, and those questions crowded out the one that mattered: is the thing ready, and what will it take to make it work?

Before asking for help, it's worth asking yourself what you actually want out of it. If the honest answer is "to feel relieved I wasn't laughed at," you're asking for reassurance. That's a reasonable thing to want, but it's a different request. And when a colleague answers your request with a hard question instead of a fix, it's worth considering that the question may be the help.

## Letting go of the bicycle

Anyone who has taught a child to ride a bicycle knows the timing problem. Let go too early and the child falls. Hold on too long and the child never learns to balance. The moment to let go is when the child is pushing forward on their own, not when they're looking around to see who's watching.

## In closing

The monk asked to be helped out of his shell. Jingqing asked whether he would live through it, and the monk answered with a worry about being laughed at. Read one way, Jingqing turned him down. Read the way Xuedou and Yuanwu read it, the question was the help, given twice. Whether the monk ever hatched, the record doesn't say.
