# He Couldn't Read a Word. He Still Won the Contest to Become the Next Master. The Zen Story of "Honrai Muichimotsu" (本来無一物).

A monastery in seventh-century China needs a new abbot, and the current one, Hongren, sets an unusual test: whoever writes the verse that best shows their understanding will inherit the robe and the title. The presumptive winner, a senior monk named Shenxiu, writes something careful and effortful — the body is a bodhi tree, the mind a mirror on a stand; polish it constantly, don't let dust collect. Everyone assumes that settles it. Then a second verse appears on the same wall, dictated by a monk who can't write his own name, who spends his days pounding rice in the kitchen: there was never a tree. There was never a mirror. There was never anywhere for dust to land.

## A rice-pounder who couldn't write

The second verse belongs to Huineng (638–713), who would become the Sixth Patriarch of Chan and the central figure of the *Platform Sutra* — one of the few Buddhist texts composed by someone born in China rather than translated from Sanskrit. Before he arrived at Hongren's monastery, he'd supported his mother by selling firewood; once there, illiterate, he was assigned to the kitchen to pound rice, eight months of plain labor before the verse contest ever came up. Tradition holds that he had someone else write his lines on the wall for him.

## What the oldest copy actually says

Worth being honest about the text itself. The version most people quote today — "originally not a single thing exists, so where could dust ever land" — comes from later editions of the *Platform Sutra*, compiled generations after Huineng's death. The oldest surviving copy, a manuscript found at Dunhuang and dated close to his own era, has a different third line: "Buddha-nature is always pure," not "originally not a single thing." Scholars also point to Huineng's own disciple Shenhui as someone who actively shaped this text decades later, promoting Huineng's teaching over Shenxiu's rival school. What we're reading is a document with a stake in the argument it's making, not a neutral transcript.

## Two ways to keep something clean

Shenxiu's verse describes a maintenance program: you have something valuable — a mind like a mirror — and the job is constant upkeep, wiping away whatever settles on it before it accumulates. It isn't bad advice. Most self-improvement writing today is a version of it: build the habit, defend the routine, stay vigilant. Huineng's answer doesn't improve on the maintenance plan. It removes the object being maintained. If there was never a fixed mirror to begin with, there's nothing for dust to land on, and nothing that needs your constant supervision.

## A robe passed in secret, at night

Hongren recognized what Huineng had written, but he didn't announce it. According to the tradition, he summoned Huineng alone after dark, transmitted the robe and bowl that marked him as successor, and told him to leave immediately — before the other monks, who'd trained for years under a teacher just passed over for an illiterate kitchen worker, could act on their resentment. Huineng fled south that night and didn't teach publicly again for years. When he finally settled, at Caoxi in what's now Guangdong, he stayed for roughly thirty-seven years.

## The mirror you polish at work

- **The performance of having it together.** A lot of professional effort goes into the constant upkeep of an image — competent, calm, on top of things — rather than into the work itself. That upkeep is real labor, and it's Shenxiu's verse exactly: wipe, polish, don't let anything show.
- **Impostor syndrome as a maintenance problem.** The anxious need to keep proving your competence assumes there's a fixed, fragile thing — your reputation, your worth — that requires constant defense against dust.
- **The cost of permanent vigilance.** Monitoring yourself constantly is expensive, whether or not the thing you're monitoring is even real.

None of this argues for carelessness. It argues for checking whether what you're defending so vigilantly actually needs the defense, or whether the vigilance itself has become the heaviest thing you're carrying.

## No mirror to keep clean

Turned inward, Huineng's answer isn't comforting so much as it's a genuine question: what exactly are you protecting when you protect your self-image? If there's a fixed thing in there that dust settles on, constant polishing is simply the price of ownership. But it's worth actually checking, rather than assuming, whether the vigilance is protecting something real or just protecting the habit of vigilance itself.

## In closing

Hongren never explained to the rest of the monastery why the kitchen worker got the robe. He didn't need to. Shenxiu's verse asked how to keep something clean forever. Huineng's asked what, exactly, needed cleaning in the first place — and he was gone before anyone could argue with the answer.
