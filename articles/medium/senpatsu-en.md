# He Asked for a Teaching. He Got a Chore. The Zen Case "Joshu Senpatsu" (趙州洗鉢).

A monk, newly arrived at the monastery, asked Zhaozhou for guidance. Zhaozhou asked whether he'd eaten his morning gruel. He had. "Then go wash your bowl," Zhaozhou said. The monk had a realization on the spot.

## A request for guidance, answered with a chore

The monk's question wasn't casual. Arriving at a monastery meant arriving with an assumption already in place: that real practice was something separate from the ordinary business of eating and cleaning up, and that a teacher's job was to hand over that separate thing. Whatever the monk expected — a phrase to sit with, a technique to practice, some sign that he'd just been handed the real thing — none of it arrived. Zhaozhou didn't correct the assumption with an argument. He asked one plain question, got one plain answer, and pointed at the sink.

## The rule was already written down

Nothing about "wash your bowl" was new information. Communal meals in Chan monasteries were governed by monastic codes going back to Baizhang Huaihai¹, active a generation before Zhaozhou, and washing your bowl after the morning gruel wasn't a personal habit — it was already required of every monk in the hall, including the one asking the question. Zhaozhou didn't reveal a technique. He named a step the monk was already obligated to take, whether or not anyone had just told him something profound.

## Attested before the anthology that made it famous

Most people who know this exchange know it from the *Gateless Gate*² (無門関), compiled in 1228. It's older than that. Hongzhi Zhengjue³, who died in 1157, had already written a verse on this exact case — the verse that later became Case 39 of the *Book of Serenity*⁴, decades before the *Gateless Gate* existed. Whether the exchange itself was recorded earlier still, in one of the ninth or tenth-century lamp histories, isn't something I can confirm from the sources available to me; what's certain is that it was already circulating as a teaching case a full century before the anthology most readers encounter it in today.

## No second task was hiding behind the first

The monk's request assumed there were two categories of activity: the maintenance of daily life, and the real work of practice sitting somewhere behind it, waiting to be handed over. Zhaozhou's answer collapses the distinction without arguing against it. He doesn't say the two categories are the same in theory. He just refuses to produce the second one. The bowl in front of the monk was the entire assignment.

## The instinct outlives the first week

The instinct to look past the obvious next task for something more instructive shows up constantly with new hires, and not only new hires. Someone joins a team and asks for the playbook, the framework, the thing that will tell them how to really operate here — when the actual next useful action, the email that needs a reply, the file that needs reviewing, is already sitting in front of them, unglamorous and unmysterious. Waiting for someone to hand over something more official-sounding is often just a slower way of not starting the task that's already sitting there. The same pattern shows up past the first weeks in a role, too — a team stalled on a project, waiting for a clearer mandate from above, when what's actually missing is a decision that was theirs to make the whole time.

## The same habit runs quieter, alone

It shows up alone, too, just quieter: hunting for a better system before doing the thing the current, unimproved system already tells you to do: the workout already on the calendar, the conversation that's overdue, the draft sitting half-finished. Reading one more method, downloading one more app, refining one more plan — none of it is dishonest exactly, but all of it can function as a way of not touching the bowl that's actually in the sink.

## In closing

The monk didn't get a technique, and he didn't get turned away. He got the same instruction the monastery's own rules already gave him, said back to him by someone whose only real move was declining to add anything to it. What he did with that moment isn't recorded beyond the fact that something in him settled. The plainer question — what's the one obligation already sitting in front of you, right now, that no teaching could make more urgent than it already is — doesn't need a monastery to ask.
