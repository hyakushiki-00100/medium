# He Asked for a Teaching. He Got a Chore. The Zen Case "Joshu Senpatsu" (趙州洗鉢).

A monk, newly arrived at the monastery, asked Zhaozhou¹ for guidance. Zhaozhou asked whether he'd eaten his morning gruel. He had. "Then go wash your bowl," Zhaozhou said. The monk had a realization on the spot.

## A request for guidance, answered with a chore

The monk's question wasn't casual. Arriving at a monastery meant arriving with an assumption already in place: that real practice was something separate from the ordinary business of eating and cleaning up, and that a teacher's job was to hand over that separate thing. Whatever the monk expected — a phrase to sit with, a technique to practice, some sign that he'd just been handed the real thing — none of it arrived. Zhaozhou didn't correct the assumption with an argument. He asked one plain question, got one plain answer, and sent the monk back to the bowl in front of him.

## The rule was already an old habit

Nothing about "wash your bowl" was new information. Eating together and clearing up afterward was already the daily rhythm of a Chan monastery in Zhaozhou's time, tied to the monastic discipline associated with Baizhang Huaihai², active a generation before him. The written code most associated with Baizhang doesn't survive intact, though; the oldest complete Chan monastic code that does survive is a later reconstruction, compiled more than two centuries after he lived. What's clear is that washing the bowl you'd just eaten from wasn't a personal habit invented on the spot. It was already how the day worked, for every monk in the hall, including the one asking the question. Zhaozhou didn't reveal a technique. He named a step the monk already knew, whether or not anyone had just told him something profound.

## Older than the anthology that made it famous

Most people meet this exchange through the *Gateless Gate*³ (無門関), compiled in 1228, the version retold here, opening line included. It isn't the earliest form. Several later teachers and compilers point to an older telling already active in Zhaozhou's own recorded sayings and the Song-dynasty lamp histories, well before either the *Gateless Gate* or Hongzhi Zhengjue's⁴ verse — the verse, written by his death in 1157, that later became Case 39 of the *Book of Serenity*⁵. In that older telling, by most accounts, the monk doesn't open with quite the same line repeated in every retelling since. I wasn't able to check the earliest Chinese text directly to confirm its exact wording. What's reasonably clear is that this had already been an old teaching case, retold more than once, well before the version most readers meet it in today.

## No second task was hiding behind the first

The monk's request assumed there were two categories of activity: the maintenance of daily life, and the real work of practice sitting somewhere behind it, waiting to be handed over. Zhaozhou's answer collapses the distinction without arguing against it. He doesn't say the two categories are the same in theory. He just refuses to produce the second one. The bowl in front of the monk was the entire assignment.

## The instinct outlives the first week

The instinct to look past the obvious next task for something more instructive shows up constantly with new hires, and not only new hires. Someone joins a team and asks for the playbook, the framework, the thing that will tell them how to really operate here — when the actual next useful action, the email that needs a reply, the file that needs reviewing, is already sitting in front of them, unglamorous and unmysterious. Waiting for someone to hand over something more official-sounding is often just a slower way of not starting the task that's already sitting there. The same pattern shows up past the first weeks in a role, too — a team stalled on a project, waiting for a clearer mandate from above, when what's actually missing is a decision that was theirs to make the whole time.

## When no one is checking your work

Alone, the instinct doesn't disappear — it just gets harder to catch. Before doing what the current, unimproved system already tells you to do, there's a pull toward finding a better one first: the workout already on the calendar, the conversation that's overdue, the draft sitting half-finished. Reading one more method, downloading one more app, refining one more plan — none of it is dishonest exactly, but all of it can function as a way of not touching the bowl that's actually in the sink.

## In closing

The monk didn't get a technique, and he didn't get turned away. He got the same instruction the day's own rhythm already gave him, said back to him by someone whose only real move was declining to add anything to it. What happened in him right then isn't spelled out beyond the plain fact of a realization. The plainer question — what's the one obligation already sitting in front of you, right now, that no teaching could make more urgent than it already is — doesn't need a monastery to ask.
