# He Sank His Family's Entire Fortune in a River. Then Called Carrying Water the Miracle. The Zen Verse "Unsui Hansai" (運水搬柴).

Layman Pang¹ came from a family with an open road into government office. After a turn in his practice, he loaded the entire family fortune onto a boat, rowed to the middle of the river, and sank it. Not donated. Not distributed. Sunk, with no one left able to trace where it had gone. He spent the rest of his life making and selling bamboo goods for a living, and left behind two lines that outlasted the fortune he threw away: supernatural power, marvelous action — carrying water, chopping wood.

## A path into office, never taken

Pang was born into a family of officials in Hengyang — his own father is said to have served as the region's governor — and studied the sutras alongside his wife and daughter before he ever sought out a teacher. In his mid-forties, he traveled to study with Shitou Xiqian². One day Shitou asked him a plain question: since arriving here, what have your daily activities actually been? Pang answered with a verse. He later spent two more years training under Mazu Daoyi, deepening what he'd started at Shitou's side. What he never did, at any point, was become a monk. He stayed a layman his entire life — no robes, no ordination, and no seat in the civil service his own family background would have made available to him.

## The eight lines he offered in reply

The verse runs eight lines. Loosely: my daily work is nothing unusual, I've simply come into agreement with it — nothing to grasp, nothing to refuse, no friction anywhere I turn. Who decided which colors mark the rank of officials³? The hills and mountains hold not a speck of dust. Supernatural power, marvelous action: carrying water, chopping wood.

The closing couplet is the part that survived. It's preserved in sources including the *Zutang Collection*⁴ (祖堂集) and the later *Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp*⁵ (景徳伝灯録) — the tradition's account of what Pang wrote, not a document in his own hand that's reached us directly.

## What he was actually turning down

The line about "colors marking the rank of officials" is doing real work, not decoration. Purple and vermilion were the literal, legally prescribed dress colors of high Tang officials — unmistakable, worn specifically to be seen. For a man from Pang's background, that color was a live option, not an abstraction. He set it against a mountain with no dust on it, then landed on two chores nobody would ever notice a person doing.

The fortune in the river reads the same way. Asked why he hadn't given it to the poor instead, he's said to have answered that it had been a source of harm to him for a long time, and he had no wish to pass that harm on to anyone else. That's the reason the tradition kept. My own reading goes a step further: sinking it also meant there was nothing left to convert into visible virtue — no monastery with his name on a gate, no reputation for generosity anyone could point to. Whatever came next couldn't be mistaken for a performance, because there was no longer any prop left to perform with.

## The record nobody asked to see

The habit Pang refused shows up today in a narrower, more familiar shape: needing good work to leave a trail. Catching a problem quietly before it becomes anyone else's, then making sure it's known who caught it. Doing the unglamorous maintenance and slipping a mention of it into the next update, just so it registers somewhere.

None of that is dishonest — sometimes the update is genuinely useful information. The distinction is narrower: whether the report exists to inform people, or exists because the work didn't feel complete until it had a witness.

## The ledger kept for no one

There's a quieter version of the same habit. Plenty of people keep a private tally of the good they've done, ready to produce it if anyone ever doubts them — evidence banked against a future accusation of not doing enough. Pang's fortune is closer to that tally than it first looks. What went into the river wasn't the value of the work itself. It was the proof of it, kept on hand in case someone eventually asked to see it.

## In closing

Pang kept the sutras he'd studied and let go of everything that would have made his practice legible to anyone watching. No temple, no title, no fortune left to show where it had gone. What remained was a man hauling water up from a well and splitting firewood, on a day exactly like every other day, with nothing left to point to as proof that any of it had mattered.
