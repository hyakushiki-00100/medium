# In Bitter Cold, a Monk Burned the Temple's Wooden Buddha to Keep Warm. When Challenged, He Said He Was After the Relics. The Zen Story "Tanka Shobutsu" (丹霞焼仏).

The cold was severe, and Danxia¹ was staying at a temple called Huilin. He took one of its wooden Buddhas and burned it to keep warm. The man in charge of the temple objected. Danxia told him he was cremating it, to collect the relics. "What relics are there in a piece of wood?" the man said. "Then why are you blaming me?"

## Either it's the Buddha or it's firewood

The objection only holds if the statue is two things at once. If it really is the Buddha, then what Danxia was doing was a cremation, and a cremation is supposed to leave relics — the small remains gathered from the Buddha's ashes, and later from those of great teachers, and enshrined. If it's wood, it leaves ash, and burning wood in bitter cold is what wood is for. The keeper wanted the statue treated as sacred without being willing to claim it would produce relics, so his complaint had nowhere left to stand. Danxia never argued about reverence. He took the man's position seriously, followed it one step further, and let the man hear where it went.

In the telling preserved in the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall, the keeper stepped forward and his eyebrows fell out on the spot. Chan stories use falling eyebrows as the stock sign of someone who has spoken falsely about the teaching, and the scene is often read as turning that penalty on the man who defended the statue rather than the monk who burned it. Some readers take it the other way, as a sign that the keeper had just seen through something too.

## An exam he never sat

Danxia Tianran² (739–824) trained first as a Confucian scholar and was on his way to the capital to sit the civil service examinations when a Chan monk he met at an inn asked him how being chosen as an official compared with being chosen as a buddha. He went to Mazu³ instead, who sent him on to Shitou⁴. According to the Jingde-era record, when he later returned to Mazu's monastery he climbed onto the neck of the statue of the holy monk in the meditation hall, and Mazu called him "my natural son" — the origin of the name Tianran, "natural."

The statue-burning appears in the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁵, first compiled in 952, and that telling already has the eyebrows. The only text of the Anthology that survives, though, is a Korean printing of 1245, expanded more than once along the way, so not every passage in it can be dated to 952. The Jingde-era record of the lamp⁶, compiled in 1004, tells it more briefly, and the objector there is simply "someone." The version most often retold today — Danxia stirring the ashes with his staff and offering to burn two more statues — comes from later Song compilations such as the Compendium of the Five Lamps⁷, compiled in 1252.

## Hezekiah and the bronze serpent

The Hebrew Bible has a bronze object with a similar career. In the wilderness, Moses made a bronze serpent so that people bitten by snakes could look at it and live (Numbers 21). Centuries later, according to 2 Kings 18, the people were burning incense to it, and King Hezekiah broke it in pieces. The text gives it the name Nehushtan — roughly, "a bronze thing." It's traditionally read as Hezekiah's own dismissive name for it, though some translations have the people calling it that. Hezekiah's reasons were religious; Danxia's were practical, since he was cold. Both stories end once the object is named for what it's made of. In Danxia's, the word "wood" came out of the objector's own mouth.

## Protecting the process document

Most teams have a document, a meeting, or a template that was built for a real reason by someone people respected, and that has since become hard to touch. The weekly status report still goes out. The forty-field intake form is still required. The kickoff deck still opens with the founder's slides. Suggest cutting one of them and the objection is rarely about what it does. It's that it has always been done, or that the person who made it knew what they were doing.

Danxia's question works here almost word for word. If the report is still doing its job, point to the job, and it stays. If it isn't, then it's a document, and documents are allowed to be edited.

## Saving the good notebook

A good notebook sits on the shelf unused because nothing seems important enough to go in it first. The good pen, the bottle kept for an occasion, the expensive shoes saved for a race — each gets promoted from an object to a symbol of the person you'll be once you deserve it. Meanwhile the cheap notebook gets all the actual thinking. The good one was bought to be written in, and it can't do that from the shelf.

## In closing

Danxia burned a statue because he was cold, and when he was challenged he didn't defend himself on principle. He accepted the other man's reverence at face value and asked what, in that case, he'd done wrong. The statue was either the Buddha, with relics to show for it, or it was wood, and it had kept a monk warm.
