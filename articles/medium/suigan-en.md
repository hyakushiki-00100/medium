# After the Summer Retreat, a Teacher Asked His Monks to Check His Eyebrows. Three Teachers Answered, One of Them in a Single Word. The Zen Koan "Suigan Bimo" (翠巌眉毛).

At the end of the summer retreat, Cuiyan¹ spoke to his monks. "All summer I've been talking to you, brothers," he said. "Look: are my eyebrows still there?" Baofu² said, "A thief's heart is uneasy." Changqing³ said, "They've grown." Yunmen⁴ said, "Barrier."

## The penalty for a wrong word

In Chan stories, a teacher who speaks falsely about the teaching loses his eyebrows. It's a stock penalty, and this case is one of its best-known uses. So read plainly, Cuiyan's question isn't vanity. After a whole season of explaining things that are supposed to be beyond explanation, he seems to be asking whether he has said too much, or said it wrong.

That plain reading has a famous objector. Yuanwu, whose commentary makes up the Blue Cliff Record, wrote that many people take it this way: that Cuiyan owned up to his faults at the end of the summer and checked himself before anyone else could. He called that reading beside the point. For Yuanwu, the question was a hook let down to see who would bite, not a confession.

The three answers can be read on the surface: Baofu teases that only someone who has done wrong worries this much about being caught; Changqing says the eyebrows haven't fallen out, they've grown; Yunmen gives one word, the name for a checkpoint on a road, which can be heard as closing the subject or as saying the question itself is the place nobody gets through. Yuanwu also rejects one easy reading of Changqing's reply: that he was simply going along with Cuiyan. Commentators have read all three in more than one way, and the record doesn't settle it.

## Answers collected after the fact

Cuiyan Lingcan⁵ taught at Cuiyan in what is now Ningbo; his dates aren't recorded. He, Changqing Huileng (854–932), Baofu Congzhan (d. 928) and Yunmen Wenyan (864–949) were all students of the same teacher, Xuefeng. The three who answered were his fellow students, not his pupils, and in the early records they weren't necessarily in the room.

The earliest collection, the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁶, first compiled in 952 (though it survives only in a later, expanded Korean edition), has Cuiyan say that for thirty years not a day has gone by without his talking with his brothers. Nobody answers. When the story is later repeated to Changqing, he supplies an answer on their behalf: "They've grown." The Jingde-era record of the lamp⁷, in 1004, has "this summer" and still only Changqing.

By the mid-eleventh century, the end-of-summer setting and all three answers appear together, both in Yunmen's own recorded sayings and in the verses of Xuedou⁸, which later became the core of the Blue Cliff Record⁹. In Yunmen's record, he is told the story with Baofu's and Changqing's replies already attached, and adds his one word. Later compilations, from 1183 on, also attach a stock phrase from the teacher Cuiyan Shouzhi: he spent himself for the assembly and brought the trouble on himself.

## Pascal's long letter

In the sixteenth of his Provincial Letters, written in 1656 to the Jesuits he was arguing against, Blaise Pascal remarked that he had made this letter longer than usual only because he hadn't had the time to make it shorter. The quarrel is mostly forgotten; the line is still quoted, because it names something most people who explain things for a living recognize. Saying a lot is easy. Saying only what's needed takes more work.

## Checking in with the team

After a quarter's worth of all-hands meetings, a long onboarding, or a week spent explaining a reorganization, a manager asks the team whether any of it landed, or whether it was too much. The answers often sort themselves the way the three did. Someone teases: if you're asking, you already know. Someone says people got a lot out of it. Someone says almost nothing, which may be the most honest response in the room.

Each answer is worth having. The question underneath them is the plain-reading one: did all that talking leave the listeners better off, or was some of it for the speaker?

## After a long talk at home

A long conversation with a teenager, a partner, or a friend in trouble can end with the same quiet check: was that helpful, or was I lecturing? Asking it out loud, the way Cuiyan did, gets you something that keeping it to yourself can't: an answer from the other person, whether it's a joke, a kind word, or a short "Don't worry about it."

## In closing

Cuiyan had talked all summer, or for thirty years, depending on the record, and at the end he asked to have his eyebrows checked. On the surface, three answers came back: a jab, something like reassurance, and a single word. Whether he was confessing or setting a hook is still argued over. Either way, the question was worth asking out loud.
