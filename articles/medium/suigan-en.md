# After the Summer Retreat, a Teacher Asked His Monks to Check His Eyebrows. Three Teachers Answered, One of Them in a Single Word. The Zen Koan "Suigan Bimo" (翠巌眉毛).

At the end of the summer retreat, Cuiyan¹ spoke to his monks. "All summer I've been talking to you, brothers," he said. "Look: are my eyebrows still there?" Baofu² said, "A thief's heart is uneasy." Changqing³ said, "They've grown." Yunmen⁴ said, "Barrier."

## Why the eyebrows

In Chan stories, a teacher who speaks falsely about the teaching loses his eyebrows; it's a stock penalty, and this case is one of the places it comes from. So Cuiyan's question isn't vanity. After a whole season of explaining things that are supposed to be beyond explanation, he's asking his monks to check whether he has said too much, or said it wrong.

The three answers go three different ways. Baofu's is a jab: only someone who has actually done something wrong worries this much about being caught. Changqing's goes the other way: the eyebrows haven't fallen out; if anything they've grown. Yunmen's is one word, the name for a checkpoint on a road, and it can be read as closing the subject, or as saying the question itself is the place nobody gets through. Commentators have read all three in more than one way, and the case doesn't pick.

## Thirty years, then one summer

Cuiyan Lingcan⁵, an heir of Xuefeng, taught at Cuiyan in what is now Ningbo; his dates aren't recorded. Changqing Huileng (854–932) and Baofu Congzhan (d. 928) were fellow students under Xuefeng, and Yunmen Wenyan (864–949) became the most famous of that generation.

The oldest record, the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁶, compiled in 952, tells a different story. There Cuiyan says that for thirty years not a day has gone by without his talking with his brothers, and asks whether his eyebrows are still there. Nobody answers. When the question is reported to Changqing later, he supplies an answer on their behalf: "They've grown." The Jingde-era record of the lamp⁷, in 1004, shrinks the thirty years to "this summer" and still has only Changqing. By the Blue Cliff Record⁸, completed around 1125, it's the end of the summer retreat, and three answers stand side by side. The Compendium of the Five Lamps⁹, in 1252, keeps the three and adds a fourth line from a later teacher, Cuiyan Shouzhi: he wore himself out for the assembly, and the trouble came from inside his own gate. Each retelling, in other words, gave the old teacher a little more company in his worry.

## Pascal's long letter

In the sixteenth of his Provincial Letters, written in 1656, Blaise Pascal apologized to his reader for the length: he had made this letter longer than usual, he wrote, only because he hadn't had the time to make it shorter. The line has outlived the controversy it was written for, because it names something most people who explain things for a living recognize. Saying a lot is easy. Saying only what's needed takes more work.

## Asking the room afterward

After a quarter of all-hands meetings, a long onboarding, or a week of explaining a reorganization, a manager asks the team whether any of it landed, or whether it was too much. The answers that come back tend to sort themselves the way the monks' did. Someone teases: if you're asking, you already know. Someone reassures: people learned a lot. Someone says nothing much at all, which may be the most honest response in the room.

Each of those is useful. The question underneath them is the one Cuiyan put to himself: did all that talking leave the listeners better off, or was some of it for the speaker?

## After the long talk at home

A long conversation with a teenager, a partner, or a friend in trouble can end with the same quiet check: was that helpful, or was I lecturing? Asking it out loud, the way Cuiyan did, is better than replaying it alone afterward. The answer may be a joke, or a kind word, or a short "Don't worry about it." Any of the three tells you more than the replay would.

## In closing

Cuiyan had talked all summer, or for thirty years, depending on which record you read, and at the end he asked his monks to check his eyebrows. Three answers came back: a jab, a reassurance, and a single word. Not one was flattery, and all three took the question seriously.
