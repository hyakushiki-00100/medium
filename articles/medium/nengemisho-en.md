# "We Just Get Each Other" Isn't a Compliment. It's the Zen Story of "Holding Up a Flower."

"We've always just gotten each other — no explaining needed."

Said like that, it sounds like a nice bit of chemistry between two people. But trace the phrase back to its Zen root, and what you find isn't chemistry at all. It's a single flower, held up in silence, in a room full of people who had no idea what it meant.

## One flower, one smile

The story goes like this. The Buddha was giving a sermon on Vulture Peak. Instead of speaking, he simply lifted a flower and turned it slowly in his hand, showing it to the crowd.

No one understood. Except for one disciple, Mahakasyapa, who quietly smiled.

The Buddha is said to have responded: "I possess the treasury of the true dharma eye, the sublime mind of nirvana — and I now entrust it to Mahakasyapa." No sermon. No scripture. No further words at all. A flower, and a smile that showed someone had truly received it. This is the origin of the Zen phrase *nenge-mishō* (拈華微笑) — "holding up a flower, a subtle smile."

## This story probably never happened

Here's the honest part.

The scripture long cited as the source of this story, the *Sutra of the Questions of Brahma King* (大梵天王問仏決疑経), is now widely regarded by scholars as an apocryphal text — one composed in China long after the fact, not an authentic record of the Buddha's teaching. Earlier Buddhist accounts even place Mahakasyapa far from the Buddha's side at the time of his death, learning of it only a week later. Historically speaking, the scene likely never took place.

In writing, the story doesn't clearly settle into Zen literature until around 1183, in the *Compendium of the Linked Flame* (聯燈会要) — roughly 1,700 years after the Buddha's lifetime.

Which suggests something more interesting than a historical record: this is a story later generations distilled, like a single painted image, out of **the value Zen cares about most**. That's likely exactly why it survived so long. The textual and historical points above rest on a firm modern scholarly consensus — and whatever the facts of that morning on Vulture Peak, what Zen has actually carried forward was never the event itself, but the teaching the story holds.

## A culture that puts "unspeakable" straight into the alcove

Zen holds another core idea alongside this story: *furyū-monji, kyōge-betsuden* (不立文字・教外別伝) — the truth cannot be fully carried by words and letters alone. *Nenge-mishō* became the symbol that embodies this stance.

You can still feel this instinct in unexpected places. A single flower, placed in the alcove of a tea room. Not an arrangement that explains itself, not a bouquet that says too much — just one flower, set down quietly. To me, that aesthetic grows from the same root as the flower on Vulture Peak: instead of exhausting language to make a point, you offer one thing, and leave the rest to what the other person notices.

## "They should just get it" is usually an excuse

Here's where the phrase gets genuinely useful.

Most of us have expected a colleague to "just understand" without being told — and been disappointed when they didn't. But think again about Mahakasyapa. He wasn't able to smile because he was unusually sharp. He was able to smile because of years spent training beside the Buddha. The flower didn't create that understanding. It simply lit a fuse that had already been laid.

- **Am I explaining this for them, or for my own unease?** More slides often signal anxiety about being misunderstood, not an actual information gap.
- **Are they missing information, or am I missing trust?** Confuse the two, and you'll keep adding words to a problem words can't fix.
- **Can I wait instead?** Sometimes the more disciplined move is to hold back and let the smile — the sign it landed — arrive in its own time.

*Nenge-mishō* isn't telling you to stop talking. It's telling you that until you've built the kind of relationship where a smile is enough, the patient, unglamorous work of putting things into words still has to happen first.

## The flower was never the answer

What the Buddha handed the crowd wasn't an answer. It was a single flower — a question. Nearly everyone in the room fell silent. Only one person answered it with a smile.

Careful, thorough explanation is the right call in most situations. But without well-placed silence and trust built up over time sitting alongside it, you can hold up a flower to a room and no one will smile back.

Like the single flower in a tea room's alcove, sometimes the move is to set language down for a moment and simply face the person in front of you. That, too, is a small skill this old story hands down.
