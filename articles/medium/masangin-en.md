# He Was Asked What Buddha Is. He Answered With the Weight on His Scale. The Zen Koan "Masangin" (麻三斤).

A monk asks a teacher the biggest question there is: what is Buddha? Tradition says the teacher was in the storeroom at the time, weighing out hemp for the monastery's rope and cloth. He doesn't reach for a sutra, a metaphor, or a moment of dramatic silence. He looks up and says: three pounds of flax.

## A monk with a question, and a teacher mid-task

The teacher was Dongshan Shouchu (910–990), a student of the influential teacher Yunmen Wenyan. The exchange survives in two of Chan's major koan collections: Case 12 of the *Blue Cliff Record* (碧巌録), compiled by Yuanwu Keqin in 1125 as commentary on an earlier verse collection by Xuedou Chongxian (c. 1038), and Case 18 of the *Gateless Gate* (無門関), compiled by Wumen Huikai in 1228. Dongshan wasn't performing indifference, and he wasn't dodging the question.

## What the case actually says, and what tradition added

Worth being precise here. Both base texts record the same two lines: a monk asks what Buddha is, Dongshan answers three pounds of flax. Neither original case spells out the storeroom, the scale, or the hemp mid-weighing — that scene is the traditional context handed down alongside the exchange, repeated so consistently across commentary and retelling that it's effectively part of the story now, but it isn't in the base text word for word.

It's also worth resisting the easy version of the takeaway. Yuanwu's own commentary on Case 12 is unusually blunt about this: he warns that plenty of students think they've understood the exchange the moment they decide it simply means "answer with whatever's in front of you," and that this reading, treated as a formula, misses the point as thoroughly as reaching for grand abstraction would. Turning "stay concrete" into a technique is just a smaller kind of performance.

## Three pounds, one robe

There's a detail worth sitting with, even though the base text doesn't spell it out either: three catties of hemp was, by the standard of the time, roughly what it took to weave one monk's work robe. Asked for the largest possible answer, Dongshan is said to have given, in effect, the raw material of the clothes on his own back — not a symbol chosen for the occasion, just the actual weight on the scale that happened to equal a person's covering.

## The answer that refuses to rise to the question

There's a natural pull, when asked something enormous, to answer at the same scale — to reach for language that sounds proportionate to the size of the question. Dongshan's reply never rises to meet "what is Buddha" with anything resembling majesty. It stays exactly where he was standing, mid-task, in the plainest kind of storeroom labor. And per Yuanwu's own warning, the lesson isn't "always answer this way as a rule." It's closer to: notice how much effort usually goes into sounding equal to the question, and ask what that effort is actually for.

## Answering the big question with what's in your hands

- **The strategy answer that outruns the actual work.** Asked what the vision is, there's real pressure to supply something inspirational rather than the plainer, truer answer: here's what we're actually doing this week, and why.
- **Vocabulary borrowed to cover a gap.** Reaching for a framework, a buzzword, or someone else's confident phrasing often isn't proof of understanding — it's frequently what happens when the honest, specific answer feels too thin to say out loud on its own.
- **The room that rewards the bigger-sounding answer.** Meetings often reward whoever answers from the highest altitude, which quietly trains everyone to keep climbing rather than to stay accurate.

Copying Dongshan's answer word for word would just be a new kind of borrowed language — the point was never the specific words, but whether they were actually his. The test worth applying to your own answer is simpler: does it match what you actually know, or is it dressed for whoever's listening?

## The three pounds you're actually holding

Applied to yourself, this stops being about answers you give other people and becomes about the ones you give yourself. Asked what you're doing with your life, or whether you're on track, the same pull shows up: reach for an answer sized for the occasion — a five-year plan, a mission statement, something that belongs on a wall. The honest answer is usually smaller and more specific than that, and it's sitting right in front of you: the actual task, the actual conversation, the actual weight on today's scale.

## In closing

No one recorded whether the monk who asked walked away satisfied. What's recorded is only the weighing, and the answer, and nothing added to make either one sound like more than it was.
