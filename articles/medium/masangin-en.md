# He Was Asked What Buddha Is. He Answered With the Weight on His Scale. The Zen Koan "Masangin" (麻三斤).

A monk asks a teacher the biggest question there is: what is Buddha? The teacher doesn't reach for a sutra, a metaphor, or a moment of dramatic silence. He's in the storeroom when the monk finds him, weighing out hemp for the monastery's rope and cloth. He looks up and says: three pounds of flax.

## A monk weighing hemp in the storeroom

The teacher was Dongshan Shouchu (910–990), a student of the influential teacher Yunmen Wenyan. The exchange appears as Case 12 of the *Blue Cliff Record* (碧巌録), compiled by Yuanwu Keqin in 1125 as commentary on an earlier verse collection by Xuedou Chongxian (c. 1038). Dongshan wasn't performing indifference or dodging the question. By most readings, he simply said what was true at that exact moment: he was standing in the storeroom, mid-task, with three pounds of hemp on the scale.

## Two teachers named after the same mountain

Worth clearing up a genuine point of confusion, one that trips up even reference sources. There are two well-known Chan teachers called "Dongshan" — the name marks a mountain, not a family, and more than one abbot took it from the place he taught. Dongshan Liangjie (807–869) founded the Caodong school, a generation earlier and unconnected in lineage. Dongshan Shouchu, our monk with the hemp, came later, trained under Yunmen, and has nothing to do with founding Caodong beyond sharing the mountain's name. General-audience sources mix the two up constantly. Worth being the kind of reader who checks.

## The answer that refuses to rise to the question

There's a natural pull, when asked something enormous, to answer at the same scale — to reach for language that sounds proportionate to the size of the question. Dongshan doesn't do that. His answer never rises to meet "what is Buddha" with anything resembling majesty. It stays exactly where he was standing, mid-task, in the plainest kind of storeroom labor. If there's a joke here, it's aimed at anyone who expected ultimate truth to show up dressed for the occasion, rather than in whatever ordinary task happened to be in front of him at the time.

## Answering the big question with what's in your hands

- **The strategy answer that outruns the actual work.** Asked what the vision is, there's real pressure to supply something inspirational rather than the plainer, truer answer: here's what we're actually doing this week, and why.
- **Borrowed grandeur.** Reaching for language that sounds like it belongs to someone more important than you are — a framework, a buzzword, a borrowed metaphor — is often a tell that the honest, specific answer feels too small to offer out loud.
- **The room that rewards the bigger-sounding answer.** Meetings often reward whoever answers from the highest altitude, which quietly trains everyone to keep climbing.

That's not a case for having nothing to say. It's a check on whether the answer you're giving is actually true at the scale you're delivering it, or borrowed from a register that was never yours to begin with.

## The three pounds you're actually holding

Applied to yourself, this stops being about answers you give other people and becomes about the ones you give yourself. Asked what you're doing with your life, or whether you're on track, the same pull shows up: reach for an answer sized for the occasion — a five-year plan, a mission statement, something that belongs on a wall. The honest answer is usually smaller and more specific than that, and it's sitting right in front of you: the actual task, the actual conversation, the actual three pounds on today's scale.

## In closing

Dongshan didn't tell the monk to come back with a better question, and he didn't apologize for the size of his answer. Weighing hemp was what he was doing, so weighing hemp was what Buddha turned out to be — for exactly as long as it took to say so.
