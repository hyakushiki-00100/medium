# A Student Said the Birds Had Already Flown Away. His Teacher Grabbed His Nose and Twisted. The Zen Koan "Yaousu" (野鴨子).

Mazu¹ and his student Baizhang² were walking together when a flock of wild ducks passed overhead. "What's that," Mazu asked. "Wild ducks," Baizhang said. "Where have they gone?" "They've flown away." Mazu reached over, took hold of Baizhang's nose, and twisted it. Baizhang cried out. "When have they ever flown away?" Mazu said.

## An accurate answer, and the wrong tense

Baizhang was one of Mazu's students at the time, years before he'd go on to found a monastery of his own, and nothing he'd said was false. The ducks had, in fact, flown past and out of sight, and reporting that wasn't stupidity or inattention — it was the ordinary, reasonable way to answer a question about something you just watched happen. That was exactly the problem. The moment Baizhang spoke, the ducks became a fact about the past, something already filed away and settled, and he answered as a man commenting on an event rather than a man standing in the middle of one. The pain of a twisted nose had nowhere to go but the present tense, the one place his answer hadn't touched. There's no reporting that in the past tense. There's only feeling it.

## What the earliest version doesn't smooth over

The line most readers know — "when have they ever flown away?" — comes from the Blue Cliff Record³, compiled in 1125 from a verse commentary written roughly a century earlier. An earlier telling survives in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁴, compiled in 1004, more than two centuries after the exchange itself; an earlier chronicle still, the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁵ from 952, is often cited as containing this exchange too, though its exact wording there isn't independently confirmed. The 1004 version has Mazu say something blunter — closer to "you still say they flew past?" than the sharper line later readers know. It doesn't resolve on the spot, either: Baizhang has a stirring of insight, walks back to his room, and cries, and whatever finished the understanding apparently needed another day and another exchange with Mazu. The retelling didn't get truer over the centuries. It got tidier.

## Wild geese carry a different cargo in poetry

The bird in this exchange is a duck. The bird Japanese poets have reached for across centuries of waka and haiku is usually its cousin the wild goose — a separate seasonal word entirely, tied to the turning of autumn, to parting, to letters carried from someone far away. What geese share with the koan's ducks is simpler than any one of those meanings: a poet uses them because they're already leaving by the time anyone names them, gone from the sky before the line describing them is even finished. Mazu wasn't reaching for that kind of image. He wanted to know whether the actual ducks, in front of two actual men on an actual afternoon, needed one.

## The live dashboard, not the incident report

Someone reports an outage in a status meeting: it happened overnight, the immediate cause is understood, the incident is closed. That can be entirely accurate and still be operating in the wrong tense, if the underlying condition that let it happen hasn't actually gone anywhere. The useful next move is usually the dashboard itself, the current state of the thing, independent of how confidently anyone can now narrate what already occurred. A closed incident report and a resolved system are two different claims, and only one of them can be checked by looking at what's happening right now.

## The story about yourself, and what's actually here

People do this with their own history constantly — that temper, that old habit of disappearing when things got hard, that particular fear, filed as something that used to be a problem, back when. The filing isn't necessarily dishonest. It might even be mostly true. But "used to be" is a claim about the past, made by someone who is, at this exact moment, either reacting the old way or not, and the only way to find out which is to check what's actually running right now, not what the last update said about it.

## In closing

Nobody handed Baizhang an explanation. What reached him was a hand on his nose and a question with nowhere comfortable to file it, and by his own account it took the rest of that day to land. Whatever he understood, he understood while something was actually happening to him — not while describing something that already had.
