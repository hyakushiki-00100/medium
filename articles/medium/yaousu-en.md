# A Student Said the Birds Had Already Flown Away. His Teacher Grabbed His Nose and Twisted. The Zen Koan "Yaousu" (野鴨子).

Mazu¹ and his young student Baizhang² were walking a field path together when a flock of wild ducks passed overhead. "What's that," Mazu asked. "Wild ducks," Baizhang said. "Where have they gone?" "They've flown away." Mazu reached over, took hold of Baizhang's nose, and twisted it hard enough to make him cry out. "When have they ever flown away?" he said.

## An accurate answer, and the wrong tense

Nothing Baizhang said was false. The ducks had, in fact, flown past and out of sight, and reporting that wasn't stupidity or inattention — it was the ordinary, reasonable way to answer a question about something you just watched happen. That was exactly the problem. The moment Baizhang spoke, the ducks became a fact about the past, something already filed away and settled, and he answered as a man commenting on an event rather than a man standing in the middle of one. The twist landed in the present tense, the one place his answer hadn't been. There was no reporting a nose being wrenched sideways in real time. There was only feeling it.

## Recorded long before it became famous

The Blue Cliff Record³ is how this exchange reaches most readers today, assembled in the twelfth century from a much-quoted verse commentary written a century or so before that. The story itself is considerably older than either. It appears in the Anthology of the Patriarchs' Hall⁴, compiled in 952, and again in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁵, compiled in 1004 — both finished well within two centuries of the event they describe, long before the version most readers actually encounter today. The core of the exchange hasn't drifted across that gap: a plain question about birds, a plain answer, and a teacher unwilling to let the plainness pass as understanding.

## An image poets have used for the opposite point

Wild geese crossing an autumn sky are one of the oldest stock images in Japanese poetry, invoked for centuries as a symbol of a season, or a mood, or a moment that's already ending the instant it's noticed. A poet reaches for the birds precisely because they represent something passing — the whole point of putting them in a line of verse is to let them stand for transience itself. Mazu wasn't reaching for a symbol. He wanted to know whether the actual birds, right there, in front of two actual men on an actual path, needed one.

## Checking whether it's still happening, not whether it happened

Someone reports an outage in a status meeting: it happened overnight, the immediate cause is understood, the incident is closed. That can be entirely accurate and still be the wrong tense to be operating in, if the underlying condition that let it happen hasn't actually gone anywhere. The useful next move usually isn't accepting the narration. It's checking the live dashboard, the current state of the thing itself, independent of how confidently anyone can now describe what already occurred. A closed incident report and a resolved system are two different claims, and only one of them can be verified by looking at what's happening right now.

## The story about yourself, and what's actually here

People do this with their own history constantly — that temper, that old habit of disappearing when things got hard, that particular fear, filed as something that used to be a problem, back when. The filing isn't necessarily dishonest. It might even be mostly true. But "used to be" is a claim about the past, made by someone who is, at this exact moment, either reacting the old way or not, and the only way to find out which is to check what's actually running right now, not what the last update said about it.

## In closing

Baizhang didn't get an explanation. He got a hand on his nose and a question with no comfortable place to file it. Whatever he understood after that, he understood it while something was actually happening to him — not while describing something that already had.
