# The Final Picture Isn't Enlightenment. It's a Man Covered in Mud, Laughing. The Zen Image "Nitten Suishu" (入鄽垂手).

Chest bare, feet bare, smeared with dirt and ash, grinning ear to ear, a man walks into a market town full of wine shops and fish stalls. This is the tenth and final picture in one of Zen's most famous visual sequences — and it comes after complete realization, not before it.

## A different ending to the same circle

The sequence is the *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*¹, most widely known today, at least in Japan and the West, in the version attributed to Kuoan Shiyuan², a 12th-century Chan master of the Linji lineage. An earlier version, generally attributed to a monk named Qingju and usually said to predate Kuoan's own, ended at a single empty circle: the ox and the seeker searching for it both forgotten, nothing left to picture at all. Kuoan judged that ending incomplete, and built a ten-stage sequence of his own that carries the circle two stages further.

## What happens after the circle

In Kuoan's sequence, the circle is the eighth of ten stages — nothing drawn, nothing left of either the ox or the self that had been chasing it. The ninth shows nature exactly as it is, a river running, blossoms opening, with no person pictured anywhere in the frame. Only at the tenth does a human figure reappear at all — and not as someone transformed into something rarefied. He walks straight back into the noise of an ordinary market.

## The words that come with the picture

The prose introduction attached to the tenth image — traditionally credited to a monk named Ciyuan, said in some accounts to have been Kuoan's student and in others merely a close associate — reads, in part: the brushwood gate stands shut, and not even the thousand sages know what's behind it; he buries his own radiance, not even following the path the old sages walked; gourd in hand, he enters the market, then leans on his staff and heads home; in wine shops and fish stalls alike, he turns whoever he meets into someone awake³. Kuoan's own verse adds the image everyone remembers: chest bared, feet bare, he enters the market caked in dirt and ash, grinning from ear to ear — no immortal's secret technique required, just the plain fact of a withered tree bursting back into flower. Exactly how the prose and verse were divided between the two men, and how much of Qingju's original survives outside later summaries, isn't settled with full confidence — but the broad shape both sides agree on is an earlier sequence that stopped at emptiness, and a fuller one that didn't.

## Why the circle was never the finish line

The empty circle looks, at a glance, like the obvious place to end: nothing left to want, nothing left to lose. Kuoan's version argues the opposite — that stopping there is its own quiet form of hiding. Two more stages were needed precisely because emptiness, held onto as an achievement, becomes one more thing to protect from the mess of ordinary contact — proof that realization isn't finished until it's unrecognizable from everyone else buying fish at the market.

## What competence quietly buys you

The instinct the tenth picture argues against shows up plainly at work. Someone spends years earning real expertise, then starts operating from a small distance above the people still doing the messy version of the job — reviewing rather than doing, advising rather than getting hands dirty, protecting the achievement rather than spending it. Distance is just what accumulated competence tends to produce, quietly and mostly without any arrogance behind it, unless something pushes back against it.

The tenth picture pushes back on purpose. The mud and the ash aren't an accident the awakened man failed to avoid. They're the point — proof he actually went back.

## The same instinct, aimed at your own past

Recovering from something, learning a hard lesson, finally understanding a pattern in yourself — real growth like this can calcify into an identity held slightly apart from people still in the middle of the same struggle. Watching from a small height feels like wisdom. It's closer to the empty circle mistaken for the whole path: technically true, and quietly unwilling to get dirty again.

## In closing

The man in the tenth picture isn't recognizable as anyone special. That's the entire achievement pictured. Ten pictures build toward someone laughing, smeared in the same dirt as everyone else in the market, having apparently arrived nowhere at all — except back among the people he started with, minus whatever was keeping him separate from them in the first place.
