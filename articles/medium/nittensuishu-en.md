# The Final Picture Isn't Enlightenment. It's a Man Covered in Mud, Laughing. The Zen Image "Nitten Suishu" (入鄽垂手).

Chest bare, feet bare, smeared with dirt and ash, grinning ear to ear, a man walks into a market town full of wine shops and fish stalls. This is the tenth and final picture in one of Zen's most famous visual sequences — and it comes right after complete realization, not before it.

## A ten-picture answer to an eight-picture ending

The sequence is the *Ten Ox-Herding Pictures*¹, most widely known today in the version attributed to Kuoan Shiyuan², a 12th-century Chan master of the Linji lineage. An earlier version by a monk named Seikyo had existed before his, ending at a single empty circle: the ox and the seeker searching for it both forgotten, nothing left to picture at all. According to the preface his student Jien wrote for the collection, Kuoan judged that ending incomplete, and added two further pictures past the emptiness.

## What happens after the circle

The eighth picture, in Kuoan's version, is still that same plain circle — nothing drawn, nothing left of either the ox or the self that had been chasing it. The ninth shows nature exactly as it is, a river running, blossoms opening, with no person pictured anywhere in the frame. Only at the tenth does a human figure reappear at all — and not as someone transformed into something rarefied. He walks straight back into the noise of an ordinary market.

## The verse that comes with the picture

Kuoan's own preface to the tenth image reads, in part: the brushwood gate stands shut, and not even the thousand sages know what's behind it; he buries his own radiance, yet walks the same road the old masters walked; gourd in hand, he enters the market, then leans on his staff and heads home; in wine shops and fish stalls alike, he turns whoever he meets into someone awake³. His verse adds the image everyone remembers: chest bared, feet bare, he enters the market caked in dirt and ash, grinning from ear to ear — no immortal's secret technique required, just the plain fact of a withered tree bursting back into flower.

## Why the circle was never the finish line

The empty circle looks, at a glance, like the obvious place to end: nothing left to want, nothing left to lose. Kuoan's addition argues the opposite — that stopping there is its own quiet form of hiding. Two more pictures were needed precisely because emptiness, held onto as an achievement, becomes one more thing to protect from the mess of ordinary contact. The tenth picture isn't a reward tacked onto enlightenment. It's the claim that enlightenment isn't finished until it's unrecognizable from everyone else buying fish at the market.

## The advisor who won't get back in the water

The instinct the tenth picture argues against shows up plainly at work. Someone spends years earning real expertise, then starts operating from a small distance above the people still doing the messy version of the job — reviewing rather than doing, advising rather than getting hands dirty, protecting the achievement rather than spending it. None of this happens out of arrogance most of the time. Distance is just what accumulated competence tends to produce, quietly, unless something pushes back against it.

The tenth picture pushes back on purpose. The mud and the ash aren't an accident the awakened man failed to avoid. They're the point — proof he actually went back in.

## The same distance, with no one else around

Recovering from something, learning a hard lesson, finally understanding a pattern in yourself — real growth like this can calcify into an identity held slightly apart from people still in the middle of the same struggle. Watching from a small height feels like wisdom. It's closer to the empty circle mistaken for the whole path: technically true, and quietly unwilling to get dirty again.

## In closing

The man in the tenth picture isn't recognizable as anyone special. That's the entire achievement pictured. Ten pictures build toward someone laughing, smeared in the same dirt as everyone else in the market, having apparently arrived nowhere at all — except back among the people he started with, minus whatever was keeping him separate from them in the first place.

³ Kuoan's preface and verse survive in classical Chinese ("柴門獨掩，千聖不知...酒肆魚行，化令成佛" and "露胸跣足入鄽來，抹土塗灰笑滿腮，不用神仙眞祕訣，直敎枯木放花開"); the rendering above follows the sense of the original rather than a strict word-for-word translation.
