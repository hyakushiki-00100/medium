# The Zen Word "Un (吽)" — What a Single Closed-Mouth Character Teaches Us About Silence and Endings

## What this article covers

"Un" (吽) is one of the single-character Zen words often found on hanging scrolls in Japanese tea rooms. In this article, we'll explore where this character comes from, what it means, and how its two core ideas — *closing the mouth* and *bringing things to an end* — can be put to work in everyday life.

One clarification before we begin, for accuracy's sake: "Un" did not originate within Zen Buddhism itself. It is a phonetic transcription of the Sanskrit syllable *hūṃ*, a character treasured in Esoteric Buddhism as a mantra. In Japan, however, it became deeply woven into Zen culture — through the closed-mouthed guardian statues (ungyō) at temple gates, the komainu lion-dogs at shrines, and its use as a one-character Zen word in the tea ceremony. It is best understood as a Buddhist term that came to be *treated as* a Zen word.

---

## What kind of character is "Un"?

In the Sanskrit writing system (Siddham script), "A" (阿) is the first sound, made with the mouth fully open, while "Un" (吽, *hūṃ*) is the last sound, made with the mouth fully closed. In other words, "Un" is **the single character that symbolizes the final destination of all sounds and all words.**

Esoteric Buddhism layered further meanings onto this one syllable: the wisdom to which all things return, and *nirvana* — the completion of awakening. Where "A" represents the origin from which everything arises, "Un" represents the ending, the place to which everything comes home. It is the second half of a paired worldview.

Picture the fierce Niō guardian statues at a temple gate. The statue on the right has its mouth open (*agyō*); the one on the left has its mouth firmly shut (*ungyō*). The komainu at Shinto shrines follow the same pattern. That closed-mouthed figure *is* "Un" — **the taut stillness that exists just before a word is spoken, or just after the last word has been said.**

---

## Why is a closed-mouth character so revered?

We moderns tend to place our value on *output*. Voicing opinions on social media, speaking up in meetings, pitching ideas — the skills of *opening the mouth* (the "A" side) are taught everywhere.

The skills of *closing the mouth* (the "Un" side) are taught almost nowhere.

But think back for a moment.

- The most trusted person in a meeting is rarely the one who talks the most. Isn't it **the one who listens all the way through, then says a single sentence?**
- That night you drafted a furious reply to an infuriating email — and stopped just short of hitting Send. How much did **the choice *not* to send** save you the next morning?
- Sitting beside a friend in grief, unable to find anything clever to say, simply staying there in silence. Haven't you later been told, "It helped that you were just there"?

All of these are the workings of "Un." **Silence is not the absence of words; it is a response that goes beyond words.** No one demonstrates this more eloquently than a guardian statue that says nothing at all.

---

## "Un" as the power to finish things

"Un" has a second face: as the symbol of "the terminus of all things," it stands for **the power to bring things properly to a close.**

In daily life, finishing things is, in truth, harder than starting them.

- The proposal left half-written
- The friend you told "let's catch up soon" — and never did
- The meeting that drags on with no one willing to end it
- The relationship carried forward, unresolved, for years

When things that have begun never end, they keep occupying memory in the background. Psychology describes how unfinished tasks linger in the mind more stubbornly than finished ones (a phenomenon known as the Zeigarnik effect) — the unclosed quietly drains us.

The single character "Un" teaches: **close your affairs the way you close your mouth.** Finish today's work today. Once you've said what needed saying, say no more. Release a relationship that has ended, with gratitude. Just as the Japanese syllabary ends with "n" (its ordering is said to have been influenced by the Sanskrit alphabet), **everything has a sound with which it can be beautifully closed.**

---

## Practicing "Un" in daily life — three scenes

### 1. "Un" at the end of the day

Five minutes before sleep: put the phone down, close your mouth, and exhale fully through the nose. Put a period on the day — "this is where today ends." Leave tomorrow's worries to tomorrow's "A." This alone changes the quality of your sleep.

### 2. "Un" in conversation

When the other person finishes speaking, don't answer immediately — stay silent for just one breath. That one-second "Un" leaves them with the feeling of having been truly received. The heart of good listening lies in exactly this one-beat pause.

### 3. "Un" before pressing Send

After writing an emotionally charged message, close your lips and read it once more before sending. Ask: "Is this something tomorrow's me would still want to send?" The closed-mouthed Niō is, in effect, **a gatekeeper standing guard in front of your outbox.**

---

## In closing — what lives inside the closed mouth

The ungyō statue's mouth is closed, but it is not asleep. Its whole body is charged with strength, eyes wide open, ready to move at any instant — and it deliberately does not speak. **"Un" is silence with power stored inside it: stillness with a will.**

Precisely because we live in an age overflowing with words, the weight of a single closed-mouth character stands out. The next time you pass through a temple or shrine gate, pause for a moment before the statue with its lips sealed. The face that says nothing may turn out to be the one that tells you the most.

## Appendix: Sorting fact from interpretation

- "Un" (吽) is a phonetic transcription of Sanskrit *hūṃ*, regarded as the closed-mouth sound placed last in the Sanskrit alphabet (fact — standard dictionary definition).
- In Esoteric Buddhism, "A" symbolizes the origin from which all things arise, and "Un" the terminus to which all things return — associated with wisdom and nirvana (fact — per Buddhist reference dictionaries).
- The closed-mouthed statues among the Niō guardians and komainu are called *ungyō* (fact).
- The single character 吽 is used as a one-character Zen word on tea-ceremony scrolls (fact — listed in tea-ceremony Zen-word compilations).
- The theory that the Japanese syllabary's ordering was influenced by the Sanskrit alphabet is the widely cited standard account, though scholars still debate the details of its formation — treat it as a well-supported tradition rather than a settled fact.
- The reference to the Zeigarnik effect is the author's modern framing, not a Zen teaching.
