# The Golden Wind Was the Whole Answer. The Zen Case "Tai Ro Kin Pu" (體露金風).

A monk asked Yunmen: when the tree withers and the leaves fall, then what? Yunmen answered: the body, exposed in the golden wind.

## A question about ending, answered without a story

Yunmen Wenyan¹, a dharma heir of Xuefeng Yicun and founder of his own teaching lineage, was known for compressing entire exchanges into a handful of words. Here a monk hands him the oldest kind of question there is — dressed as a question about trees, but really about what's left once everything that made a thing recognizable has dropped away. Yunmen doesn't answer with a doctrine, a comfort, or a denial that the withering is happening at all. He names the weather. That's the whole reply.

## The ordinary word behind the image

"Golden wind" (金風) wasn't poetic license invented for this exchange. In the traditional Chinese five-phase system, autumn corresponds to metal, and metal's color is gold — so "golden wind" was simply the ordinary period term for the wind of autumn, the season when leaves fall. Yunmen isn't reaching for a striking metaphor. He's using the plainest available word for exactly what's happening outside, at exactly the moment the monk is asking about it.

## A verse compiled a century later, and a caution built into it

The exchange survives today mainly through Xuedou Chongxian²'s verse commentary, compiled roughly a century after Yunmen's death, and later folded into the fuller *Blue Cliff Record*³ with commentary by Yuanwu Keqin in 1125. Whether the exchange was written down any earlier remains unconfirmed. Xuedou's own opening lines don't try to crack the exchange open further: the question already carried its own logic, he wrote, and the answer simply matched it⁴ — three words settle it, one arrow crossing open sky. He isn't adding an interpretation on top of Yunmen's answer. He's pointing out that there was never a gap for one to fill.

## The reply that refuses to soften or dramatize

Most people, handed a question about decline, reach for one of two moves: soften it with reassurance, or dramatize it into tragedy. Yunmen does neither. He doesn't say the withering isn't real, and he doesn't treat it as a crisis requiring a response. He states the exact condition of the tree, in the exact season it's occurring, and stops. The absence of commentary is the commentary.

## Where the explaining usually starts

The instinct to add a story shows up hard whenever something at work is visibly on its way down: a product losing relevance, a team getting smaller, a role that used to matter now not needing to exist. People reach immediately for a frame — this is temporary, this is actually an opportunity, this is someone's fault — because the plain fact by itself feels unbearable to just leave standing there. Most of that framing is a way of not looking directly at the season that's actually underway.

## In closing

Yunmen's monk didn't ask for a diagnosis and didn't get a eulogy. He asked what happens when a thing stops being what it was, and got back only a description of the weather he was already standing in. A harder, quieter version of the same question waits on the other side of it: what it actually feels like from the inside when a person, not a tree, is the one exposed — and what naming that plainly, instead of explaining it away, actually does.

³ The *Blue Cliff Record* (碧巌録), Case 27, compiled with commentary by Yuanwu Keqin (圜悟克勤) in 1125.
⁴ Xuedou's verse survives in classical Chinese, opening: "問既有宗，答亦攸同，三句可辨，一鏃遼空" ("the question already had its own principle, the answer matched it in kind; the point can be told apart in three words, like a single arrow crossing open sky"); the rendering above follows the sense of the original rather than a strict word-for-word translation. The verse's closing lines, widely read as a reference to Bodhidharma, aren't addressed here.
