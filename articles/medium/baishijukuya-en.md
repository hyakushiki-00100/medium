# His Teacher Changed the Answer. He Refused to Change His Mind. The Zen Story of "Baishi Jukuya" (梅子熟也).

A monk travels to a mountain hermitage to test its famous resident. Years earlier, this hermit — Damei Fachang — had asked his teacher, Mazu Daoyi, a single question: what is Buddha? Mazu's answer, "this very mind is Buddha," landed so completely that Damei left for the mountains that day and never went back to ask anything else. Now the monk delivers his message: your teacher doesn't say that anymore. These days Mazu teaches "no mind, no Buddha." Damei doesn't blink. "Let him teach whatever he wants," he says. "I'm sticking with 'this mind is Buddha.'" When the monk reports this back, Mazu doesn't call it stubbornness. He says: "The plum has ripened."

## A question asked once, and answered

Damei Fachang (752–839) came to Chan practice in his twenties and eventually found his way to Mazu Daoyi, one of the most influential teachers of the Tang dynasty. He didn't linger long as one of Mazu's many students. The first time he put a direct question to him — what is Buddha? — the answer he got arrived as a single, personal exchange, not a line absorbed secondhand from years of sitting in a crowded hall. It landed completely. Damei withdrew to a mountain that legend associates with Mei Fu, a Han-dynasty official turned recluse better known by his courtesy name, Zizhen — "the Plum." He stayed there roughly forty years, deepening a realization that had arrived, by tradition's account, in one sentence.

## Two different documents, one story

Worth being precise about where this story actually comes from. The enlightening exchange itself — Mazu's answer, "this mind is Buddha" — is genuinely Case 30 of the *Gateless Gate* (無門関), compiled by Wumen Huikai in 1228. But the case is just that one line. The later test, and Mazu's "the plum has ripened" verdict, come from a separate source: Damei's biographical entry in the *Transmission of the Lamp* (景徳伝灯録), presented to the Song court in 1004, itself over a century after Damei's death. Worth knowing, too, that Wumen's own commentary on Case 30 isn't simple praise — he warns that Damei's line has misled plenty of people into treating it as a fixed marker, and that anyone who really understood it would, on hearing it recited yet again, want to cover his ears and walk off. Even the phrase's wording has a minor wrinkle: the Gateless Gate has Mazu say 即心是仏, while accounts of Damei's later reply use 即心即仏 — functionally the same claim, in slightly different dress.

## Why the teacher moved the target

Mazu's shift to "no mind, no Buddha" wasn't a retraction. Other students had started reciting "this mind is Buddha" the way people recite any line repeated past the point of meaning — confidently, correctly, and with nothing much happening behind it. Mazu changed the words specifically to break that habit and force a moment of real confusion back into teaching that had calcified into a slogan. It worked on the people who needed it. It had no effect on Damei, because Damei had never held the phrase as a slogan in the first place — he'd had one direct exchange, at a moment he badly needed an answer, and the words were simply the shape that arrival took. Even Wumen's warning about clinging to the phrase doesn't really land on him: Damei isn't defending four characters. He's reporting what's still true for him.

## The joke inside the verdict

The line Mazu is said to have used when he heard about the test wasn't praise exactly, but a small joke resting on Damei's own name: 大梅, "Great Plum." *Meizi shu ye* — the plum has ripened. Not "he passed." Not "he's right." Ripened, as in: this was never going to happen on anyone's schedule but its own, and it wasn't about to start now just because a messenger showed up with news.

## Chasing the updated position at work

- **Updating the moment the room does.** When a respected voice changes their public stance, the reflex is to shift with them — even when your own understanding never actually came from secondhand repetition to begin with.
- **Mistaking movement for growth.** Constantly revising your stated positions can look like intellectual honesty. Sometimes it's the opposite: evidence that nothing was ever tested deeply enough to hold still.
- **Reading consensus as proof.** When everyone around you shifts position at once, it's tempting to take that as evidence the new position is correct — rather than as evidence that most people's original position was never tested very hard.

Mazu's second answer wasn't wrong for the people who needed it. What actually matters is knowing which one you are, on any given belief: reciting a formula, or someone who tested it directly and found it held.

## What you'd refuse to update

Applied to your own convictions, the question gets narrower than "stay confident." It asks whether there's anything you hold the way Damei held his answer — not defensively, not because you're afraid to look wrong, but because you checked it once, directly, and it hasn't come loose since. Most opinions aren't that. The ones that are don't need managing. They're just what's left standing after everything else got tested and moved on.

## In closing

Mazu never sent a second monk to argue the point. There wasn't an argument left to have. A plum, once it's actually ripe, doesn't check with the branch before it falls.
