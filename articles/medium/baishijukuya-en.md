# His Teacher Changed the Answer. He Refused to Change His Mind. The Zen Story of "Baishi Jukuya" (梅子熟也).

A monk travels to a mountain hermitage to test its famous resident. Years earlier, this hermit — Damei Fachang — had asked his teacher, Mazu Daoyi, a single question: what is Buddha? Mazu's answer, "this very mind is Buddha," hit him so hard he left for the mountains that day and never came back to ask anything else. Now the monk delivers his message: your teacher doesn't say that anymore. These days Mazu teaches "no mind, no Buddha." Damei doesn't blink. "Let him teach whatever he wants," he says. "I'm sticking with 'this mind is Buddha.'" When the monk reports this back, Mazu doesn't call it stubbornness. He says: "The plum is ripe."

## Forty years on a mountain named for a plum

Damei Fachang (752–839) had, by his own era's standards, already done the reading. Ordained young, he spent years memorizing scripture before turning to Chan practice and eventually studying under Mazu Daoyi, one of the most influential teachers of the Tang dynasty. The line that undid him — "this very mind is Buddha" — wasn't new; Mazu had been teaching some version of it for years. But Damei heard it differently, was immediately and thoroughly enlightened, and withdrew to a mountain that Han-dynasty legend held was once home to a hermit surnamed Mei — Plum. He stayed there roughly forty years, deepening a realization that had arrived, by tradition's account, in a single sentence.

## Worth being honest about the record

This story survives mainly through the *Transmission of the Lamp* (景徳伝灯録), compiled by the monk Daoyuan and presented to the Song court in 1004 — over a century and a half after Damei's death, in a genre built to celebrate lineage more than to document it precisely. The core exchange also appears, slightly compressed, as Case 30 of the *Gateless Gate* (無門関), compiled in 1228. What's stable across versions is the shape of the story; the exact wording belongs to tradition, not to a transcript.

## Why the teacher moved the target

Mazu's shift to "no mind, no Buddha" wasn't a retraction. Students had started reciting "this mind is Buddha" the way people recite any formula repeated past the point of meaning — confidently, correctly, and without much actually happening inside them. Mazu changed the words specifically to break that habit, to force a moment of genuine confusion back into teaching that had calcified into a slogan. It worked on the people who needed it. It had no effect on Damei, because Damei wasn't reciting a slogan. He'd never been taught the formula in the abstract — he'd had a single, direct experience, and the words were just the container it happened to arrive in.

## The word that made the joke work

The line Mazu is said to have used when he heard about the test wasn't praise exactly, but a small joke resting on Damei's own name: 大梅, "Great Plum." *Meizi shu ye* — the plum has ripened. Not "he passed." Not "he's right." Ripened, as in: this was never going to happen on anyone's schedule but its own, and it wasn't about to start now. A monk who'd genuinely realized something didn't need re-testing every time the test changed.

## Chasing the updated answer at work

- **The latest reframing.** Every time a new methodology, book, or influential voice restates a familiar idea, there's a scramble to adopt the new vocabulary — often by people who never fully understood the old one.
- **Mistaking movement for growth.** Constantly updating your stated beliefs can look like intellectual honesty. Sometimes it's the opposite: evidence that nothing was ever tested deeply enough to hold still.
- **The authority who changes their mind.** When a mentor, a boss, or a public expert shifts position, the reflex is to shift with them. Worth asking first whether your own understanding came from them, or from something you actually verified yourself.

Mazu's second answer wasn't wrong for the people who needed it — some students genuinely needed shaking loose. What actually matters is knowing which one you are, on any given belief: reciting a formula, or someone who tested it and found it held.

## What you'd refuse to update

Turned inward, the story asks a narrower question than "stay confident." It asks whether there's anything you hold the way Damei held his answer — not defensively, not because you're afraid to look wrong, but because you actually checked, once, thoroughly, and it hasn't come loose since. Most opinions aren't that. The ones that are don't need managing. They're just what's left after everything else got tested and moved on.

## In closing

Mazu never sent a second monk to argue the point. He didn't need to. Damei's mountain had a name that had been waiting for him before he ever arrived on it, and a plum, once it's actually ripe, doesn't ask the branch for a second opinion.
