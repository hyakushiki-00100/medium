# His Teacher Asked Why He Was Planting So Many Pines Deep in the Mountains. The Monk Gave Two Reasons, Then Struck the Ground With His Hoe. The Zen Story "Rinzai Saisho" (臨済栽松).

Linji¹ was planting pines when his teacher, Huangbo², came by. "Deep in the mountains like this," Huangbo said, "what are you planting so many for?" Linji gave two reasons. "One, to give the monastery some scenery. Two, as a landmark for the people who come after." Then he struck the ground three times with his hoe. "Be that as it may," said Huangbo, "you've already taken thirty blows from me." Linji struck the ground three more times and blew out a long breath, twice. "Through you," Huangbo said, "our school will flourish in the world."

## A view now, a landmark later

Linji's reasons are ordinary ones. The first is for the monastery as it is: a few more trees around the place. The second is for people who come later. A pine planted as a seedling takes decades to grow into a landmark anyone could point to, and by then the man who planted it would likely be gone. (The word he used for "landmark" can also mean an example to follow.) He didn't present this as a sacrifice. He named the purpose and went back to the ground.

Huangbo's reply is left unexplained in the record. It's usually read less as a rebuke than as a test, or a backhanded kind of approval. Whatever he meant, Linji didn't argue or add a third reason. He struck the ground again. Huangbo's last line, that the school would flourish through Linji, looks past both of them. A conversation that began with trees for people who come later ends on the same note.

## Once or twice, then three times

Linji Yixuan³ (birth year unrecorded, d. 866) gave his name to the school later known in Japan as Rinzai; Huangbo Xiyun⁴ (d. around 850) was his teacher. The Record of Linji in the form read today goes back to an edition printed in 1120. The pine-planting scene is older than that edition: it appears, almost word for word, in the Tiansheng-era Extended Record of the Lamp⁵, compiled in 1036. There are small differences. In 1036, Linji strikes the ground "once or twice," and then twice; in the 1120 edition, it's three times both times. The count keeps moving after that: the commentary in the Blue Cliff Record, from around 1125, has a single strike each time. The earlier Jingde-era record of the lamp, from 1004, doesn't include the scene at all.

The Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁶ does have a different monk planting pines. Qinglin, training under Dongshan, wrote a verse after planting pines there: "Barely a foot tall, thin over the green grass. Who knows which generation will see this pine grow old?"

## Honi and the carob tree

The Babylonian Talmud (Taanit 23a) tells of Honi, a sage known as the circle-drawer, who saw a man planting a carob tree and asked how long it would take to bear fruit. Seventy years, the man said. Honi asked whether he expected to live another seventy years. The man said that he had found carob trees already growing in the world, and that just as his ancestors had planted them for him, he was planting for his descendants. Honi then fell asleep for seventy years, and woke to see the planter's grandson gathering the fruit.

## Writing for whoever inherits the system

Most of what keeps a team working after someone leaves is the kind of thing nobody asked for at the time: the setup notes, the explanation of why a strange decision was made, the test that catches the mistake the next person will certainly make. It rarely shows up in a quarterly review. It's planted for someone who hasn't been hired yet. If someone asks why you're spending the afternoon on it, Linji's answer is about right: it makes the place better now, and it gives the next person something to find their way by.

## Paying off after you've stopped checking

Learning a language at forty-five, putting money away for a child's education, reading to a kid who won't remember the particular books — each of these is planted on a schedule you don't control, for people whose benefit you may not get to see. Linji's two reasons work here too. Does it improve the place now? Will it help whoever comes along later? If either is true, it doesn't need a third reason.

## In closing

Asked why he was planting so many pines so deep in the mountains, Linji named two plain purposes, one for the present and one for people who would come later, and kept digging. The conversation ended with his teacher talking about the future too.
