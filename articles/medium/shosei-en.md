# As He Addressed His Monks, a Zen Teacher Heard the Monastery Bell. He Asked His Monks Why They Answer It. The Zen Koan "Shosei Shichijo" (鐘声七条).

Yunmen¹ was addressing the monks in the hall when the bell rang. He said: "The world is this wide. Why do you put on your seven-panel robe² at the sound of a bell?"

The earliest record stops there. It gives no answer from the monks, and none from Yunmen. Collections compiled centuries later add that the monks said nothing and Yunmen supplied a line of his own.

## A robe for the hall

In a Chan monastery, bells, drums and wooden boards marked the day. In the monastic codes of the Song era, a bell woke the monks and a bell announced the meal, and one code tells monks who hear the bell after breakfast to come properly robed. The seven-panel robe was the one they wore over the others for assemblies and ceremonies. When the signal came, putting it on was simply what you did next. You didn't stop to decide.

That's what makes the question awkward. Read plainly, it leaves the bell where it is and asks whether any of them knew why they answered it. The world is wide enough to go anywhere and do anything; one sound, and everyone reaches for the same robe.

## The oldest surviving text

The line doesn't appear in the two earliest histories to cover Yunmen's generation, the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall of 952 and the Jingde record of 1004, even though both have chapters on him. The oldest surviving text is the edition of his recorded sayings³ printed in 1076, more than a century after his death; its preface says it corrects errors in copies already in circulation. There it is one line in a long sequence of talks: "Hearing the bell, he said: the world is this wide; why put on the seven-panel robe at the bell?"

In 1228 Wumen⁴ made it case 16 of the Gateless Barrier, in a slightly different wording that had been around for some time: why put on the robe *within* the sound of the bell? His comment begins with a warning against chasing sounds and sights, and then turns the warning around. Even being awakened to the Way by a sound, or seeing into the mind at the sight of something, he says, is commonplace; a real monk rides on sounds and sits on top of sights. Then he asks: does the sound come to the ear, or does the ear go out to the sound? He closes with a couplet borrowed from an older Chan verse: listen with the ear and it's hard to get; hear the sound with the eye, and you're close. His verse repeats the same two lines with the conditions swapped: if you understand, everything is one family; if you don't, there are a thousand differences. If you don't understand, everything is one family; if you do, there are a thousand differences.

## A rule written in Italy

In the sixth century, Benedict of Nursia⁵ wrote a rule for monks in Italy. Chapter 43 tells them that at the hour of prayer, as soon as the signal is heard, they're to put down whatever is in their hands and hurry to the oratory, with seriousness rather than giddiness. It states the principle in the next breath: nothing is to be put before the work of God. The same chapter even specifies where latecomers have to stand.

Both kinds of monastery ran their days by signals, and both expected monks to answer them promptly. Benedict's rule hands the monk the principle. Yunmen's question, read this way, leaves the monks to find their own.

## The meeting on the calendar

A working week is shaped by signals: the reminder five minutes before the call, the Monday meeting that's been on the calendar for three years, the phone that buzzes as you sit down to think. You answer most of them, and you should. A team where everyone ignores the signals doesn't function.

But some of those meetings have outlived their purpose, and some of the signals belong to someone else's priorities. A useful exercise is to pick one recurring commitment and say, in a sentence, why you go. If the sentence comes easily, go with a clear conscience. If it doesn't, that's the one worth bringing up with whoever owns it. The point is less to judge the meeting than to check whether you know why you're in the room; a meeting you can explain feels different from one you simply turn up to.

## The phone at the table

At home the bell is usually a phone. It buzzes during dinner, and a hand is halfway to it before anyone has decided anything. Most of the time the message can wait. Occasionally it can't. Either way, it's worth noticing that the hand moved first and the reason came afterward, if it came at all.

## In closing

Yunmen heard the bell as he addressed his monks and asked why everyone answers it. The bell, presumably, went on ringing in his monastery, and the robes went on. What he left behind was a question to carry into the hall: in a world this wide, why this robe, and why now?
