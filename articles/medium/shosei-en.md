# Partway Through His Talk, a Zen Teacher Heard the Monastery Bell. He Asked His Monks Why They Answer It. The Zen Koan "Shosei Shichijo" (鐘声七条).

Yunmen¹ was speaking to the assembly when the bell rang. He said: "The world is this wide. Why do you put on your seven-panel robe² at the sound of a bell?"

The record stops there. It gives no answer from the monks, and none from Yunmen.

## A robe for the hall

In a Chan monastery, the bell marked the day: when to rise, when to eat, when to gather in the hall. The seven-panel robe was the one monks put on for assemblies and ceremonies. When the bell for an assembly rang, putting it on was simply what you did next. You didn't stop to decide.

That's what makes the question awkward. Yunmen didn't tell his monks to ignore the bell. He asked whether any of them knew why they answered it. The world is wide enough to go anywhere and do anything; one sound, and everyone reaches for the same robe.

## First written down in 1076

The line doesn't appear in the two oldest Chan histories, the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall of 952 and the Jingde record of 1004, even though both have chapters on Yunmen. The earliest version that survives is in his recorded sayings³, printed in 1076, more than a century after his death. There it is one line in a long sequence of talks: "Hearing the bell, he said: the world is this wide; why put on the seven-panel robe at the bell?"

In 1228 Wumen⁴ made it case 16 of the Gateless Barrier and sharpened the wording slightly: why put on the robe *into* the sound of the bell. His comment begins with a warning against chasing sounds and sights, and then turns the warning around. Even waking up at the sound of something, he says, is commonplace; a real monk rides on sounds and sights instead of being carried along by them. Then he asks: does the sound come to the ear, or does the ear go out to the sound? He closes the comment with a couplet: listen with the ear and it's hard to get; hear the sound with the eye, and you're close. His verse plays the same trick twice: if you understand, everything is one family; if you don't, there are a thousand differences. If you don't understand, everything is one family; if you do, there are a thousand differences.

## A rule written in Italy

In the sixth century, Benedict of Nursia⁵ wrote a rule for monks in Italy. Chapter 43 tells them that at the hour of prayer, as soon as the signal is heard, they're to put down whatever is in their hands and hurry to the oratory, with seriousness rather than giddiness. It gives the reason in the next breath: nothing is to be put before the work of God. The same chapter even specifies where latecomers have to stand.

Both kinds of monastery ran their days by signals, and both expected monks to answer them promptly. The difference is that Benedict's rule hands the monk the reason, while Yunmen asked his monks to supply one themselves.

## The meeting on the calendar

Most working weeks are shaped by signals: the reminder five minutes before the call, the Monday meeting that's been on the calendar for three years, the phone that buzzes as you sit down to think. You answer most of them, and you should. A team where everyone ignores the signals doesn't function.

But some of those meetings have outlived their purpose, and some of the signals belong to someone else's priorities. A useful exercise is to pick one recurring commitment and say, in a sentence, why you go. If the sentence comes easily, go with a clear conscience. If it doesn't, that's the one worth bringing up with whoever owns it. The point is less to judge the meeting than to check whether you know why you're in the room; a meeting you can explain feels different from one you simply turn up to.

## The phone at the table

At home the bell is usually a phone. It buzzes during dinner, and a hand is halfway to it before anyone has decided anything. Most of the time the message can wait. Occasionally it can't. Either way, it's worth noticing that the hand moved first and the reason came afterward, if it came at all.

## In closing

Yunmen heard the bell in the middle of his talk and asked why everyone answers it. He didn't say they should stop, and the record doesn't say anyone stopped. The bell went on ringing in his monastery, and presumably the robes went on. What he left behind was a question to carry into the hall: in a world this wide, why this robe, and why now?
