# There Is No Right Answer. That's the Point. Hakuin's Koan of the Sound of One Hand.

"Two hands clap and there is a sound. What is the sound of one hand?" It's probably the single most famous line in all of Zen, quoted so often it's nearly lost its edge — treated as a bit of pleasant nonsense, a riddle for a fortune cookie. It was never meant as a riddle. It was built as a wall, on purpose, and the wall was the entire point.

## An 18th-century teacher builds a better trap

The koan comes from Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769), the Japanese master usually credited with rescuing and rebuilding Rinzai Zen after a long institutional decline. Hakuin trained students for years on an older koan — "Does a dog have Buddha-nature? Mu" — but found that many of them could talk their way around it without ever really getting stuck. He wanted a question that produced genuine doubt faster, one with no clever verbal exit. The sound of one hand was his answer, and unlike much of what circulates in Zen literature, this one comes with a documented author and a stated purpose, not a legend reconstructed centuries later.

## Why every clever answer is wrong

Ask around and you'll hear the workarounds: hold up one hand, wave it, snap with two fingers, answer with silence itself. Hakuin's teaching heirs rejected all of them, and the rejection is the actual lesson. Every clever answer is a way of staying comfortable — solving the koan the way you'd solve a puzzle, with technique, while never touching the thing the koan was built to expose: that some questions don't have an answer waiting to be extracted through cleverness. They have to be lived with until cleverness itself runs out.

That exhaustion is the design, not a bug in it. The question keeps every analytical door locked long enough that eventually you stop reaching for the doorknob.

## Where this bites at work

Most professional training rewards exactly the move this koan forbids: find the clever answer, present it with confidence, move on. That instinct is often right. Occasionally it runs straight into a wall it can't talk its way past.

- **The reorg with no good option.** Every path costs someone something real, and the meeting keeps generating clever frameworks to avoid saying so plainly. At some point the frameworks are the avoidance.
- **The mentee asking a question you can't actually answer.** "Should I take this job" or "is this relationship worth saving" isn't solved by a smarter answer. It's solved by sitting with them in the not-knowing long enough that they find their own footing.
- **Grief, a diagnosis, a failure with no clean lesson attached.** The instinct to extract a takeaway, a silver lining, a next step, can itself be a way of refusing to simply be where you are.

None of this argues against expertise. It argues for noticing the specific moment expertise runs out, and resisting the urge to fake it clever anyway.

## The version with no one else in the room

There's a private edition of the same koan. Sit alone with a question that has no answer — not "what should I do" but something closer to "who is asking" — and watch how fast the mind starts manufacturing candidates just to make the discomfort stop. That manufacturing is worth catching in the act. The koan isn't cruel for withholding an answer. It's honest about the fact that some silences aren't waiting to be filled.

## In closing

Hakuin didn't build this koan to torment his students. He built it because the easier question let them fake their way to a stopping point that wasn't real understanding, and he wanted something sturdy enough to survive every trick they'd bring to it. The next time you hit a question with no clever exit — at work, at a bedside, in your own head at 2 a.m. — the instruction is the same one Hakuin gave four centuries ago. Don't answer it. Stay with it until the wanting-to-answer wears out on its own.
