# "In a hundred years I'll be reborn as a water buffalo at the foot of this mountain, five characters on my left side: this monk of Guishan. What, then, should you call me?" No one answered. The Zen Case "Guishan's Water Buffalo" (潙山水牯牛).

A teacher tells his assembled students: in a hundred years, after I've left this world, I'll be reborn as a water buffalo at the foot of the mountain, at the house of one of our lay supporters. On my left side will be written five characters: this monk of Guishan. If you call me "the monk of Guishan" at that point, I'm also a water buffalo. If you call me "water buffalo," I'm also the monk of Guishan. So — what would be the right thing to call me? No one answered.

## The kicked-over pitcher that decided who'd run the mountain

Before any of this, Guishan Lingyou¹ was a student under Baizhang Huaihai², not yet a teacher of anyone.

One day Baizhang set a water pitcher in front of the assembly and posed a challenge: you may not call this a pitcher — so what do you call it?

Hualin³, the senior disciple, stepped forward first. "You can't call it a block of wood either," he offered.

Guishan said nothing. He walked over, kicked the pitcher onto its side, and left the room.

Baizhang laughed. "Hualin lost to a pitcher," he said. "Guishan is the one who'll run the mountain."

That's how Guishan Lingyou came to found a monastery on the mountain that would carry his name for the rest of Chan history.

## Two stories asking the same question, decades apart

The pitcher story is recorded in the Song Biographies of Eminent Monks⁴ and in the Jingde Chuandenglu⁵, compiled in 1004. The water buffalo story survives separately, in Guishan's own recorded sayings. Exactly when that collection was compiled isn't something that can be confirmed — but Guishan's life and teaching are independently documented in the 1004 record, so there's no doubt he was a real historical figure whose words were being preserved.

What connects the two stories is that both are about names failing to hold still. In the first, Guishan answered a ban on naming with an action instead of a word. In the second, decades later as an old teacher, he handed his own students a case where two contradictory names could both be true of the same thing at once — and, as far as the record shows, no one in the room had an answer ready.

## An animal working a field with a name written on its side

Picture the buffalo itself: pulling a plow, hauling loads, sweating through a field somewhere at the base of the mountain, five characters marked on its flank that nobody working alongside it has any reason to notice.

A farmer who saw only the animal would use it exactly like any other buffalo — the whip, the yoke, a full day's labor.

Someone who happened to read those five characters would suddenly be unsure whether they were looking at livestock or at a teacher who deserved respect.

That's the discomfort Guishan built into the question. Not "which name is correct," but the fact that a single, actual creature can honestly answer to two names that appear to rule each other out.

## The meeting that stalls on what to call the problem

- **The same issue, named differently by different departments.** Sales calls it a pricing problem. Engineering calls it a quality problem. Every minute spent arguing over which label is correct is a minute not spent on the thing both labels are pointing at.
- **"Is this a management issue or an execution issue?"** — a line drawn as though the two were mutually exclusive. Most of the time the same action would answer to either name. Insisting on choosing one can be its own kind of wasted effort.
- **Treatment changing the instant a title does.** Same person, same skills, same track record — and the room shifts the moment someone starts calling them "manager." The label moved. Nothing underneath it did.

Guishan wasn't asking his students to go find the correct name. He was asking them to notice what stays put while the name keeps changing around it.

## Answering to a different name at home than at work

The same question turns up in private. Reliable at the office, useless around the house — plenty of people are quietly answering to two contradictory names depending on which room they're standing in.

Trying to settle which one is the real self usually just produces more of the same confusion.

What Guishan's question suggests is that the whole search for a single correct label might never have been necessary. Names shift with context. What's actually doing the working, the feeling, the thinking underneath those names was there before either label got attached, and stays exactly where it was after.

## In closing

No one in the room had an answer, and that isn't the failure it might look like — there was never a correct answer waiting to be produced. What Guishan actually left his students with wasn't a solution. It was the image of an animal: pulling a plow, five unreadable characters worked into its hide, going on with the field regardless of what anyone decided to call it. That animal, going on working, may be the quietest answer this question ever gets.
