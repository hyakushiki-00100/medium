# A Teacher Returned From the Hills, and a Monk Asked How Far He'd Got. His Answer Named No Place at All. The Zen Koan "Chosa Yuzan" (長沙遊山).

One day Changsha¹ went walking in the hills. When he got back to the gate, the head monk asked him where he'd been. "Walking in the hills," Changsha said. "And where did you get to?" Changsha said: "I went out following the fragrant grass, and came back chasing the falling flowers." "Very much the feeling of spring," said the head monk. "Better than autumn dew dripping on the lotus leaves," Changsha said.

## The second question

The first answer was already complete. Changsha had been walking in the hills, and he said so. The head monk asked again anyway, and the second question is the interesting one: not what he'd done but where he'd got to, as if a walk only counts once it has a destination you can name.

Changsha's reply doesn't name one. It describes a route by what he followed on the way out and what he followed on the way back. There's no summit in it, no landmark, nothing to put on a map. It sounds like a man who let the hillside decide where he went.

Yuanwu, the twelfth-century teacher whose commentary fills out the Blue Cliff Record, put his finger on exactly this. He was lecturing at Jiashan, and if one of the monks of his own day had been asked, he wrote, the answer would have been "I went as far as the Jiashan pavilion": a named spot that everyone listening knew. Changsha had no such answer to give, and didn't seem to miss it.

The exchange after that is harder to pin down. The head monk's "very much the feeling of spring" can be heard as appreciation or as a mild jab, and Changsha's line about autumn dew has been read in more than one way. Xuedou, who chose the case, appended a dry "Thanks for your answer," which Yuanwu reads as the last word Xuedou supplies on the head monk's behalf.

## "Cen the Tiger"

Changsha Jingcen², who trained under Nanquan alongside Zhaozhou, was not a soft figure. One of the best-known stories about him has him knocking the younger monk Yangshan flat with a kick. The older records set it while the two were sitting in the sun in a courtyard; later ones, including the Blue Cliff Record, move it to a night of moon-viewing. Yangshan, who called him "uncle" in the dharma, got up and said he was like a tiger, and the nickname "Cen the Tiger" stuck.

The walk in the hills is best known as case 36 of the Blue Cliff Record³, completed around 1125 from a selection of cases made by Xuedou⁴ in the previous century. It also appears in the Essentials of the Collected Lamp⁵ of 1183. It isn't in the long chapter on him, under the name "Master Cen," in the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁷ of 952, or in his chapter in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁶ of 1004.

## Thoreau's saunterer

In his essay "Walking," published in 1862, Henry David Thoreau passes on two origins for the word "saunter," both taken from older dictionaries, Samuel Johnson's among them. One is the medieval vagrants who begged their way across the countryside claiming to be bound "à la Sainte Terre," for the Holy Land, until children called out, "There goes a Sainte-Terrer." The other is "sans terre," without land or home. Modern dictionaries reject both. What's telling is which one Thoreau chose. He granted that the second gave a fine sense of the walker who is equally at home everywhere, then said he preferred the first: every walk, he wrote, is a sort of crusade. Even the essay most often quoted in praise of walking wanted its walks to be heading for a Holy Land. Changsha's answer at the gate doesn't have one.

## Reporting back on thinking time

Plenty of workplaces have made room for unstructured thinking time on paper, and then asked people to account for it in the next update: what you explored, what came out of it, what's next. The question is reasonable. It also quietly turns the walk into a trip with a destination, and the time starts getting spent on whatever will make a good answer.

Changsha's reply suggests a different kind of report, one that's honest about what actually happened: I followed what caught my attention, and came back by a different route.

## A walk with no number attached

A weekend walk can arrive pre-loaded with a step target, a route on the phone, a podcast to finish, and a photo for later. That's all fine, and it's still worth taking the occasional one where the only plan is to go out, follow whatever's interesting, and come back when you're ready, without having anything to say afterward except what you saw.

## In closing

Changsha was asked where he'd got to and answered with grass and flowers instead of a place. The head monk wanted a destination. What he got was an accurate account of a day's walk.
