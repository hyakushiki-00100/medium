# A Monk Described His Travels in Precise Detail. The Teacher Said He'd Earned Sixty Blows With a Stick. The Zen Koan "Santon" (洞山三頓).

When Dongshan Shouchu¹ arrived at Yunmen's² monastery, Yunmen asked him where he'd come from. "Chadu," Dongshan said. Yunmen asked where he'd spent the summer retreat. "Baoci, in Hunan." Yunmen asked when he'd left there. "The twenty-fifth of August." Yunmen said: "I'll let you off sixty blows of the stick." Dongshan had no idea what he'd done wrong.

## Every answer was exactly right

Nothing in Dongshan's account was false or evasive. The place was real, the temple was real, the date was specific enough to check. He hadn't dodged a single question. And Yunmen still told him he'd earned a beating, then withheld it, and sent him off to wonder why. Overnight, wondering apparently wasn't enough — the next day Dongshan came back and asked directly where his answers had gone wrong. Yunmen didn't explain. He said: "You rice bag. Is that how you've been wandering around — now west of the river, now south of the lake?" At that, Dongshan had a great awakening. He'd given a complete, verifiable itinerary of everywhere he'd been, and somehow managed not to be in any of it. An answer can be accurate down to the date and still be the record of someone who was never actually there.

## The disciple who would later answer with a scale

Dongshan Shouchu³ lived from 910 to 990, a disciple of Yunmen Wenyan (864–949) who went on to succeed him as a teacher in his own right. This exchange survives in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁴, compiled in 1004 — the earliest source generally credited with it — and appears in its more widely quoted form roughly two centuries later as Case 15 of the Gateless Gate⁵, compiled in 1228, where it's known as "Santon," Dongshan's three periods of the stick. He isn't to be confused with the earlier Dongshan Liangjie, founder of the Caodong school, discussed elsewhere in this series. Years after this exchange, once he was teaching on his own, a monk asked this same Dongshan Shouchu what Buddha is. He answered: three pounds of flax — a different exchange, discussed elsewhere in this series, from a man who by then had stopped accounting for himself and started just answering from wherever he actually was.

## A general who could describe the battle and still couldn't say what courage was

In one of Plato's dialogues, the Athenian general Laches is asked to define courage and offers, without hesitation, a soldier who holds his position and doesn't run from the enemy — a specific, battle-tested answer from a man who'd actually done the holding. Socrates keeps asking simple follow-up questions, and the definition doesn't survive them; a soldier retreating strategically can still be courageous, and Laches, despite decades commanding real men in real battles, can't say what the word he's used his whole career actually means. Concreteness and specificity read as evidence of understanding. They aren't the same thing.

## The report that accounts for everything and lands nowhere

Someone asks how the project is going, and what comes back is a full itinerary: which tickets closed, which meetings happened, which stakeholders were looped in, dated and timestamped. All of it checks out. None of it answers whether the project is actually going anywhere, because the question wasn't really "what did you do" — it was "where does this stand" — and a complete travel log of activity can substitute for an answer to that question for a remarkably long time before anyone notices the substitution.

## Every minute accounted for, none of it inhabited

The same gap shows up at the end of an ordinary day. Asked how it went, there's a full itinerary ready — the meetings, the errands, the messages answered, each one real and each one able to be checked against a calendar. What's harder to produce is any sense of having actually been present for the day being described, rather than having merely processed it in order and moved to the next entry. The itinerary was accurate. The question was never really about the itinerary.

## In closing

Dongshan gave Yunmen three correct answers in a row and still walked away having earned a beating he didn't understand. What changed by the next morning wasn't the itinerary — every date and place stayed exactly as he'd first given them. What changed was that he stopped defending the accuracy of the record and let himself hear what Yunmen was actually asking.
