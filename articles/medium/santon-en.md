# A Monk Described His Travels in Precise Detail. The Teacher Said He'd Earned Sixty Blows With a Stick. The Zen Koan "Tozan Santon" (洞山三頓).

When Dongshan Shouchu¹ arrived at Yunmen's² monastery, Yunmen asked him where he'd come from. "Chadu," Dongshan said. Yunmen asked where he'd spent the summer retreat. "Baoci, in Hunan." Yunmen asked when he'd left there. "The twenty-fifth of the eighth month." Yunmen said: "You have three rounds of the stick coming. I'll let you off." Dongshan had no idea what he'd done wrong.

## Every answer was exactly right

Nothing in Dongshan's account was false or evasive. He named a specific place, a specific temple, a date exact enough to check against a calendar. And Yunmen still told him he owed a beating, then let him off it, and sent him away to wonder why. Wondering wasn't enough — the next day Dongshan came back and asked directly where his answers had gone wrong. Yunmen didn't explain. He said: "You rice bag. Is that how you've been wandering around — now west of the river, now south of the lake?" At that, Dongshan had a great awakening. He'd given a complete, verifiable itinerary of everywhere he'd been, and somehow managed not to be in any of it. An answer can be accurate down to the date and still be the record of someone who was never actually there.

## The disciple who would later answer with a scale

Dongshan Shouchu³ lived from 910 to 990, a disciple of Yunmen Wenyan (864–949) who later taught on his own at Mount Dong in Xiangzhou. The earliest record of this exchange that can be confirmed is the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁴, compiled in 1004 — only fourteen years after Dongshan's death, unusually close for this series — though an earlier account may exist in a Yunmen-specific record that wasn't possible to check directly. A longer version, with an extra exchange tacked on after the awakening, appears in Yuanwu Keqin's commentary on Case 12 of the Blue Cliff Record⁵ (1125), the same case that preserves Dongshan's later "three pounds of flax" exchange. The terser, more widely read form dates to roughly two and a half centuries after his death, as Case 15 of the Gateless Gate⁶ (1228), known as "Tozan Santon" — three rounds of the stick, conventionally sixty blows in English, twenty to a round, though that arithmetic isn't in the base text. He isn't to be confused with the earlier Dongshan Liangjie, founder of the Caodong school, discussed elsewhere in this series. Later, teaching on his own, this same Dongshan Shouchu was asked what Buddha is. He answered: three pounds of flax — a different exchange, discussed elsewhere in this series, from a man who by then had stopped accounting for himself and started just answering from wherever he actually was.

## A general who could describe the battle and still couldn't define it

In one of Plato's dialogues, the Athenian general Laches is asked to define courage and offers, without hesitation, a soldier who holds his position and doesn't run from the enemy — a battle-tested answer from a man who'd actually done it. Socrates keeps asking simple follow-up questions, and the definition doesn't survive them: a soldier retreating strategically can still be courageous. Laches tries again, and that one falls too. By the end, none of them — not Laches, not his fellow general Nicias, not Socrates himself — can settle on what the word actually means, despite two men in the room who'd spent their careers being it. Concreteness reads as evidence of understanding. It isn't the same thing.

## An itinerary offered in place of a yes or no

Asked whether a deadline will hold, the answer that comes back is often a complete week: this task finished Monday, that review happened Wednesday, the ticket moved to done on Friday morning. Every item checks out, and the sheer completeness of the list makes it awkward to keep pressing — objecting can feel like disputing the facts themselves. But the question was never about the week. It was whether the date holds, and an honest itinerary of effort can stand in for that answer for a remarkably long time before anyone notices it was never actually given.

## Every minute accounted for, none of it inhabited

Evenings do this too. Asked how the day went, there's a full itinerary on hand — meetings, errands, messages answered, each one checkable against a calendar. Harder to produce is any sense of having actually been present for the day, rather than having processed it in order and moved on. The itinerary was accurate. The question was never really about the itinerary.

## In closing

Dongshan gave three correct answers in a row and still walked away owing a debt of blows he didn't understand. What had changed by the next morning wasn't the itinerary — every date and place stayed exactly as first given. What changed was where his attention was pointed: no longer on whether the record could withstand scrutiny, but on what Yunmen was actually asking him.
