# A Monk Asked Him Not to Point at Anything Outside the Question. The Teacher Named the Same Tree Again. The Zen Koan "Teizen Hakujushi" (庭前柏樹子).

A monk asked Zhaozhou¹ what Bodhidharma² actually meant by making the whole journey from India — the standard way, in his day, of asking a much bigger question: what is the actual point of any of this? Zhaozhou told him: the cypress tree in the garden. The monk asked him, please, not to answer using something borrowed from outside the question. Zhaozhou said he wasn't borrowing anything. Then he said it again. The cypress tree in the garden.

## A question every student already knew how to ask

By Zhaozhou's time, "why did Bodhidharma come from the West" wasn't really a question about a historical journey. It was a formula — the standard opening move a student used to ask a teacher, in effect, what actually matters here, and dozens of different masters are recorded giving dozens of different, equally strange answers to the identical line. Zhaozhou's students would have known the question was formulaic the moment they heard it. What none of them could have predicted was a teacher who, told his answer sounded like a metaphor, would simply deny that and repeat it exactly.

## Two centuries collapse into fifty-five years

The version most people encounter today comes from the Gateless Gate³, compiled more than three centuries after Zhaozhou died. It preserves only the first exchange. The fuller scene — the monk's objection, and Zhaozhou's flat refusal to have been speaking figuratively — survives in the Anthology of the Patriarchs' Hall⁴, compiled a mere fifty-five years after his death, with the tree itself written one character differently: "the cypress in front of the hall," rather than "in the garden." Whether that's a copying variant or two slightly different tellings of the same afternoon isn't something either record settles from the outside. What both agree on is that nothing about the second answer had softened into an image.

## Trees a visitor can still stand under

Bailin Chan Temple⁵, in the town where Zhaozhou spent his last decades, still has roughly twenty cypresses on its grounds dated to the Tang dynasty — the same era as the man himself. Local tradition connects them to this exact exchange, though nobody can verify that any particular trunk standing today is the one he pointed at. It hardly matters. A visitor can walk under trees that old, in that exact courtyard, and get exactly as far as the monk did: closer to the tree, no closer to an explanation.

## Refusing to let the object become a metaphor

Someone in a meeting asks what the company actually stands for, underneath the mission statement. A colleague answers by pointing at the support ticket still open on their screen — this, right here, is what we're actually doing. The person who asked pushes back: that's just one ticket, they wanted the real answer, the underlying thing the ticket is an example of. The honest response isn't a better example. It's the same ticket, pointed at again, because it was never standing in for anything. Turning it into a symbol would have been the retreat, not the answer.

## Asking the question again, louder

The same refusal shows up when people go looking for meaning and get handed something too plain to accept: this ordinary Tuesday, this specific conversation, whatever's actually in front of you right now. It doesn't sound like an answer, so the question gets repeated, this time with more feeling behind it — surely there's something underneath the plain version. There usually isn't a second, deeper layer waiting to be unlocked by asking with more urgency. There's the same plain thing, unchanged by how many times it gets asked for again.

## In closing

Zhaozhou never told the monk what the tree meant, because as far as he was concerned there was nothing behind it to explain. He'd already given the whole answer the first time. The second time was just proof he meant it.
