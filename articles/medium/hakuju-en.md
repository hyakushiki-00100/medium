# A Monk Asked Him Not to Answer Using Anything External. The Teacher Named the Same Tree Again. The Zen Koan "Teizen Hakujushi" (庭前柏樹子).

A monk asked Zhaozhou¹ what Bodhidharma² actually meant by making the whole journey from India — the standard way, in his day, of asking a much bigger question: what is the actual point of any of this? Zhaozhou told him: the cypress tree in the garden. The monk asked him, please, not to answer using anything external. Zhaozhou said he wasn't pointing at anything external. Then he said it again. The cypress tree in the garden.

## The question was never about a journey

By Zhaozhou's time, "why did Bodhidharma come from the West" wasn't really a question about a historical journey. It was a formula — the standard opening move a student used to ask a teacher, in effect, what actually matters here, and dozens of different masters are recorded giving dozens of different, equally strange answers to the identical line. Zhaozhou's students would have known the question was formulaic the moment they heard it. What none of them could have predicted was a teacher who, told his answer sounded like a metaphor, would simply deny that and repeat it exactly.

## Older than the version most people read

The version most people encounter today comes from the Gateless Gate³, compiled more than three centuries after Zhaozhou died, and it preserves only the first exchange. A fuller scene — the monk's objection, and Zhaozhou's flat refusal to have been speaking figuratively — is already in place in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁴, compiled in 1004, and appears to reach back further still to the Anthology of the Patriarchs' Hall⁵, traditionally dated to 952, fifty-five years after Zhaozhou's death. The only complete copy of that anthology to survive, though, is a woodblock printing made in Korea in 1245, and how much of its later chapters matches the original single-scroll version from 952 isn't something scholars have settled. The tree itself gets one word swapped between versions: "the cypress in front of the pavilion," rather than "in the garden." Whether that's a copying variant or two slightly different tellings of the same afternoon isn't something any of these records settles from the outside. What none of them do, across three centuries of copying, is soften the second answer into something more explanatory than the first.

## Trees a visitor can still stand under

Bailin Chan Temple⁶, in the town where Zhaozhou spent his last four decades, still has roughly twenty cypresses on its grounds that the temple dates to the Tang dynasty — the same era as the man himself, though the temple buildings around them have been rebuilt many times since. Local tradition connects the trees to this exact exchange, though nobody can verify that any particular trunk standing today is the one he pointed at. It hardly changes anything. A visitor can walk under trees that old, on the same grounds, and get exactly as far as the monk did: nearer the tree, no nearer an explanation.

## Refusing to let the number become a metaphor

Someone questions a metric in a review — surely this figure is just a rough stand-in for what actually matters, so what's the deeper thing underneath it? Sometimes it is a rough stand-in, and a better measure exists. Often the honest answer is plainer: the number was never standing in for anything grander. It isn't a proxy for customer trust. It's the count of tickets closed within a day, and reaching for some loftier version of it wouldn't make it truer. Treating a plain measure as secretly symbolic of something bigger is usually where the real distortion creeps in, not where it gets corrected.

## Asking the question again, louder

The same refusal shows up when someone goes looking for meaning and gets handed something too plain to accept — an ordinary conversation, a chore already half finished, whatever is actually happening right now. It doesn't sound like an answer, so the question comes back, pressed harder this time: surely there's a second layer underneath the plain one. Usually there isn't. There's the same plain thing, restated exactly, no matter how many times it gets demanded again.

## In closing

The tree was never standing in for anything else, and Zhaozhou wasn't willing to pretend it was just because the first answer had sounded like a metaphor. He'd said everything he had to say the first time. What changed the second time wasn't the answer. It was only whether the monk could finally hear it.
