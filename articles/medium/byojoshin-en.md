# Stop Chasing the Peak State. The Zen Answer "Ordinary Mind Is the Way" (平常心是道).

A student asks his teacher what the Way actually is — the whole point of the practice, the thing everyone is supposedly training toward. The teacher's answer is five words. *Byōjōshin kore dō.* Ordinary mind is the Way. Not an elevated mind, not a mind in the zone, not a peak state you have to earn. The plain, unremarkable mind you're using to read this sentence.

## A famous teacher, an even more famous student

The exchange is between Nanquan Puyuan (748–835) and his student Zhaozhou Congshen (778–897), who would go on to become one of the most quoted voices in the entire Zen canon and reportedly lived to the improbable age of 119. The phrase itself, though, predates Nanquan: it's usually traced to his own teacher, Mazu Daoyi, and Nanquan is best understood as carrying it forward rather than coining it outright. Zhaozhou asks, "What is the Way?" Nanquan answers, "Ordinary mind is the Way." Zhaozhou, characteristically, pushes back: "Then should I try to direct myself toward it?" Nanquan's reply is the sharper half of the exchange: "The moment you aim at it, you've already turned away."

The exchange is preserved as Case 19 of the *Gateless Gate*, compiled in 1228 — nearly four centuries after Nanquan's death, so what we have is the teaching as the tradition carried it forward, rather than minutes taken that afternoon. Nanquan spent roughly three decades living self-sufficiently on Nanquan Mountain before he ever taught publicly, farming and studying in near isolation — the kind of ordinary, repetitive labor his own answer would later point back to. The exact wording of the exchange almost certainly sharpened with retelling before it reached the page.

## Aiming is the departure

That second line is the whole teaching, and it's easy to read past it. The Way isn't reached by striving toward it, because striving — leaning forward, monitoring whether you've arrived yet — is itself the act of leaving ordinary mind behind. You cannot force your way into being unforced. The moment you're managing your state *while doing the thing*, you've stopped simply doing it. A brief check-in that ends and lets you return to the task is different from running a live commentary on the task the whole way through — one interrupts and releases, the other never lets go.

This isn't a claim that calm is the goal, or that effort is bad. Ordinary mind isn't a mood at all. It's the mind doing whatever's in front of it — sweeping a floor, answering an email — without a second layer of commentary running on top, quietly grading the performance. The "peak state" everyone chases is usually just ordinary mind plus a great deal of anxious narration about whether it's the right state. Drop the narration, and what's left was already sufficient.

## Where the reaching shows up at work

- **Waiting to feel ready.** Motivation gets treated as a prerequisite for starting. The steadier move is the reverse — begin in an entirely ordinary state and let engagement arrive through the work itself, not before it.
- **The fragile setup.** "I do my best work in total silence, after my routine, on no sleep debt." Every condition you require is a condition that can be taken away. Ordinary mind is what still functions on the noisy, under-slept, imperfect Tuesday.
- **Forcing calm mid-crisis.** Telling yourself to stay calm while something is on fire usually adds a second problem to the first — now you're managing the crisis and your failure to feel calm about it. The instruction that actually helps is smaller: do the next ordinary thing.

## The unremarkable Tuesday

There's an inward version of this, and it's not about where meaning is located — it's about what you're doing to your own mind while you look for it. We tend to file our "real self" under the exceptional moments — the great talk, the flow session, the day everything clicked — and quietly grade every ordinary day against them. That grading is the same aiming Nanquan warned about, just aimed inward and stretched across a lifetime instead of a single afternoon. Mastery, read this way, isn't the ability to summon a peak state on command. It's an ordinary mind left alone long enough to just do the forgettable Tuesday in front of it.

## In closing

The Way was never a door you pass through in some elevated condition. It's the ground you're already standing on, the plain mind you keep trying to upgrade into something more impressive. The warning Nanquan carried forward from his own teacher holds up remarkably well: the harder you reach for a special state, the further you drift from the one that was here the entire time. Put the reaching down. Do the ordinary thing, in your ordinary mind. That, he says, is already the Way.
