# Stop Chasing the Peak State. The Zen Answer "Ordinary Mind Is the Way" (平常心是道).

A student asks his teacher what the Way actually is — the whole point of the practice, the thing everyone is supposedly training toward. The teacher's answer is four words. *Byōjōshin kore michi.* Ordinary mind is the Way. Not an elevated mind, not a mind in the zone, not a peak state you have to earn. The plain, unremarkable mind you're using to read this sentence.

## A famous teacher, an even more famous student

The exchange is between Nanquan Puyuan (748–835) and his student Zhaozhou Congshen (778–897) — a pairing that would go on to produce some of the most quoted lines in the entire Zen canon, Zhaozhou in particular living to the improbable age of 119 and becoming one of the tradition's most cited voices. Zhaozhou asks, "What is the Way?" Nanquan answers, "Ordinary mind is the Way." Zhaozhou, characteristically, pushes back: "Then should I try to direct myself toward it?" Nanquan's reply is the sharper half of the exchange: "The moment you aim at it, you've already turned away."

The exchange is preserved as Case 19 of the *Gateless Gate*, compiled in 1228 — nearly four centuries after Nanquan's death, so what we have is the teaching as the tradition carried it forward, not a transcript of that afternoon. Nanquan himself is well documented as a major Tang-dynasty teacher, and this exchange with Zhaozhou is among the most frequently cited in the literature, but the exact wording almost certainly sharpened with retelling before it reached the page.

## Aiming is the departure

That second line is the whole teaching, and it's easy to read past it. The Way isn't reached by striving toward it, because striving — leaning forward, monitoring whether you've arrived yet — is itself the act of leaving ordinary mind behind. You cannot force your way into being unforced. The moment you're managing your state, you've stopped simply being in it.

This isn't a claim that calm is the goal, or that effort is bad. Ordinary mind isn't a mood at all. It's the mind doing whatever's in front of it — sweeping a floor, answering an email — without a second layer of commentary running on top, quietly grading the performance. The "peak state" everyone chases is usually just ordinary mind plus a great deal of anxious narration about whether it's the right state. Drop the narration, and what's left was already sufficient.

## Where this lands at work

- **Waiting to feel ready.** Motivation gets treated as a prerequisite for starting. The steadier move is the reverse — begin in an entirely ordinary state and let engagement arrive through the work itself, not before it.
- **The fragile setup.** "I do my best work in total silence, after my routine, on no sleep debt." Every condition you require is a condition that can be taken away. Ordinary mind is what still functions on the noisy, under-slept, imperfect Tuesday.
- **Forcing calm mid-crisis.** Telling yourself to stay calm while something is on fire usually adds a second problem to the first — now you're managing the crisis and your failure to feel calm about it. The instruction that actually helps is smaller: do the next ordinary thing.

## The unremarkable Tuesday

There's an inward version of this. We tend to file our "real self" under the exceptional moments — the great talk, the flow session, the day everything clicked — and treat the ordinary days as filler to get through on the way to the next good one. But a life is mostly ordinary days. If the Way only shows up under ideal conditions, it's barely showing up at all. Mastery, read this way, isn't the ability to summon a peak state on command. It's an ordinary mind that's just as available on a forgettable Tuesday as on the day that matters.

## In closing

The Way was never a door you pass through in some elevated condition. It's the ground you're already standing on, the plain mind you keep trying to upgrade into something more impressive. Nanquan's warning holds up remarkably well: the harder you reach for a special state, the further you drift from the one that was here the entire time. Put the reaching down. Do the ordinary thing, in your ordinary mind. That, he says, is already the Way.
