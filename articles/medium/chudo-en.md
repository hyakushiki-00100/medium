# The Hardest Possible Self-Punishment Was Already Tried. The Buddhist Word "Chudo" (中道).

For years — by tradition, six of them — Siddhartha Gautama nearly starved himself. He trained alongside five other men who'd made the same bet: that self-denial, carried far enough, would burn away whatever stood between them and awakening. Then, weakened past the point of function, he gave it up and began eating ordinary food again. The five men watching him understood exactly what they thought they were seeing: their teacher had lost his nerve. They left him there and went on without him, still practicing the discipline he'd apparently just failed at.

## Comfort, then its total renunciation

The starvation wasn't Gautama's first attempt at an answer. He'd grown up shielded from hardship, married, and by tradition lacked for nothing material — and none of it had touched the question he was carrying. So he tried the opposite. He left, joined a circle of ascetics, and spent years pushing his body toward the edge of what it could survive. He would later describe, by tradition, his ribs jutting out like the crazed rafters of a barn with its roof fallen in, the skin of his belly sunk against his spine — touch one, and you were touching the other. Comfort first, then comfort's total renunciation — two extremes, tried in sequence, by the same person, years apart. Neither one got him anywhere.

What actually shifted things was smaller than either extreme. He bathed in the Nerañjarā river, and — tradition holds — a woman from a nearby village, said to be a local landowner's daughter, offered him a bowl of milk-rice. He ate it. That same day, meditating beneath a tree at Bodh Gaya, he reached the awakening years of self-denial hadn't produced. He then traveled to a deer park near Varanasi, sought out the five men who'd abandoned him, and gave them his first teaching. One of them, Kondañña¹, is said to have grasped it before the other four — earning, by tradition, a new name that meant roughly "the one who understood." All five became his first students.

## Older than Zen by about a thousand years

What survives as that sermon — later titled the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, "Setting the Wheel of Dharma in Motion" — wasn't transcribed as it happened. Like the rest of the earliest Buddhist canon, it was carried by memory, generation to generation, for a long stretch before anyone wrote it down. The dates around all of this are less settled than confident retellings suggest: the long-accepted birth and death years for Gautama place him in the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, but more recent scholarship has argued for dates roughly a century later. The chronology isn't resolved.

What matters more is what this word isn't. Chudo — the middle way, majjhimā paṭipadā in the sermon's own language — comes from Gautama himself, in the first teaching he's recorded as giving, very roughly a thousand years before anything resembling Chan Buddhism existed in China. It doesn't belong to any Japanese sect, and it isn't a Zen teaching adopted into a wider vocabulary later. It's closer to the reverse: the founding move of Buddhism itself, an idea every later school — Zen included — inherited rather than invented. Later philosophers built on it in directions Gautama never spelled out that day — Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka reads chudo as a middle way between existence and non-existence, a more abstract claim than the practical one taught at the deer park — but the word's root sits here, before any of those schools existed to reinterpret it.

## The wheel that didn't stop

The sermon takes its name from an image: the wheel of the teaching, turned for the first time. Buddhist art rendered it concretely — a many-spoked wheel carved into railings and gateways, standing in for the path itself; the convention of reading each of its eight spokes as one element of that path settled in only later. At Sarnath itself, where tradition places that sermon, a stone stupa² still marks the ground, rebuilt more than once across the centuries since. It isn't a monument to one dramatic moment. It's a monument to a wheel that started turning and didn't stop.

## Failure, in both directions

- **The instinct to punish yourself into results.** More hours, less recovery, treating exhaustion as evidence of seriousness. This is the ascetic extreme, and someone already ran it to its logical conclusion and nearly died finding out it doesn't work.
- **The instinct to declare the whole effort optional.** Comfort chased for its own sake, standards dropped under the banner of self-care until nothing hard gets attempted. This is the other extreme, and it fails just as reliably — quietly, instead of dramatically.
- **Mistaking "middle" for "average."** Chudo isn't splitting the difference or picking a comfortable midpoint once and calling it settled. It's a sustained, deliberate calibration — closer to a discipline than a compromise, and one that has to be re-found continually rather than located and left alone.

## Being read as the one who quit

Ease off after overextending, and the read from outside — sometimes from inside your own head — tends to be identical to what the five men concluded: you couldn't hack it, you're rationalizing a retreat. That judgment lands hardest right after you've made the sanest decision available, which is exactly when it's least deserved and most convincing. The instinct that reads recovery as failure is the same instinct the five men were running on, and it was wrong about the one case history actually preserved for us to check.

## In closing

Gautama went and found the five men who'd walked away, and this time they couldn't keep the distance they'd planned to keep. What had looked like him giving up was the decision that let those years of failure finally pay for something. The wheel he's said to have set moving that day at the deer park wasn't turned by punishing himself further, and it wasn't turned by comfort either. It was turned by a man who'd already tried both, all the way to their limits, and had the evidence to know neither one was it.
