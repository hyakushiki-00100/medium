# Hanging From a Branch by Your Teeth. Someone Below Asks You a Question. The Zen Koan "Xiangyan's Man Up a Tree" (香厳上樹).

Picture a man hanging from a tree branch over a sheer thousand-foot drop, holding on with nothing but his teeth clamped around it. His hands have nothing to grip. His feet have nothing to stand on. Someone below calls up a serious question. Stay silent, and you've failed the person asking. Open your mouth to answer, and you fall.

## A teacher who liked to corner people

Xiangyan Zhixian¹, who trained under Guishan Lingyou and later became known for burning his own library after a question he couldn't answer, posed this scene to his own students as a teaching device. The earliest surviving record of it is the *Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp*² (景徳伝灯録), compiled in 1004 — roughly a century after Xiangyan died, not the three centuries a more familiar later version might suggest.

That later version is Case 5 of the *Gateless Gate*³ (無門関), compiled by Wumen Huikai in 1228. It's the leaner telling — the one most people quote today — and it drops two details the earlier record kept: the exact height of the drop, and what happened right after Xiangyan finished posing the riddle.

## The answer that wasn't an answer

In the earlier record, a monk named Zhao⁴ stood up. He didn't try to solve the puzzle on its own terms. He said: I'm not asking about the moment you're already up the tree. What about before you climbed it? Xiangyan just laughed and said nothing more.

Zhao's move wasn't a clever loophole inside the trap. It stepped outside the trap's own timeline — back to the choice, long before any branch was involved, that put someone in a position where every remaining option looked like death.

## No river of words gets you down from there

Wumen's own remarks on the case don't try to solve the riddle for the reader either. Even eloquence that flows like a river is useless here, he writes. Even mastering every scripture ever written doesn't help. Answer the question properly from that position, he says, and you give life to the dead and kill the living. Fail, and you'd better go ask Maitreya⁵ — the future Buddha who, by tradition, isn't due to appear for billions of years yet. It's a dry way of saying there's no sitting this one out and trying again later.

His verse calls Xiangyan himself truly careless, his venom without limit⁶ — high praise in this genre, where the harsher the teacher's device, the more credit it gets for actually stopping students in their tracks instead of letting them talk their way past the point.

## The part hardly anyone lands on first

Most people spend their first pass at this koan hunting for a clever third option — some answer that's neither speech nor silence. That instinct misses where the real trap sits. It isn't the two exits. It's the teeth. Whatever's being bitten down on that hard is the reason letting go, in either direction, feels like dying. Zhao never touched the branch at all. He asked what put the man up there gripping something in the first place.

## The branch, dressed for the office

The dilemma has an ordinary shape: telling a superior something they don't want to hear, at real risk to your own standing, versus staying quiet and watching a bad decision go through anyway. Framed as speak-and-lose-your-footing or stay-silent-and-fail-them, both options look like a fall. What's actually being gripped in that moment usually isn't the truth of the situation — it's how the messenger will be seen for delivering it.

This isn't a case for recklessness dressed up as candor. It's a question worth asking before you decide the only choices are speaking and losing everything, or staying silent and betraying someone: what's actually clamped in your teeth right now?

## Nobody has to be watching for it to count

The same bind has a quiet, unwitnessed cousin: a conversation you owe someone — an apology, a boundary, a hard truth about where things stand — that you've filed under "speaking ruins it, staying quiet betrays it," when the branch you're actually biting is your own need to be seen as someone who never has to say the hard thing at all. Most people never notice the teeth. They just notice that both directions feel like falling.

## In closing

The man in the tree was never really choosing between speech and silence. He was choosing whether to keep clenching his jaw around the one thing he couldn't imagine losing. Zhao's laugh-inducing question is the closest thing this koan offers to a way down: don't solve the dilemma from inside it. Ask what you were already holding onto before you ever climbed up.
