# A Monk Bowed to Everyone He Met and Called Them Future Buddhas. They Threw Stones — He Kept Shouting It Anyway. The Buddhist Word "Jofukyo" (常不軽).

He didn't spend his time reciting scripture the way other monks did. According to a chapter of the Lotus Sutra¹, a monk who lived long after a buddha named King of Awesome Sound had died would simply walk up to anyone he passed — monk, nun, layman, laywoman, it made no difference — bow, and tell them he deeply respected them, that he wouldn't presume to treat them with contempt, because every one of them was walking the path to buddhahood and would get there eventually. No sermons, no argument for why they should believe him — just the bow, and the same handful of words, delivered to whoever happened to be standing in front of him.

## Twenty-four words, over and over

In the Chinese translation most widely read across East Asia, that line runs to exactly twenty-four characters — tight enough that it's known, especially among Nichiren's later followers, as the twenty-four-character Lotus Sutra. The sutra names his method plainly: 但行礼拝, "only the practice of bowing" — no lecturing, no debating scripture with anyone, just the bow and the line. Some of the people on the receiving end found it insufferable rather than kind. Arrogant monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen took the constant bowing and the claim that they'd become buddhas as mockery, cursed him, and beat him with sticks, wood, roof tiles, and stones. He didn't argue back, and he didn't stop. He retreated out of throwing range and kept calling out the same promise in a loud voice, from as far away as he had to stand. The insult even made it into his own name: the text says the arrogant crowd nicknamed him this — Never Despise — out of what he kept saying to them, needling him with the very words he refused to drop.

## A parable already several removes from history

This text is a particular kind of thing, and it's worth naming that plainly. The sutra doesn't set this story in ordinary historical time — it places it in the age of a buddha so distant the text itself reaches for units too large to be literal, the way "longer ago than anyone could count" functions as a phrase rather than a date. That's a deliberate signal that this is parable, not reportage, and it sits on top of a second layer: the Lotus Sutra itself was composed in stages over several centuries, long after the historical Buddha — whose own dates aren't settled either. Nothing here claims to be a transcript. And yet the sutra doesn't leave the story anonymous. It states outright, later on, that the bowing monk was Shakyamuni himself, in one of his own earlier lives.

## Nichiren read his life into this chapter

This isn't a Zen teaching, and it doesn't come out of a Zen lineage — it belongs to the Lotus Sutra, and in particular to how one later teacher read it. Nichiren² founded what became Nichiren Buddhism and, in the mid-thirteenth century, proclaimed the chant at the center of his teaching. He read his own life directly into this chapter, treating it as one of the clearest instructions the Lotus Sutra offers on what it costs to keep teaching people who'd rather you stopped. His own life was repeatedly disrupted by opposition and exile, and by his own account he read that pattern against this exact story — a man who kept extending the same respect to people actively trying to drive him off.

## The verdict issued after one bad meeting

A direct report bombs a presentation once, and the assignments that follow get quietly smaller — no malice required, just a downward adjustment nobody has to explain out loud. A client is short with you in one meeting and your investment in the relationship never quite recovers. What this practice narrows in on is smaller and more specific than trusting someone or handing them your best work regardless of a real problem: whether your estimate of what someone is still capable of becoming has to wait for good behavior, or whether it gets permanently revised the moment they hand you one data point you didn't like.

## Lowering someone's rank without a word

Off the clock, the recalculation usually runs faster. A friend snaps at you once, and your private estimate of them quietly resets — not hatred, just a notch removed, a slightly cooler weight assigned to their future opinions. You never announce the downgrade; it just governs how much of yourself you bring to the next conversation. What the sutra actually describes is narrower than blanket trust: the monk retreated — he protected himself from the stones — but he didn't revise what he was calling out. Getting out of someone's range and revising your estimate of them are two separate operations, and it's possible to do the first without doing the second.

## In closing

The sutra doesn't end with the beating. Near the end of his life, the text says, he heard the old teaching once more, out of thin air, and his life extended, his senses sharpened — and he kept teaching, in public, at full strength. The very people who'd once cursed him watched what he'd become and, this time, believed him. Whatever you make of a story staged in an unmeasurable past, the detail surviving every layer of retelling is the plainest one: cornered and outnumbered, he trimmed the words down, not the promise inside them — and he never took back what he'd said to any of the people who'd tried to make him stop.
