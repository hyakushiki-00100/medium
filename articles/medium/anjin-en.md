# "My Mind Isn't at Peace. Fix It." "Show Me This Mind, and I Will." The Zen Story of "Anjin" (安心).

A man arrives at a cave, asking to study under the teacher meditating inside. The teacher, already years into a long, silent practice, doesn't turn around. Later retellings say the man stood outside through a snowstorm all night, and, when that still wasn't answered, went further still to prove he meant it. What's better attested is what happened once he was finally let in. He said: my mind is not at peace — please pacify it for me. The teacher answered: bring me this mind of yours, and I'll pacify it. The man searched. I've looked for it, he said, and I can't find it anywhere. The teacher said: there. I've pacified it for you.

## A student who wouldn't be turned away

The man was Huike (487–593), who would become the Second Patriarch of Chan after studying under Bodhidharma, the semi-legendary founder of the tradition in China. Huike came to him in his forties, already educated in both Confucian classics and Buddhist scripture, and stayed roughly six years. What survives of their exchange is short enough to read in a breath — and it's the exchange, not the drama around it, that carried forward.

## What the earliest record doesn't confirm

Worth being honest about the drama. The detail everyone remembers — Huike cutting off his own arm in the snow to prove his sincerity — isn't well attested in the earliest sources. Some of the oldest biographical strands describe Huike's arm as lost to bandits while he was later spreading the teaching elsewhere entirely, not severed at Bodhidharma's door; other accounts suggest the vivid snow scene may have absorbed details from a different monk's story. Modern scholars treat the self-severed arm as likely legendary embellishment rather than established fact. What's more consistently attested is the substance of the exchange itself, recorded in the *Transmission of the Lamp* (景徳伝灯録, compiled 1004, roughly four centuries after Huike's death) and later as Case 41 of the *Gateless Gate* (無門関, compiled 1228). Even Bodhidharma's own dates remain disputed among historians — the record for this period of Chan's founding is thin enough that precision itself should be handled carefully.

## The search that was the answer

Bodhidharma's reply isn't a trick. Huike came in treating his unease as a fixed thing — a problem with a shape, sitting somewhere inside him, that a powerful enough teacher could reach in and remove. Told to produce it, he went looking for the actual object: not the feeling in general, but this exact unrest, right now, located somewhere specific. He couldn't find it, because there was nothing there to find in that form. The pacifying wasn't a technique applied to the mind. It was the collapse of the assumption that there'd been a fixed thing to pacify in the first place.

## Nine years facing a wall

There's a concrete detail worth holding alongside the exchange. Before Huike ever arrived, Bodhidharma had reportedly spent nine years at Shaolin facing a wall in silent meditation, so still and unmoving that people called him "the wall-gazing brahmin." The record of it appears in the same *Transmission of the Lamp* that preserves the exchange with Huike. Whatever else is legend, the image is consistent: a teacher who answers with almost nothing, because he'd already spent nine years finding out how little needed to be said.

## Looking for the anxious mind at work

- **Treating a feeling as a problem to be extracted.** Dread before a hard conversation, a knot before a presentation — the instinct is to find a technique that removes it, the way you'd remove a splinter, rather than actually looking at what it's made of right now.
- **Managing anxiety instead of examining it.** Breathing exercises and productivity systems can help, but they often work by distraction, not by ever actually locating the thing they're managing. Sometimes the more direct question — what exactly is this, precisely, right now — does more.
- **Assuming someone else has to remove it for you.** Huike wanted Bodhidharma to reach in and fix something. What he got instead was an instruction to look himself, which turned out to be the only place the answer was ever going to come from.

Huike's unrest was real enough to carry him across a mountain range in winter, so this isn't a claim that anxiety is somehow fake. The actual question is narrower: whether what you're managing is a fixed thing lodged inside you, or a feeling that changes shape the moment you turn and actually look at it.

## The mind you can't locate

The exercise is simple to describe and hard to actually do: the next time something won't settle, try to find it exactly, the way Huike was asked to. Not the general mood, not the story about it — the specific sensation, located precisely, right now. Most people discover what he did. It's real while you're avoiding a direct look at it, and considerably less solid the moment you actually try to point to it.

## In closing

Bodhidharma never told Huike what his mind was, or wasn't. He asked for it, watched him search, and let the search do what no explanation could have. Nine years of facing a wall had taught him that much, at least — that the most useful thing a teacher can sometimes do is ask a question precise enough that the answer arrives on its own.
