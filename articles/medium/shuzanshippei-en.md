# Every Answer Was Already Wrong. The Zen Koan "Shuzan Shippei" (首山竹篦).

Shoushan held up a bamboo stick in front of the assembled monks. Call this a bamboo stick, he said, and you've already gotten it wrong. Refuse to call it a bamboo stick, and you've gotten it wrong the other way. So — what do you call it?

## A stick with no correct name

Shoushan Shengnian¹ was a dharma heir of Fengxue Yanzhao² in the Linji lineage, and his own most notable student, Fenyang Shanzhao³, went on to become one of the first teachers to systematically collect and comment on koans — a discipline this exact case would later belong to. The stick in question was a *shippei*⁴: a length of split bamboo lacquered into a flat blade, used across Chan monasteries as a plain teaching instrument, sometimes literally applied to a dozing monk's shoulder. Shoushan wasn't using his to discipline anyone here. He held it up and posed a trap with two doors, both wrong.

## The escape hatch that doesn't open either

Wumen, collecting the case centuries later, refused to let silence count as a third option. Name it, he wrote, and you've touched it wrongly. Don't name it, and you've turned your back on it. You can't speak your way out, and you can't stay quiet your way out either. Say something — now.⁵ The case doesn't survive as a debate to be won. It survives as a door with no side you can stand on.

## A ten-year gap swallowed by a much bigger one

Shoushan died in 994, and his biography appears in the *Transmission of the Lamp*⁶, compiled just a decade later — an unusually short gap for this series. Whether this particular exchange, with this exact stick, made it into that early biography isn't something this piece can confirm. What's certain is the case's form as most people encounter it today: Wumen's *Gateless Gate*⁷, compiled well over two centuries after Shoushan's death, is the confirmed source for the story exactly as told here.

## The stick's second life

The *shippei* itself outlived the koan. In Japanese, *shippegaeshi* — literally "*shippei* returned" — became the everyday word for tit-for-tat payback, from the custom of a struck student occasionally grabbing the stick back and returning the blow. That's a fact about the object, not about this exchange; nobody in Shoushan's story hits anybody. But it's a useful reminder that the stick itself was never precious. It was a prop built to be picked up, argued with, and occasionally swung back.

## Neither label was ever going to fit

The same rigged choice shows up constantly at work, usually disguised as a request for a verdict. Was the miss negligence or bad luck. Is this a technical problem or a people problem. Is the plan too aggressive or too timid. Both labels usually capture something real and distort something else, and picking the "correct" one mostly just picks which distortion you'll spend the next month defending. The sharper move is rarely finding the right box. It's noticing when a question has already been built with only two boxes in it.

## Turning the same question inward

The same shape gets aimed at yourself in quieter moments. Was that decision right or wrong. Am I someone who handles pressure well or badly. Both verdicts tend to arrive already dressed as the obvious truth, and both leave out most of what actually happened. Refusing to answer doesn't get you out of it either — that's its own quiet verdict, usually the harshest one going. Shoushan's stick was never really asking for the correct word. It was asking whether you'd noticed the two doors were built from the same wood.

## In closing

Nobody in the assembly answers Shoushan in the record that survives. Maybe that's the most honest response available: not silence as a strategy, not a clever third label smuggled in past the two bad ones, just a long look at a piece of bamboo that was never going to hold still long enough to be named correctly.
