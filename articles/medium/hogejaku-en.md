# Put It Down — Even "I'm Carrying Nothing." The Zen Word "Hōgejaku (放下著)."

"Just let it go." It's the advice everyone gives and almost no one can follow. Trace the Zen phrase behind it — *hōgejaku* (放下著), "put it down" — and you find something sharper than reassurance: a teaching that even your letting-go is one more thing to set down.

## The monk who brought nothing

The phrase comes from an exchange with Zhaozhou (778–897), the Tang-dynasty master famous for answering enormous questions with almost nothing at all — famously telling two monks who gave opposite answers to the same question, "have you been here before," the identical instruction: go have some tea. A monk came to him and announced, with evident pride, "I have brought not a single thing." He meant it as an achievement: no attachments, no baggage, arrived empty-handed.

Zhaozhou said: "Put it down." (*Hōgejaku.*)

The monk was baffled. "But I've brought nothing at all — what is there to put down?"

Zhaozhou replied: "Then carry it off with you."

## What the monk was actually carrying

Read quickly, it sounds like a riddle. But the monk had, in fact, brought something heavy: the idea *"I have nothing."* His emptiness had become a possession. His non-attachment had become the one thing he was most attached to. "Put it down," Zhaozhou tells him — put down even the pride of having put everything down. And when the monk can't, the master's second line lands like a shrug: fine, then carry it. The load stays yours for exactly as long as you insist on it.

The character 著 on the end isn't decoration; it's an emphatic imperative — *now, this instant, drop it.* Not someday. Not as a project. Now.

## A caveat about the record

Worth being honest: exchanges like this were preserved and shaped by later generations, collected in Zen anthologies compiled well after Zhaozhou's lifetime. We're reading a teaching distilled by the tradition, not a courtroom transcript. What it carries is not a historical fact so much as a stubbornly useful one.

## Where this lands at work

Most of us know the obvious loads: the task list, the unread inbox, the worry we replay at 2 a.m. Hōgejaku points instead at the ones we don't notice, because we're quietly proud of them.

- **The identity of the person who handles everything.** Delegating a task is easy. Delegating the belief that *only you* can do it right is the real weight — and until you set that down, you'll take the task back within a week.
- **The system itself.** The productivity method, the perfect note-taking setup, the morning routine — each begins as relief and slowly becomes another thing to maintain, defend, and carry. When the tool for lightening the load becomes part of the load, that is your "not a single thing."
- **The open loops.** Every "I'll deal with it later" is a small object still sitting in your hands. Closing it — or deciding, cleanly, not to — is the actual act of putting down.

None of these show up on a to-do list, which is exactly why they never get done. They aren't tasks. They're grips.

## The heaviest thing you carry

There's an inward version, and it's the sharpest one. The person who says "Oh, I've completely let that go" is very often the one carrying it hardest. "I'm not bothered by what they think." "I'm over it." "I don't need the recognition." Each of these can be genuine — or it can be the monk's "I have nothing," a load wearing the face of freedom.

Notice how much energy it can take to keep insisting you've moved on. The test is simple. If the putting-down were truly finished, you wouldn't need to announce it. Hōgejaku asks you to drop the announcement too.

## In closing

Your hands are not empty because you are holding "nothing." They are empty when they stop gripping — including their grip on emptiness itself. Zhaozhou never told the monk he was wrong to arrive with nothing. He only pointed out that the monk was still clutching it, knuckles white, and offered the one instruction that fits every case: put it down. And if you can't yet, at least know exactly what it is you're carrying.
