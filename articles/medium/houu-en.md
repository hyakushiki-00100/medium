# Dharma Rain: The Same Rain, Different Growth

The word *houu* (法雨, "Dharma rain") comes from the Parable of the Medicinal Herbs (*yakusou-yu*) in the Lotus Sutra, where the Buddha's teaching is compared to rain falling equally on every kind of plant. The same rain falls on all of them, yet a small herb drinks a little, a tall tree drinks a lot, and each grows to whatever height its own roots and trunk allow. The core insight: the teaching is given equally, but how each being grows from it is allowed to differ. It translates directly into organizations that assume uniform training or feedback should produce uniform results.

**Source**: Chapter 5 (the Parable of the Medicinal Herbs) of the Lotus Sutra, one of the seven parables of the sutra. The content holds consistent across the sutra text itself and multiple sectarian commentaries.

---

## The Parable

The Buddha addresses his disciple Mahakashyapa. Picture the whole world: mountains, valleys, and plains covered with grasses, shrubs, and great trees of every size and shape. A single cloud spreads across the sky and rains down equally on all of it, without exception. Each small herb draws up water in proportion to its roots and stem; each great tree draws up water in proportion to its trunk and branches. One and the same rain falls, yet every plant flourishes, flowers, and bears fruit according to its own capacity.

This rain is the metaphor for the Buddha's teaching — the Dharma rain. The teaching itself falls equally on everyone, without favoring anyone. But how much each person absorbs, and what they grow into, depends on the vessel each person already is.

## What This Means Today

What's striking about this parable is that it doesn't treat the resulting differences as a flaw. A small herb growing less than a great tree isn't evidence that the rain was unfair. It simply has less root and less stem to draw water with — the capacity to receive is different to begin with. The teaching makes no attempt to flatten that difference. It affirms each plant growing into whatever form is true to it.

This is a quiet counterargument to how easily "equal" and "identical" get conflated.

| Common conflation | What the parable actually shows |
|---|---|
| Equal = guaranteeing the same outcome for everyone | Equal = offering the same opportunity (rain) to everyone |
| Different growth speeds = someone's failure | Different growth speeds = a natural result of different capacity |
| The teacher's job = eliminating the differences | The teacher's job = continuing to provide the rain, equally |

## Where This Shows Up at Work

- In training or on-the-job coaching, the same explanation lands at different speeds for different people. That's not evidence the coaching was unfair — it reflects differences in prior experience and aptitude, the "vessel" each person brings.
- The same feedback delivered to an entire team changes some people's behavior and not others'. Before questioning the quality of the feedback, it's worth asking whether the recipients' own readiness was ever equal to begin with.
- A manager pushes to "develop everyone the same way" and grows anxious at every difference in growth rate. Providing equal rain — equal access, equal information, equal support — and shaping everyone into an identical form are simply two different jobs.

What a person in a coaching role can actually control ends at providing the rain, consistently and equally. What grows from it, and how much, is left to the recipient's own capacity and time. Holding that line clearly can spare a manager or mentor a great deal of needless self-blame.

## Two Questions for Practice

1. Am I confusing the responsibility to "provide the rain equally" with the responsibility to "control exactly how every tree grows"?
2. When I notice a gap in growth, am I treating it as proof that coaching failed — before considering that it may simply reflect a difference in capacity?

### A Related Phrase: "One Rain Moistens a Thousand Mountains"

The same theme appears condensed into a four-character Zen phrase, *ichiu senzan wo uruosu* (一雨潤千山) — "one rain moistens a thousand mountains" — describing how a single rainfall nourishes countless mountains equally, used for how one teaching or action can benefit many at once, though the specific classical text it's drawn from is harder to confirm. Where "Dharma rain" carries the full parable, including the differences in how each plant grows, "one rain moistens a thousand mountains" is a more compact phrase focused simply on how widely a single benefit reaches.
