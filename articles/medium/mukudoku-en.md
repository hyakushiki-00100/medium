# You Did the Right Thing. Stop Waiting for the Receipt. The Zen Answer "No Merit" (無功徳).

Emperor Wu of Liang had, by any reasonable accounting, done an enormous amount of good. He'd built temples across his kingdom, funded the ordination of monks, commissioned the copying of sutras and the casting of Buddhist images. So when the Indian monk Bodhidharma arrived at his court, the emperor asked what seems like a perfectly fair question: how much merit had all of that earned him? Bodhidharma's answer was two words. *Mukudoku.* No merit at all.

## An emperor asks for his receipt

Bodhidharma is traditionally credited as the founder of Zen in China, though how much of his biography is history and how much is later legend is genuinely unclear — his own dates are disputed, and this exact exchange survives mainly through much later retellings. What isn't in dispute is how the story functions in the tradition: Emperor Wu, a serious and generous patron of Buddhism, lists his accomplishments and asks for the tally. Bodhidharma refuses to give him one. The emperor, baffled, presses further — asks what the highest truth of Buddhism actually is. Bodhidharma answers, "Vast emptiness, nothing holy." The emperor asks who, then, is standing before him. "I don't know," Bodhidharma says, and leaves for the kingdom of Wei.

## What "no merit" doesn't mean

It's tempting to read this as Bodhidharma dismissing the emperor's works as worthless. He wasn't. Temples got built either way; monks were fed either way. The refusal wasn't aimed at the deeds. It was aimed at the ledger the emperor was keeping alongside them — the quiet expectation that virtue, deposited correctly, should accrue interest and eventually pay out. Good works done *toward* a return aren't disqualified from being good works, but they've been quietly converted into a transaction, and a transaction is a much smaller thing than what the emperor thought he was engaged in.

## Where this lands at work

- **The favor with an invoice attached.** Help extended with half an eye on the future ask isn't generosity; it's financing. The tell is how it feels when the return never arrives — resentment is what a loan looks like when it isn't repaid.
- **The mentor keeping a ledger.** Time spent developing someone, remembered later as a debt they owe you, was never really given. Something was lent.
- **Recognition-seeking dressed as diligence.** Publishing the win, making sure the right people saw the effort — none of this is wrong, but it's worth being honest about which parts were the work and which parts were the receipt.

None of this is an argument for self-erasure, or for pretending you don't want to be seen doing good work. Wanting to be seen is human, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of performance. It's a distinction between the deed and the accounting attached to it — and a question worth asking before either one: which am I actually doing right now?

## The ledger you keep on yourself

There's a private version of this that has nothing to do with anyone watching. Plenty of people do the right thing and then spend the rest of the day cashing it in internally — quietly filing it as evidence they're a good person, a credit toward some running total of self-worth. That, too, is a ledger, just kept in a private account with an audience of one. Bodhidharma's answer applies there with the same force: the moment a good act gets converted into proof of your own goodness, it has stopped being simply what it was and started being collateral.

## In closing

Mukudoku isn't nihilism about good deeds. It's a refusal to let them get quietly annexed by the accounting department. Build the temple. Feed the monks. Do the actual work in front of you. And when you catch yourself waiting for the return on it — recognition, gratitude, some future favor owed — that's the moment worth noticing, not because the waiting is a moral failure, but because it means you've stopped doing the good thing and started doing something smaller that merely resembles it.
