# "Results Take Time" Isn't a Pep Talk. It's the Zen Case for Sequence.

"Don't rush the results — they'll come in time."

Hearing that, most people don't quite buy it. It sounds like something you say to make impatience feel better. But trace the phrase back to its Zen origin, and instead of a pep talk, you find something closer to a description of how plants are actually built.

## Bodhidharma's last verse

Bodhidharma, the semi-legendary founder of Zen in China, is said to have left this verse for his student Huike shortly before his death, recorded at the end of the *Shōshitsu Rokumon-shū*:

"I came originally to this land to transmit the teaching and save deluded beings. One flower opens five petals, and the fruit forms of itself."

This is the source of *ikke goyō o hiraku* (一華開五葉) — "one flower opens five petals" — paired with *kekka jinen ni naru* (結果自然成), "the result forms naturally." The round, tumbling *daruma* doll familiar in Japan is modeled on this same Bodhidharma. It's worth pausing on that: the toy most people know him for is a lighthearted echo of a man whose last recorded words were about patience.

## There are actually two readings

Tradition holds two interpretations of this line side by side, and it's worth naming both rather than picking one.

The first: Bodhidharma was predicting that the single school of Zen he brought to China would branch into five schools and flourish. It's a fitting prediction — over the following centuries, Chinese Zen did split into five recognized lineages, including what became Rinzai and Sōtō.

The second, associated with the teacher Dōgen: the "one flower" and "five petals" aren't a prophecy about the future at all — they point to nothing more than **the state of mind, fully present, right here and now**. Flower and petals are already here. *Confidence: high that both readings are long-established and taught side by side — this isn't a case of one being the "correct" interpretation.*

## A plum tree does not rush its fruit

Whichever reading you take, the image underneath is about **sequence**.

First the flower opens. Then leaves fill in. Only then does fruit form. This order can't be reversed, and it can't be skipped. Zen temples traditionally hang this verse as a scroll in early spring — the plum blossom season, when flowers push through before the cold has fully lifted, leaves following only afterward. The sequence in the verse and the sequence on the branch line up exactly.

We tend to try to reverse this sequence anyway. We want results before the groundwork is laid, recognition before trust is built, a harvest before the seed is even in the ground. What this old line hands down instead is a kind of trust in sequence: do the work in the right order, and the result tends to arrive on its own.

That's not an argument for passivity. Preparing the soil and watering the roots is unglamorous, repetitive work. We often misread cause and effect as something we can bend to our own schedule — set the cause in motion, and expect to pull the result out whenever suits us. Nature's causality isn't that obliging. You can control the preparation. You cannot control when the fruit decides to form. Most of the impatience we feel comes from trying to override that one part anyway.

## Applying it at work

- **If you're still in the "flower" stage, don't demand fruit yet.** Learning a new skill, building trust with a team, laying institutional groundwork — these are flower-stage work. Forcing a harvest out of them too early tends to damage the flower itself.
- **The five petals don't open at once.** When something isn't moving the way you hoped, that's not necessarily failure — it may just be that its turn hasn't come yet.
- **"Forms naturally" doesn't mean "do nothing." It means keep doing the right steps, without grabbing for the outcome.** Letting go of urgency and letting go of effort are two very different things — this phrase only asks for the first.

## The five petals are inside you too

There's also a way to read this line pointed inward. Every skill, every relationship of trust, every habit takes time in which no visible result shows up at all. That stretch isn't failure — it's the flower stage. Knowing which of the five petals you're currently trying to open, without rushing past it, tends to be the shortest path, even when it feels like the slowest one.

Bodhidharma's words are nearly 1,500 years old. In a culture built around quarterly results, they may land harder now than they did then.
