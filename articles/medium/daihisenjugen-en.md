# Two Brothers Described the Same Miracle Differently. One Answer Was Graded Only Eighty Percent Complete. The Zen Koan "Daihi Senju Gen" (大悲千手眼).

Yunyan¹ asked his older brother Daowu² what the Bodhisattva of Great Compassion actually does with all those hands and eyes. Daowu said it's like someone reaching back in the middle of the night to feel for a pillow. Yunyan said he understood. Asked how, he said: hands and eyes, all over the body. Daowu told him that was well put, but only about eighty percent there. Asked for his own version, he said: hands and eyes, the whole body itself.

## The part that's missing from an accurate answer

Nothing in Yunyan's answer was wrong. A many-armed bodhisattva does, in a sense, have capability distributed across an enormous number of points, and describing it that way isn't a bad description. It just describes a collection instead of a single thing. The pillow in the dark is the detail that gives the difference away: nobody locates a hand, then locates the pillow, then coordinates the two. There's no moment where a specific tool gets selected for the job. The whole sleeping body just finds it. Yunyan's version still had parts doing work. Daowu's had nothing left to distribute.

## Recorded by their own generation

The dialogue is best known through the Blue Cliff Record³, compiled in 1125, but the two men speaking in it were long dead by then — Yunyan died in 841, and the earlier of the two, Daowu, is dated by tradition to 769–835, though at least one later source gives him a longer life. Both appear, brothers and fellow students of Yaoshan Weiyan, in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁴, compiled in 1004. Their teacher's own generation had already been written into the Song Biographies of Eminent Monks⁵ by 988. Whichever record a reader picks up, the shape of the exchange stays exactly the same: an answer good enough to sound finished, and a brother willing to say it wasn't.

## A thousand hands, carved in wood down the hall

Temples across Japan still house thousand-armed images of this same bodhisattva, most famously the hall in Kyoto lined with over a thousand life-sized statues, each one bristling with dozens of small hands fanned out from its sides. Standing in front of one, the instinct is to count — to treat the extra hands as an inventory, more tools for more jobs. Yunyan's answer would have fit that hall perfectly. Daowu's wouldn't have needed the extra arms carved on at all.

## The workaround bolted onto the process

A team under pressure tends to solve problems by attaching something new: another checklist, another automation, another specialist brought in for the one recurring failure mode. Each addition is a reasonable, well-placed hand, and after enough of them the process does look like the temple hall — heavily equipped, visibly capable, covered in tools for the moments that used to go wrong. What that buildup rarely produces is a team that no longer needs to reach for anything, because the skill has become the way the whole team works rather than a growing collection of fixes attached to its weak points.

## Collecting instruments instead of becoming one

The same pattern shows up in how people build a self. A journaling habit for the anxious mornings, a breathing technique for the hard conversations, a tracker for the discipline that won't hold on its own — each one a hand added on, each one genuinely useful, and each one still a tool that has to be reached for and correctly applied. None of that is wrong so much as incomplete, in the specific way Yunyan's answer was incomplete: technically accurate, and still describing someone with a lot of separate parts rather than someone who has simply become more capable, all the way through.

## In closing

Yunyan's answer wasn't corrected so much as it was outgrown, right there in the same conversation. He'd described a bodhisattva with an enormous number of well-placed hands. His brother described one who no longer needed to reach for anything, because there was nothing left that wasn't already the hand.
