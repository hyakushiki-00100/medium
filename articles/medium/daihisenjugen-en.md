# Two Brothers Described the Same Miracle Differently. One Answer Was Graded Only Eighty Percent Complete. The Zen Koan "Daihi Senju Gen" (大悲千手眼).

Yunyan¹ asked his older brother Daowu² what the Bodhisattva of Great Compassion actually does with all those hands and eyes. Daowu said it's like someone reaching back in the middle of the night to feel for a pillow. Yunyan said he understood. Asked how, he said: hands and eyes, all over the body. Daowu told him that was well put, but only about eighty percent there. Asked for his own version, he said: hands and eyes, the whole body itself.

## What a correct answer still leaves out

Nothing in Yunyan's answer was wrong. A many-armed bodhisattva does, in a sense, have capability distributed across an enormous number of points, and describing it that way isn't a bad description. It just describes a collection instead of a single thing. The pillow in the dark is the detail that gives the difference away: nobody locates a hand, then locates the pillow, then coordinates the two. There's no moment where a specific tool gets selected for the job. The whole sleeping body just finds it. Yunyan's version still had parts doing work. Daowu's didn't — though the tradition itself never fully settled which phrasing wins. A verse appended to this same exchange by Xuedou Chongxian³ dismisses both answers together: whichever one you pick up, he wrote, you're still ten thousand miles short.

## Whichever brother said which line first

The earliest surviving account of this exchange appears to be the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁴, compiled in 952, not much more than a century after both brothers had died. It doesn't settle who said what, because the later chronicles don't even agree with each other. The Blue Cliff Record⁵, compiled in 1125, has Yunyan asking the question and Daowu supplying both the pillow and the winning phrase. Earlier lamp-histories, including the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁶ from 1004, tell it from Yunyan's side of the family and reverse the roles entirely — Daowu asks, Yunyan answers with the pillow, and Yunyan is the one who tops his older brother's phrase. Chan chronicles have a habit of quietly favoring whichever monk's own biography they happen to be telling, and this exchange seems to have been reshuffled more than once for exactly that reason. Even the eighty-percent verdict may be a later addition — the plainest surviving fragment of the exchange carries only the final phrase, with no score attached to anything. What holds up across every retelling, regardless of which brother gets the sharper line, is the shape of it: an answer good enough to sound complete, and someone close enough to say it wasn't quite.

## A thousand hands, standing in a hall in Kyoto

Temples across Japan still house images of this same bodhisattva built around the number in its name. The most famous is a Kyoto hall lined with a thousand life-sized standing figures surrounding one larger seated image at the center, each figure's forty-two carved arms standing in for a thousand — two folded in prayer, forty fanned out to the sides, a conventional shorthand the tradition settled on centuries ago. Standing in front of one, the instinct is to count — to treat the extra arms as an inventory, more tools for more jobs. Yunyan's answer would have fit that hall perfectly. Daowu's wouldn't have needed the extra arms carved on at all.

## The workaround bolted onto the process

A team under pressure tends to solve problems by attaching something new: another checklist, another automation, another specialist brought in for the one recurring failure mode. Each addition is a reasonable, well-placed hand, and after enough of them the process does look like the temple hall — heavily equipped, visibly capable, covered in tools for the moments that used to go wrong. What that buildup rarely produces is a team that no longer needs to reach for anything, because the skill has become the way the whole team works rather than a growing collection of fixes attached to its weak points.

## Explaining a skill instead of just having it

People get asked to explain their own competence all the time, and the explanation tends to take the same shape. Asked how they stay calm during a crisis, some people can recite the whole kit — a breath count, a phrase repeated under the tongue, a five-second pause before responding — and every item on the list is genuinely something they do. Someone else just handles the actual emergency and shrugs when asked how, because there wasn't a kit consulted in the moment, just a way of being under pressure that doesn't come apart into steps the instant it's asked to explain itself. The first description isn't false. It's still a description of parts.

## In closing

Depending on which chronicle a reader trusts, it was the older brother or the younger one who supplied the sharper phrase — and the tradition itself never quite settled which phrase deserved the higher mark. What holds steady either way is smaller than that argument: an answer that sounded finished, and a brother close enough to say it wasn't quite.
