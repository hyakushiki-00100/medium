# A Monk Described Himself as Beyond Sight and Sound. The Ferryman Threw Him in the River and Held Him Under. The Zen Story "Mosshoseki" (没蹤跡).

Chuanzi¹ called out before the young monk had even reached the bank: "Which monastery are you from?" Jiashan² said he wasn't attached to any monastery — otherwise he wouldn't look the way he looked. Asked what he looked like, he answered: something beyond sight, beyond sound, beyond awareness itself. Chuanzi grabbed him, threw him into the river, and held him under before letting him up.

## An answer assembled entirely out of other people's words

"Beyond sight, sound, and awareness" isn't a description. It's a formula — the kind of phrase a well-read monk could produce about enlightenment without having had a single moment of it, borrowed straight out of doctrine and handed back as if it were a report from the inside. Chuanzi wasn't objecting to the content. He was answering the fact that nothing in the sentence had actually come from Jiashan. Half-drowned, Jiashan tried again, and Chuanzi struck him before a word got out a second time. Something gave way that hadn't given way the first time. Jiashan nodded three times. Chuanzi pulled him out and said he'd finally landed a real fish. Then he told him where to go from here: hide in a place with no trace of you — and don't hide in "no trace" either, as if that were a cleverer place to stand. He rowed his boat to the middle of the river, tipped it over, and was never seen again.

## Decades at a river crossing

Chuanzi Decheng was a dharma heir of Yaoshan Weiyan³, though no birth or death dates for him survive; he spent an unknown number of years working a river crossing, known to the people who used it only as the boatman. Jiashan Shanhui⁴ sought him out on the advice of Daowu Yuanzhi⁵, unsatisfied with an answer Jiashan had given in a public lecture. The earliest account of the exchange that can be confirmed is the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁶, compiled in 952; the more widely circulated version, with Chuanzi's closing instruction and the boat's final trip to the middle of the river, appears in the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁷, compiled in 1004. The phrase used here as this story's name, "no trace," is Chuanzi's own line to Jiashan — it isn't a fixed case title the way some koans have one, but it's the phrase this exchange is most often remembered by.

## Sixteen days, then back to the field

In 458 BCE, with Rome's army encircled, the Senate appointed a farmer named Cincinnatus dictator, handing him total command. He raised a force, broke the siege, and within sixteen days resigned the position and returned to his field — a story ancient historians themselves treat with some suspicion, embellished if not invented outright to make a point about restraint. The point survives the doubt about the facts: the position wasn't taken from him. He set it down the moment the job that justified holding it was finished, before anyone had to ask.

## A grip that outlasted its own reason

A project wraps, and there's a specific kind of person who keeps attending its meetings anyway — reviewing decisions that were already made, weighing in on a direction the team moved past weeks ago. No one assigned them that role after the fact; they just kept it, the way a hand keeps closing around something after the reason for holding on has already gone. Letting go here doesn't require a dramatic renunciation. It just requires noticing that the job finished before the grip did.

## Nothing left to borrow the second time

There's a gap, closer to home, between an answer produced because it sounds like the kind of thing a person who'd figured this out would say, and whatever's left once that performance gets interrupted. The first kind survives most conversations intact. It doesn't survive being asked twice, or asked by someone who already knows exactly which words are borrowed. What answers the second time isn't smarter. It just hasn't had time to assemble anything.

## In closing

Jiashan came looking for a teacher and got thrown in a river instead, which turns out to have been the teaching. Chuanzi finished the one thing he'd apparently been waiting decades to do, and instead of staying to watch how the story turned out, he capsized the boat and left no trace of himself for anyone to find — not even, by his own instruction, in the place marked "no trace."
