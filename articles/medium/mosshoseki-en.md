# A Monk Said His Understanding Came From Beyond the Senses. The Ferryman Knocked Him Into the River With an Oar. The Zen Story "Mosshoseki" (没蹤跡).

Chuanzi¹ asked the visiting monk where he'd learned to talk that way. Jiashan² said: somewhere ears and eyes can't reach. Chuanzi told him a rehearsed phrase is a stake that ties up a donkey for ten thousand years, then dropped a line of his own — a thousand-foot thread straight down into a deep pool, three inches from the hook: speak. Jiashan opened his mouth. The oar caught him first, and he went into the water.

## The second attempt didn't come from anywhere

Jiashan climbed back into the boat. "Speak," Chuanzi said again. "Speak." Jiashan opened his mouth a second time, and Chuanzi struck before a syllable got out. Something gave way that hadn't given way after the first strike. Jiashan nodded three times. What changed between the two attempts wasn't skill — Jiashan hadn't had time to get more skillful in the seconds between them. What changed was that the second time, there was nothing left to reach for. The donkey-stake line was the whole diagnosis: a correct-sounding phrase, recited on cue, does the opposite of freeing anyone. It ties them to the spot where they first heard it. Chuanzi's parting instruction, in the earliest form of this story, was to hide somewhere with no trace of yourself — and to hide there too, rather than settling into "untraceable" as a fresher hiding place. A later, more quoted version of the same line turns that second half into a flat denial instead: don't hide there either. Both versions are chasing the same problem. A hiding place you can name is a hiding place you can still perform.

## A better ending, added two centuries later

Jiashan had been giving a public lecture when an answer of his failed to satisfy Daowu Yuanzhi³, who was in the audience; Daowu laughed outright and told him he was already out teaching with no teacher of his own, then sent him to find Chuanzi. Chuanzi was a dharma heir of Yaoshan Weiyan⁴ — sources disagree on Yaoshan's exact dates — and had spent an unknown number of years working a river crossing, known to the people who used it only as the boatman. The earliest account of the river exchange that survives is the Anthology of the Patriarchal Hall⁵, compiled in 952 (its earliest complete surviving text is a Korean woodblock edition from 1245); the Jingde-era record of the lamp⁶, compiled in 1004, confirms the strike and Jiashan's awakening but ends more simply, with Chuanzi abandoning his boat and departing, his fate afterward unrecorded. The fuller story — the three nods, the line about finally landing a fish with golden scales, and the version of the parting instruction phrased as a flat denial — is first confirmable in the Essentials of the Collected Lamp⁷, compiled in 1183, well over two centuries after the exchange itself.

## A general who put down power the moment the job was done

In 458 BCE, with a Roman army encircled by the Aequi, the Senate had Cincinnatus appointed dictator, handing him total command. He broke the siege, and within sixteen days resigned the position and returned to his farm. Modern historians treat the episode with some suspicion — the details look shaped to make a point about restraint, whether or not every detail happened exactly as told. What the doubt doesn't touch is the shape of the story people kept retelling: the position wasn't taken from him. He set it down himself, the moment the job that justified holding it was finished.

## Present long after the reason for it was gone

A project wraps, and there's a specific kind of person who keeps attending its meetings anyway — reviewing decisions that were already made, weighing in on a direction the team moved past weeks ago. No one reassigned them to that role afterward; they just kept it, the way a hand keeps closing around something after the reason for holding on has already gone. Letting go here doesn't require a dramatic renunciation. It just requires noticing that the job finished before the grip did.

## Nothing left to recite the second time

An answer produced because it sounds like the kind of thing a person who'd figured this out would say is a different thing entirely from whatever's left once that performance gets interrupted. The first kind survives most conversations intact. It doesn't survive being asked twice, or asked by someone who already knows exactly which words are borrowed. What answers the second time isn't smarter. It just hasn't had time to reach for anything.

## In closing

Jiashan took his leave, and kept looking back as he went — enough times that Chuanzi called out to him, raised the oar, and said he clearly still believed there was something more to find. Then he capsized the boat and went under with it. What survived him wasn't the absence he'd told Jiashan to aim for. It was a handful of fishing songs, one of them ending on a night so still the fish wouldn't bite, the boat coming back empty, loaded with nothing but moonlight.
