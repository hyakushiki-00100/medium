**1/**
Asked by a monk how to escape the cold and the heat when they arrive, a teacher gives what sounds like directions.

Go to the place, he says, where there's no cold and no heat. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Where is that place, the monk asks.

---

**3/**
The teacher answers: when it's cold, let the cold kill you. When it's hot, let the heat kill you.

---

**4/**
"Heat and cold" isn't really about weather here. In Chan idiom it stands for anything uncomfortable, anything you'd rather not be dealing with: the whole category of things worth escaping.

---

**5/**
The monk's question is a reasonable one. Everyone seeks warmth in winter and shade in summer, and there's nothing wrong with that instinct. What he's actually asking for, though, is a place where discomfort itself doesn't exist.

---

**6/**
That first answer sounds like he's granting the request. Then the second question lands, and the reply refuses to point anywhere. What he offers isn't a destination. It's an instruction to be killed by whichever one arrives.

---

**7/**
Dongshan Liangjie (807–869) didn't settle easily himself. He studied first under Nanquan Puyuan, then under Guishan Lingyou, before finally receiving transmission from Yunyan Tansheng and founding what became known in Japan as the Sōtō school.

---

**8/**
The exchange survives as case forty-three of the Blue Cliff Record, compiled in 1125 by Yuanwu Keqin, though the collection's real foundation is the verse commentary Xuedou Chongxian wrote roughly a century earlier.

---

**9/**
The dialogue itself likely predates Xuedou's verse, preserved first in Dongshan's own recorded sayings before it was chosen, centuries later, for a collection meant to last.

---

**10/**
So what survives isn't a transcript of one afternoon. It's a line that multiple generations of teachers judged worth keeping in circulation, which is its own kind of evidence for what it's doing.

---

**11/**
"Let it kill you" sounds violent for a teaching about weather. It isn't pointing at anything close to literal death. What's asked for is total, unreserved absorption into whatever's actually happening: no leftover self standing outside it, waiting for it to pass.

---

**12/**
Chan monasteries had nothing resembling central heating. Winter meditation happened on stone floors that held the cold. Breath went visible in the hall, and hands went numb around prayer beads.

---

**13/**
Telling a monk in that condition to let the cold kill him isn't an instruction to grit his teeth and endure. It's an instruction to stop treating the shivering as a problem happening to someone separate from the shivering.

---

**14/**
Notice where the relief keeps getting filed. Tight deadlines, a hard negotiation, work you haven't done before: a lot of people treat all of it as weather to get through until the next calm stretch. Then the next project arrives, carrying the same postponement.

---

**15/**
The cool place stays exactly one commitment ahead, permanently.

---

**16/**
None of this is a case for taking on less work. It's a question about whether the cool place being searched for was ever actually out there.

---

**17/**
Where this lands hardest isn't the calendar. It's the remark that starts replaying around midnight, and what Dongshan's answer means carried into that room. The article works it through, plus two more places the cool place keeps moving one step ahead 👇
