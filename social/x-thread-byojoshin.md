---

**1/**
A student asks his teacher what the Way actually is: the whole point of the practice, the thing everyone is supposedly training toward.

The teacher's answer is five words. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Byōjōshin kore dō. Ordinary mind is the Way. Not an elevated mind, not a mind in the zone, not a peak state you have to earn. The plain, unremarkable mind you're using to read this sentence.

---

**3/**
The exchange is between Nanquan Puyuan (748–835) and his student Zhaozhou Congshen (778–897), who would go on to become one of the most quoted voices in the entire Zen canon and reportedly lived to the improbable age of 119.

---

**4/**
The phrase itself predates Nanquan. It's usually traced to his own teacher, Mazu Daoyi, and Nanquan is best understood as carrying it forward rather than coining it outright.

---

**5/**
Zhaozhou asks, "What is the Way?" Nanquan answers, "Ordinary mind is the Way." Zhaozhou, characteristically, pushes back: "Then should I try to direct myself toward it?"

---

**6/**
Nanquan's reply is the sharper half of the exchange: "The moment you aim at it, you've already turned away."

---

**7/**
Worth being honest about the record: this is preserved as Case 19 of the Gateless Gate, compiled in 1228, nearly four centuries after Nanquan's death. What we have is the teaching as the tradition carried it forward, not minutes taken that afternoon.

---

**8/**
Nanquan spent roughly three decades living self-sufficiently on Nanquan Mountain before he ever taught publicly, farming and studying in near isolation: the kind of ordinary, repetitive labor his own answer would later point back to.

---

**9/**
That second line is the whole teaching. The Way isn't reached by striving toward it, because striving is itself the act of leaving ordinary mind behind. You cannot force your way into being unforced.

---

**10/**
Ordinary mind isn't a mood at all. It's the mind doing whatever's in front of it, sweeping a floor, answering an email, without a second layer of commentary running on top, quietly grading the performance.

---

**11/**
The "peak state" everyone chases is usually just ordinary mind plus a great deal of anxious narration about whether it's the right state. Drop the narration, and what's left was already sufficient.

---

**12/**
One place this bites: waiting to feel ready. Motivation gets treated as a prerequisite for starting. The steadier move is the reverse: begin in an entirely ordinary state and let engagement arrive through the work itself, not before it.

---

**13/**
