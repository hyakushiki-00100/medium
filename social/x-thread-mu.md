---

**1/**
A monk puts a doctrinal question to an old teacher: does a dog have Buddha-nature?

By every text the monk had studied, the answer was settled before he opened his mouth. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
The teacher answers anyway: "Mu." No.

No argument, no scripture, no softening. The exchange ends there.

---

**3/**
The doctrine behind the question comes from the Nirvana Sutra, which states plainly that all sentient beings possess Buddha-nature. It's a foundational claim of the tradition both men had given their lives to.

---

**4/**
Which makes asking about a dog less a question than a formality, the kind of thing you confirm before moving on to something harder. Zhaozhou Congshen (778–897) was expected to say yes.

---

**5/**
"Mu" wasn't a wrong answer in the ordinary sense. It was an answer that shouldn't have been available at all, which is exactly why generations of students have never been able to walk past it.

---

**6/**
There isn't much primary source to read here, which is itself the point. Case 1 of the Mumonkan, which Wumen Huikai assembled in 1228, reads in full: "A monk asked Zhaozhou, 'Does a dog have Buddha-nature or not?' Zhaozhou said, 'Mu.'"

---

**7/**
No scene, no follow-up question, no reasoning attached. Later retellings that give the exchange a fuller shape, a startled monk, a raised eyebrow, a lesson drawn out in the room, are additions the base text doesn't support.

---

**8/**
It isn't the only version on record either, and the difference matters. Hongzhi Zhengjue's Extensive Record, compiled decades before Wumen's case, preserves the same "no" but doesn't leave it standing alone.

---

**9/**
There a monk asks the identical question, gets the identical answer, then presses: every sentient being is said to have Buddha-nature, so why not this one? Zhaozhou answers, "because it carries karmic consciousness."

---

**10/**
So a reason existed. It's the later version, the one that became canonical, that strips the reason out and hands over the bare word with nothing attached.

---

**11/**
Wumen's own commentary, printed in the same 1228 text as the case, is unusually direct about what not to do with that word. "Day and night, take it up," he writes. "Don't understand it as blank nothingness. Don't understand it in terms of existence or non-existence."

---

**12/**
He compares it to having already swallowed a red-hot iron ball: however hard you try, it won't come back up.

---

**13/**
The instruction isn't to solve "Mu" as a riddle with a hidden correct reading. It's to hold the actual discomfort of not having an answer, for as long as it takes, instead of resolving it early with a clever guess.

---

**14/**
Picture the status meeting. Someone asks a question nobody in the room has enough information to answer, and someone answers it anyway, because silence reads as incompetence and a wrong-but-confident guess doesn't.

---

**15/**
Zhaozhou's answer worked precisely because he wasn't managing anyone's discomfort, including his own. He gave the flat no and let it sit there, unexplained, rather than dressing it up into something easier to accept.

---

**16/**
Zhaozhou gave a different monk the opposite answer, in that same earlier record, with a reason for that one too. The article picks it up there, finds two more places an open question gets closed early, then one where the unready decision is yours 👇
