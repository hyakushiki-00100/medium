**1/**
A man stands outside a cave, asking to study under the teacher meditating inside. The teacher, already years into a long, silent practice, doesn't turn around.

Later retellings say the man waited there through a snowstorm all night. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
What's better attested is what happened once he was finally let in.

He said: my mind is not at peace, please pacify it for me.

---

**3/**
The teacher answered: bring me this mind of yours, and I'll pacify it.

---

**4/**
The man was Huike (487–593), who would become the Second Patriarch of Chan after studying under Bodhidharma, the semi-legendary founder of the tradition in China. He came in his forties, already schooled in Confucian classics and Buddhist scripture, and stayed roughly six years.

---

**5/**
Worth being honest about the drama around all this. The detail everyone remembers, Huike cutting off his own arm in the snow to prove his sincerity, isn't well attested in the earliest sources.

---

**6/**
Some of the oldest biographical strands describe his arm as lost to bandits while he was later spreading the teaching elsewhere entirely, not severed at Bodhidharma's door. Others suggest the vivid snow scene may have absorbed details from a different monk's story.

---

**7/**
Modern scholars treat the self-severed arm as likely legendary embellishment rather than established fact.

---

**8/**
What is more consistently attested is the substance of the exchange, recorded in the Transmission of the Lamp (compiled 1004, roughly four centuries after Huike's death) and later as Case 41 of the Gateless Gate, a 1228 collection.

---

**9/**
Even Bodhidharma's own dates remain disputed among historians. The record for this period of Chan's founding is thin enough that precision itself should be handled carefully.

---

**10/**
One concrete detail is worth holding alongside the exchange. Before Huike ever arrived, Bodhidharma had reportedly spent nine years at Shaolin facing a wall in silent meditation, so still and unmoving that people called him "the wall-gazing brahmin."

---

**11/**
That account appears in the same Transmission of the Lamp that preserves the exchange. Whatever else here is legend, the image is consistent: a teacher who answers with almost nothing, because he'd already spent nine years finding out how little needed to be said.

---

**12/**
His reply to Huike isn't a trick, either. Huike came in treating his unease as a fixed thing: a problem with a shape, sitting somewhere inside him, that a powerful enough teacher could reach in and remove.

---

**13/**
Told to produce it, he went looking for the actual object. Not the feeling in general, but this exact unrest, right now, located somewhere specific.

---

**14/**
You can watch the same instinct at work. Dread before a hard conversation, a knot before a presentation: the reflex is to find a technique that removes it, the way you'd remove a splinter, rather than looking at what it's actually made of right now.

---

**15/**
Huike's unrest was real enough to carry him across a mountain range in winter, so none of this is a claim that anxiety is somehow fake.

---
