---

**1/**
A monk arrives at a temple in Tang China and is asked a single question: have you been here before?

He says yes. "Go have some tea," the teacher tells him. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
A second monk arrives and is asked the same question. He says no, he hasn't.

"Go have some tea," the teacher tells him too.

---

**3/**
The temple's administrator, listening, can't make sense of it. Isn't the entire point of the question to sort one answer from the other? He asks why both men got the identical instruction.

---

**4/**
The teacher was Zhaozhou Congshen (778–897), known in Japan as Jōshū, one of the most quoted figures in the Chan record. He didn't settle into a temple of his own until he was eighty, after decades testing himself against other teachers.

---

**5/**
He then taught for roughly forty years at Guanyin Temple, on the edge of what's now a county town in Hebei province: plain, flat farm country, not the mountain retreat the setting might suggest.

---

**6/**
The exchange appears in the Record of Zhaozhou and later in the Wudeng Huiyuan, a Song-dynasty compilation finished in 1252, more than three centuries after he died.

---

**7/**
Worth being honest: exchanges like this were written down generations later, in a genre built for compression, a few lines standing in for a longer, messier conversation.

---

**8/**
Even the final character carries a live dispute. The popular reading, the one carried into tea-ceremony practice, takes qu (去) as a bare emphatic particle, so the line lands as a plain "have some tea."

---

**9/**
More recent philological work argues the phrase originally meant something closer to "go drink tea and wake yourself up": a rebuke of dullness, not an invitation. Either way, the core doesn't move. Welcome or wake-up call, both monks got the same line.

---

**10/**
The natural assumption is that Zhaozhou's opening question sorted the two monks: the returning visitor gets one kind of welcome, the newcomer another. His answer refuses the calibration. The category they'd supplied turned out to be irrelevant to what they actually needed.

---

**11/**
His contemporaries taught through dramatic interruption. Linji used the shout, katsu, a more precise instrument than a raised voice but still a sudden jolt. Deshan hit students with a stick hard enough that it became a formal teaching method. Zhaozhou used neither.

---

**12/**
He answered almost everything in plain speech: tea, a washed bowl, a cypress tree in the courtyard. Later generations nicknamed his style kǒuchúnpí chán (口唇皮禅), "mouth-and-lip Chan". "Go have some tea" isn't an evasion. It's the whole method.

---

**13/**
Watch it happen in onboarding: new hires get one script, veterans get another, and the script often survives contact with someone it doesn't fit.
