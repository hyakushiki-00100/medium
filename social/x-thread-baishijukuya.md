---

**1/**
A monk climbs to a mountain hermitage to test the famous recluse living there. He carries one piece of news, and he expects it to land hard: your teacher has stopped teaching the thing that made you leave. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Years earlier the hermit, Damei Fachang, had put a single question to his teacher Mazu Daoyi: what is Buddha?

Mazu's answer, "this very mind is Buddha", landed so completely that Damei left for the mountains that day and never went back to ask anything else.

---

**3/**
That is the news the monk has come to deliver. These days Mazu teaches "no mind, no Buddha".

---

**4/**
Damei doesn't blink. "Let him teach whatever he wants," he says. "I'm sticking with 'this mind is Buddha.'"

---

**5/**
Two different documents are doing the work here. The original exchange, Mazu's answer, is genuinely Case 30 of the Gateless Gate, compiled by Wumen Huikai in 1228. But the case is just that one line.

---

**6/**
The later test on the mountain sits in a different document: Damei's biographical entry in the Transmission of the Lamp, presented to the Song court in 1004, itself over a century after Damei died.

---

**7/**
Worth knowing, too, that Wumen's own commentary on Case 30 isn't simple praise. He warns that Damei's line has misled plenty of people into treating it as a fixed marker.

---

**8/**
Anyone who really understood it, Wumen says, would want to cover his ears and walk off on hearing it recited yet again.

The wording has a minor wrinkle as well: the Gateless Gate has 即心是仏, later accounts of Damei's reply use 即心即仏. Same claim, different dress.

---

**9/**
So why did Mazu move the target? Not as a retraction. Other students had started reciting "this mind is Buddha" the way people recite any line repeated past the point of meaning: confidently, correctly, and with nothing much happening behind it.

---

**10/**
He changed the words specifically to break that habit, to force a moment of real confusion back into teaching that had calcified into a slogan.

It worked on the people who needed it.

---

**11/**
It had no effect on Damei, because Damei had never held the phrase as a slogan. He'd had one direct exchange, at a moment he badly needed an answer, and the words were simply the shape that arrival took.

---

**12/**
Even Wumen's warning about clinging to the phrase doesn't really land on him. Damei isn't defending four characters. He's reporting what's still true for him.

---

**13/**
He had the time to be sure. Damei Fachang (752–839) came to Chan practice in his twenties, didn't linger long as one of Mazu's many students, and spent roughly forty years on that mountain, deepening a realization that had arrived, by tradition's account, in one sentence.

---

**14/**
A respected voice changes their public stance, and the reflex is to shift with them, even when your own understanding never came from secondhand repetition to begin with. Updating the moment the room does is not the same as updating because you checked.

---

**15/**
Mazu's second answer wasn't wrong for the people who needed it. What actually matters is knowing which one you are, on any given belief: reciting a formula, or someone who tested it directly and found it held.
