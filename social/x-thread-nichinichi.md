# X thread — 日日是好日 (nichinichi kore kōnichi) — 元記事: articles/medium/nichinichi-en.md

元URL(CTA用): https://medium.com/@mk3372/he-challenged-the-room-to-say-something-about-the-day-after-the-fifteenth-no-one-could-57b17f195350

---

**1/**
A teacher puts a challenge to his assembled monks: I won't ask you about anything before the fifteenth. Say something about the days after it. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Nobody in the room speaks. He waits, then answers his own question, since no one else will: every day is a good day.

The teacher was Yunmen Wenyan (864–949), a dharma successor of Xuefeng Yicun and founder of his own teaching lineage.

---

**3/**
Silence, from a room full of monks who had trained for exactly this kind of moment, wasn't a small thing. It meant the question was harder than it sounded. Yunmen didn't scold anyone or explain the trick. He supplied the line himself, on their behalf, because it needed saying.

---

**4/**
The line survives in the Yunmen Guanglu, his recorded sayings compiled by his student Shoujian, in full: "I won't ask you about before the fifteenth. Say a phrase about after the fifteenth." Getting no answer, he supplied it himself: "Every day is a good day."

---

**5/**
The exchange was anthologized in 1125 as Case 6 of the Blue Cliff Record. What exactly "the fifteenth" refers to is genuinely unsettled. Zen scholarship offers several competing readings, and no version of the explanation is confirmed over the others.

---

**6/**
It may be the start of the traditional ninety-day retreat, or its end, or the fortnightly observance where monks confessed and reset together. What's consistent is the shape of the question: a marked day that counted, and an unmarked stretch after it that counted for less.

---

**7/**
Yunmen's line doesn't pick a side of that divide. It removes it. "Every day is a good day" isn't optimism about the days after the fifteenth being secretly fine. It refuses the premise that some days are pre-loaded with more significance than others.

---

**8/**
Every plan that begins "once things calm down" runs on the fifteenth. A backlog, a reorg, a launch: something is always about to make the coming stretch of ordinary days count less than the marked one on the other side of it.

---

**9/**
Yunmen's answer isn't a call to stop planning around real deadlines. The fifteenth was a real date, tied to a real practice. It's a check on whether the ordinary days around it are being treated as workable material or as a waiting room.

---

**10/**
Two more forms of waiting for the fifteenth are still unopened here, and so is the private version, the one that runs without any calendar involved. The piece closes by turning Yunmen's line on the two labels you've been sorting your own days into 👇
