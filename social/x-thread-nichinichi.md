# X thread — 日日是好日 (nichinichi kore kōnichi) — 元記事: articles/medium/nichinichi-en.md

元URL(CTA用): https://medium.com/@mk3372/he-challenged-the-room-to-say-something-about-the-day-after-the-fifteenth-no-one-could-57b17f195350

---

**1/**
A teacher puts a challenge to his assembled monks: I won't ask you about anything before the fifteenth. Say something about the days after it. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Nobody in the room speaks.

---

**3/**
He waits, then answers his own question, since no one else will: every day is a good day.

---

**4/**
The teacher was Yunmen Wenyan (864–949), a dharma successor of Xuefeng Yicun and founder of his own teaching lineage. He put the question to an audience that had presumably trained for exactly this kind of moment.

---

**5/**
Silence, from a room full of monks, wasn't a small thing. It meant the question was harder than it sounded. Yunmen didn't scold anyone or explain the trick. He simply supplied the line himself, on their behalf, because it needed saying.

---

**6/**
It survives in the Yunmen Guanglu, his recorded sayings compiled by his student Shoujian, in full: "I won't ask you about before the fifteenth. Say a phrase about after the fifteenth." Getting no answer, he supplied it himself: "Every day is a good day."

---

**7/**
The exchange became famous in its own right later, anthologized in 1125 as Case 6 of the Blue Cliff Record.

---

**8/**
What exactly "the fifteenth" refers to is genuinely unsettled. Zen scholarship offers several competing readings, and no version of the explanation is confirmed over the others.

---

**9/**
It may be the start of the traditional ninety-day retreat. Or its end. Or the closing ceremony that marked monks returning to ordinary schedules. Or the recurring fortnightly observance where the community confessed and reset together.

---

**10/**
What's consistent across all of them is the shape of the question. There was a marked day, one that counted for something, and an unmarked stretch of days after it that supposedly counted for less.

---

**11/**
Yunmen's line doesn't pick a side of that divide. It removes it. "Every day is a good day" isn't optimism about the days after the fifteenth being secretly fine too.

---

**12/**
It's a refusal of the whole premise that some days are pre-loaded with more significance than others. A marked date, a fresh quarter, a clean slate on a Monday: real on the calendar, imaginary as a difference in what a day actually is.

---

**13/**
Every plan that begins "once things calm down" runs on the fifteenth. A backlog, a reorg, a launch: something is always about to make the coming stretch of ordinary days count less than the marked one on the other side of it.

---

**14/**
Yunmen's answer isn't a call to stop planning around real deadlines. The fifteenth was a real date, tied to a real practice. It's a check on whether the ordinary days around it are being treated as workable material or as a waiting room.

---

**15/**
Two more forms of waiting for the fifteenth are still unopened here, and so is the private version, the one that runs without any calendar involved. The piece closes by turning Yunmen's line on the two labels you've been sorting your own days into 👇
