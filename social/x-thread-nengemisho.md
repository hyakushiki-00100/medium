**1/**
"We've always just gotten each other — no explaining needed."

Said like that, it sounds like a nice bit of chemistry.

Trace the phrase back to its Zen root, and you find something stranger: a flower, held up in silence, that Buddhist scholars now think never happened. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
The story: the Buddha was giving a sermon on Vulture Peak. Instead of speaking, he simply lifted a flower and turned it slowly in his hand, showing it to the crowd.

No one understood. Except one disciple, Mahakasyapa, who quietly smiled.

---

**3/**
The Buddha is said to have responded: "I possess the treasury of the true dharma eye, the sublime mind of nirvana — and I now entrust it to Mahakasyapa." No sermon. No scripture. No further words at all.

---

**4/**
This is the origin of nenge-mishō (拈華微笑): "holding up a flower, a subtle smile."

---

**5/**
Here's the honest part. The scripture long cited as the source, the Sutra of the Questions of Brahma King, is now widely regarded by scholars as apocryphal: composed in China long after the fact, not an authentic record of the Buddha's teaching.

---

**6/**
Earlier Buddhist accounts even place Mahakasyapa far from the Buddha's side at the time of his death, learning of it only a week later. Historically speaking, the scene likely never took place.

---

**7/**
The story doesn't settle into Zen literature until around 1183, roughly 1,700 years after the Buddha's lifetime.

---

**8/**
Which suggests something more interesting than a historical record: this is a story later generations distilled out of the value Zen cares about most. Whatever actually happened on Vulture Peak, what Zen carried forward was never the event, but the teaching the story holds.

---

**9/**
Zen holds another idea alongside this story: furyū-monji, kyōge-betsuden (不立文字・教外別伝), the truth cannot be fully carried by words and letters alone. Nenge-mishō became the symbol that embodies this stance.

---

**10/**
You can feel this instinct in unexpected places. A single flower, placed in the alcove of a tea room. Not an arrangement that explains itself. Just one flower, set down quietly, leaving the rest to what the other person notices.

---

**11/**
Here's where the phrase gets genuinely useful: most of us have expected a colleague to "just understand" without being told, and been disappointed when they didn't.

---

**12/**
But think again about Mahakasyapa. He wasn't able to smile because he was unusually sharp. He was able to smile because of years spent training beside the Buddha. The flower didn't create that understanding. It simply lit a fuse that had already been laid.

---

**13/**
One question worth sitting with before assuming someone should "just get it": am I explaining this for them, or for my own unease? More slides often signal anxiety about being misunderstood, not an actual information gap.
