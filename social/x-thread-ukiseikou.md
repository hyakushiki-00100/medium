**1/**
It's raining. Your plans are "ruined."

Except — the rain didn't ruin anything. That verdict came from you, a split second after the fact.

An 11th-century poem about a mountain has something sharper to say about this than most productivity advice. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
The phrase is ukiseikou (雨奇晴好), from a poem by Su Dongpo (1037–1101) about Mount Lu.

Rain makes the mountain strange and wonderful (奇, ki).
Clear skies make it beautiful (好, kou).

Either way — good.

---

**3/**
Read at face value, it sounds like simple consolation: "rainy days aren't so bad."

But the real question underneath is sharper: who, exactly, is attaching the label "good" or "bad" to a fact you had no control over in the first place?

---

**4/**
It's raining. That's a fact.

"My plans are ruined" is not the fact. It's an interpretation stacked on top of it — one of several available.

The same rain supports "today is ruined" just as easily as "today has its own particular charm."

---

**5/**
Since the weather itself isn't going to change, the only thing actually available to choose is which interpretation you pick up.

---

**6/**
Where this shows up at work:

→ A meeting gets rescheduled last-minute. "Wasted time" and "caught in time" are two readings of the same event — the event itself can't be undone.

---

**7/**
→ Market conditions turn against the plan. The downturn is given. Whether you notice what's only visible from inside it — a competitor pulling back, a chance to rebuild costs — depends on which side you're looking at.

---

**8/**
→ A teammate's illness derails a handoff.

"The schedule fell apart" and "we just found out how much this depended on one person" are two readings of the exact same event.

---

**9/**
Not a demand for forced optimism. Not pretending the downside doesn't exist.

Just the observation that most events have more than one face — and which face gets your attention is a choice you actually get to make.

---

**10/**
Two questions worth asking:

1) In a situation I'm reading as "bad" — where does the unchangeable fact end, and where does my own interpretation begin?
2) Looking at that same fact, what's a different angle (奇) worth noticing?
