**1/**
Several times a day, a monk named Ruiyan Shiyan (better known by the Japanese reading of his name, Zuigan) would stop whatever he was doing and call out to himself: "Master!"

Then answer his own call: "Yes."

He did this for years, apparently without embarrassment. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
The full exchange: "Master!" "Yes." "Are you awake?" "Yes." "Don't ever let anyone fool you." "Yes, yes."

---

**3/**
The word he used for himself, shujinkō (主人公), "master" or more literally "the one in charge", has become one of Zen's more famous single-word koans. The question it aims at: who, exactly, is running your day?

---

**4/**
Zuigan's own dates were never recorded. What's known is that he trained under Yantou Quanhuo, who died in 887, placing him in the late Tang.

---

**5/**
The exchange survives as Case 12 of the Gateless Gate, compiled in 1228, well over three centuries after he is said to have lived it. Read it as the shape a practice took in the tradition's memory, not a verified transcript of any particular afternoon.

---

**6/**
Shujinkō is worth sitting with. In ordinary Japanese it now just means "protagonist," the main character of a novel or film. Zuigan's usage is sharper: not the one things happen to, but the one in charge of the household of your attention.

---

**7/**
Most of a day runs without a master present. You open a laptop and let the inbox set the agenda. You react to a notification before deciding whether it deserves one. You inherit a mood from the last conversation and carry it, unexamined, into the next.

---

**8/**
None of this requires you. A sufficiently trained set of habits can drive the whole performance. Zuigan's practice interrupts exactly that: a repeated moment confirming someone is actually steering.

---

**9/**
"Don't ever let anyone fool you" lands harder once you notice it isn't about con artists. The people most likely to talk you out of your own judgment are the ones with the most access to you.

---

**10/**
A boss's offhand comment. A family expectation never examined. An old version of yourself you've outgrown but kept following out of habit. Being fooled, in this reading, mostly looks like agreement you never actually reached on your own.

---

**11/**
Start with the inbox that runs you instead of the reverse. Before opening it, a two-second check: master, are you here? If not, the inbox will happily make every decision for you today.

---

**12/**
What makes Zuigan's practice unusual isn't the content. Plenty of traditions recommend self-awareness. It's the frequency. He asked several times a day, treating presence as a habit that decays without maintenance, not a fact established once.

---
