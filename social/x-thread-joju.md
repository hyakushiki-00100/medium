---

**1/**
A man hangs from a tree branch by his teeth, over a thousand-foot drop. No hands, no feet, nothing to grip but that bite.

Someone below asks him a serious question. Speak, and he falls. Stay silent, and he's failed them. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Xiangyan Zhixian, the monk who once burned his own library after a question he couldn't answer, posed this scene to his own students.

The earliest surviving record dates to 1004, about a century after his death, not the three centuries the famous later version implies.

---

**3/**
That leaner, later version is Case 5 of the Gateless Gate (1228). It's the one most people quote today. It drops two things the earlier record kept: the exact height of the drop, and what happened right after Xiangyan finished posing the riddle.

---

**4/**
In the earlier record, a monk named Zhao stood up. He didn't try to solve the puzzle on its own terms:

"I'm not asking about the moment you're already up the tree. What about before you climbed it?"

Xiangyan just laughed and said nothing more.

---

**5/**
Wumen's own commentary doesn't solve it for the reader either. "Even eloquence that flows like a river is useless here," he writes. Answer properly, and you give life to the dead. Fail, and go ask Maitreya — the next Buddha, not due for billions of years.

---

**6/**
Most people spend their first pass hunting for a clever third option — some answer that's neither speech nor silence.

That instinct misses where the trap actually sits. It isn't the two exits. It's the teeth.

---

**7/**
At work, this has an ordinary shape: telling a superior something they don't want to hear, at real risk to your standing, versus staying quiet while a bad decision goes through. Framed that way, both options look like a fall.

---

**8/**
What's actually being gripped in that moment usually isn't the truth of the situation. It's how the messenger will be seen for delivering it.

---
