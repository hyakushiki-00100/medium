**1/**
For years, an old man sat at the back of Baizhang's¹ dharma talks. Every time, he filed out with everyone else. Then one day he didn't.

Baizhang asked who he was. The answer: not a monk at all, but a fox spirit, five hundred lifetimes into a sentence he couldn't end. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Long before, he'd been the abbot of this same mountain. A student once asked: does someone who has trained deeply still fall under cause and effect? He answered, "No — a person of that depth doesn't fall into cause and effect." For that sentence, he became a fox, life after life.

---

**3/**
He'd come to Baizhang's talks hoping for a way out. He finally asked for a turning word, and put to Baizhang the very question a student had once put to him. Baizhang answered: "Not blind to cause and effect." At those four words, he was released on the spot.

---

**4/**
The two lines look almost identical, and that's the point. "Doesn't fall into cause and effect" claims an exemption. "Not blind to cause and effect" claims nothing of the kind. It says only that nothing about consequence is hidden from you. One is denial. The other is full sight.

---

**5/**
A correction worth making: the earliest known written version dates to 1036, roughly two centuries after Baizhang² died, not the text most people cite. The Gateless Gate³ (1228), which made the story famous, came some four centuries after his lifetime.

---

**6/**
Even Wumen's own verse on the case refuses to let the distinction sit too neatly, calling the two phrases "two faces of the same throw of dice" rather than simply right and wrong. Later teachers have read the pairing in conflicting ways for centuries since.

---

**7/**
After the exchange, the old man told Baizhang plainly where his body lay, asking for a monk's funeral rites. Confused, since no one was sick, the monks followed Baizhang to the far side of the mountain, where he drew out a dead fox with his staff and had it cremated as asked.

---

**8/**
The old man's error has a familiar modern shape: enough seniority, enough success, enough good intentions, and the chain from action to consequence starts to feel optional. A leader assumes a strong track record buys room to cut a corner now.

---

**9/**
"I didn't mean it that way" deployed to cancel an actual impact rather than explain it. Expertise mistaken for immunity from the same failure modes that catch everyone else. None of this is malice. It's the same sentence the old man gave, spoken more quietly: depth exempts me.
