**1/**
Look into a mirror and a flower appears in it. Look at the surface of a pond and the full moon floats there.

Both are unmistakably visible. But reach for either and your hand closes on nothing.

Your like count works exactly the same way. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
That image gives us the four-character phrase kyōka-suigetsu (鏡花水月): "mirror-flower, water-moon."

Surprisingly, its earliest clear source isn't a Zen text at all.

---

**3/**
It comes from the Ming-dynasty poet Xie Zhen's treatise on poetry, the Shijia Zhishuo:

"Poetry has what can be explained and what cannot — like the flower in the mirror, the moon on the water. Don't get stuck chasing after its traces."

---

**4/**
Trace it back further and an earlier form, "water-moon, mirror-image," already appears in a Tang-dynasty stele inscription by Pei Xiu. Over the following centuries the phrase widened from a comment on poetry into a broader metaphor for the fleetingness of life itself.

---

**5/**
One honest note: why Zen adopted the phrase so readily is my own reading, not an established fact. Its stance of don't get stuck chasing after what you can only see, never hold resonates with Zen's own teaching on non-attachment. That's an inference, not a documented lineage.

---

**6/**
There's a place you can actually stand inside this phrase. Kyōko-chi (鏡湖池), the "mirror lake pond", is the pond in the garden of Kinkaku-ji, Kyoto's Golden Pavilion.

---

**7/**
On a still, clear day, the golden pavilion appears whole and unbroken on the water's surface, the famous "upside-down Kinkaku."

Worth flagging: there's no documented record the pond's name comes from this phrase. That link is my own observation, not verified etymology.

---

**8/**
The underlying structure is identical either way: something beautiful, visible on the water, and completely unreachable.

---

**9/**
This lands hard in an age of screens. Like counts, follower numbers, the curated slice of someone else's life scrolling past — all of it is a flower reflected in a mirror. Genuinely visible. None of it can be reached and taken hold of.

---

**10/**
Here's the tricky part: the mirror-flower isn't the problem. There's nothing wrong with finding it beautiful. The trouble starts the moment you try to grab it, even willing to break the mirror to get at it.

---

**11/**
Same logic applies to recognition at work: praise, ratings, approval are real in that they visibly exist, but none of them can be permanently pocketed. The very effort of chasing something ungraspable often gets praised from the outside as "hustle."
