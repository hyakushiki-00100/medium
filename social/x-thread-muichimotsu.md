**1/**
A seventh-century Chinese monastery needs a new abbot. Hongren, the current one, sets the test: write a verse on the wall showing what you understand.

The senior monk writes the expected one. Then a second appears beside it, from a kitchen worker who can't write his name. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
The senior monk was Shenxiu, and he wrote something careful and effortful. The body is a bodhi tree. The mind is a mirror on a stand. Polish it constantly, don't let dust collect.

Everyone assumed that settled it.

---

**3/**
The second verse went the other way. There was never a tree. The mirror was never mounted on a stand. There was nowhere for dust to land.

---

**4/**
It belonged to Huineng (638–713), who would become the Sixth Patriarch of Chan and the central figure of the Platform Sutra, one of the few Buddhist texts composed by someone born in China rather than translated from Sanskrit.

---

**5/**
Before he arrived, he had supported his mother by selling firewood. Once there, said to be illiterate, he was put in the kitchen to pound rice: eight months of plain labor before the contest ever came up. Tradition holds someone else wrote his lines on the wall for him.

---

**6/**
Worth being honest about the story. Modern scholars, including John McRae and Philip Yampolsky, treat the entire verse contest with real skepticism. Some read it as a later construction, quite possibly shaped by Huineng's own disciple Shenhui.

---

**7/**
The motive would have been to establish his teacher's Southern school over Shenxiu's rival Northern school. Even the illiteracy, the detail that makes the story land, is disputed: some accounts describe Huineng as coming from a locally established family, not genuine poverty.

---

**8/**
The most-quoted line has a documented edit behind it. The words "originally not a single thing exists, so where could dust ever land" come from later editions of the Platform Sutra, compiled generations after Huineng died.

---

**9/**
The oldest surviving copy, a manuscript found at Dunhuang and dated close to his own era, has a different third line: "Buddha-nature is always pure". What we're reading is a text shaped by people with a stake in the outcome, not a neutral transcript.

---

**10/**
Set the history question aside and the two verses still do different work. Shenxiu's describes a maintenance program: you have something valuable, and the job is constant upkeep, wiping away whatever settles on it before it accumulates.

---

**11/**
It isn't bad advice. Most self-improvement writing today is a version of it: build the habit, defend the routine, stay vigilant.

Huineng's answer doesn't improve on the maintenance plan. It removes the object being maintained.

---

**12/**
Take away the stand, and there's nothing fixed for the mirror to rest on. Nothing for dust to land, nothing that needs your constant supervision.

---

**13/**
The most obvious version at work is the performance of having it together. A lot of professional effort goes into the constant upkeep of an image, competent, calm, on top of things, rather than into the work itself. That upkeep is real labor.

---

**14/**
None of this argues for carelessness. It argues for checking whether what you're defending so vigilantly actually needs the defense.

---

**15/**
Hongren never announced the winner out loud. What he did after dark instead is where the story actually turns. The article has that, the two remaining mirrors people keep polishing at work, and the question all of this puts to your own self-image 👇
