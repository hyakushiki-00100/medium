# X thread — 百尺竿頭進一歩 (hyakushaku kantō shin ippo) — 元記事: articles/medium/kanto-en.md

元URL(CTA用): https://medium.com/@mk3372/a-monk-whod-already-reached-the-top-asked-what-came-next-4a48debee94b

---

**1/**
A monk who has already done the hard work, who has reached what the tradition calls the top of a hundred-foot pole, asks his teacher the obvious next question: how do you go any further from here?

There's nowhere left to climb. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
The teacher doesn't offer a technique, a stage beyond this one, or a warning about complacency. He names a mountain range and a river, real places in the region around them.

---

**3/**
His answer: "The mountains of Langzhou, the waters of Lizhou."

Real, specific places, named as if he'd been asked a geography question.

---

**4/**
The image behind the question is deliberately impossible. A hundred-foot pole has no rung above its top, and no ordinary step forward from there is a step at all. It's a fall.

---

**5/**
That's the whole difficulty the monk is naming. He hasn't failed to arrive. He's arrived, and arrival turns out not to be where the problem ends.

---

**6/**
A simple case for climbing higher would have ended right there. This one keeps going because the climbing itself is eventually what gets questioned.

---

**7/**
The teacher was Changsha Jingcen (788–868), a dharma-brother of Zhaozhou under Nanquan Puyuan, and one of the major teaching figures of Tang-dynasty Hunan.

---

**8/**
The record on this koan is worth following closely, because the most-cited version turns out to be the thinnest one.

---

**9/**
The earliest source, the Jingde Chuandenglu, compiled in 1004, records both a verse and a full exchange together, inside Changsha's own biographical entry.

---

**10/**
The verse: "The one who sits unmoving at the top of a hundred-foot pole has entered, but it isn't yet real. From the top of the pole, you must take a step — and the ten directions become your whole body."

---

**11/**
Case 46 of the Mumonkan, compiled in 1228 by Wumen Huikai, takes only the verse, with the wording shifted slightly. It strips off Changsha's name and attributes the lines instead to "an ancient worthy".

---

**12/**
It then pairs that verse with a separate question from a different teacher, Shishuang Qingzhu. The scene, and the teacher who actually answered it, both disappear in the version most people now know.

---

**13/**
Case 79 of the Congronglu, compiled a few years earlier still, in 1223–24 by Wansong Xingxiu, keeps the attribution and gives the full exchange its own formal case. It sits closer to the original record than the more famous version that followed.

---

**14/**
The more famous a line becomes, it turns out, the more it's worth tracing back to where it actually came from.

---

**15/**
Experts run out of moves this way. Someone masters a domain completely enough that every remaining problem feels like a variation on ones they've already solved, and the growth quietly stops. Not from laziness, but from running out of the kind of problem that got them here.

---

**16/**
"Further" carries real force while you're still growing. The trouble starts only once it becomes the only direction you know how to look.

---

**17/**
The monk didn't follow that answer, so Changsha added one more line, and that second one is where the koan lands. The article carries the exchange to its finish, then finds two more heights people stop climbing, and the step off your own pole 👇
