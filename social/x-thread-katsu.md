**1/**
"I'll light a fire under them." "They need a good talking-to."

Say something like that and you're picturing volume: a raised voice, a hard face.

The Zen shout underneath that instinct started somewhere else entirely. It has almost nothing to do with anger. 🧵👇

---

**2/**
Katsu (喝) is most closely tied to Linji Yixuan (d. 866), the Tang-dynasty master who founded the school known in Japan as Rinzai. His teaching was famously physical: a sudden, wordless "Katsu!" delivered at the exact moment a student had gotten lost inside his own reasoning.

---

**3/**
Zen even keeps a stock phrase for its two great interruptive styles: "Linji's shout, Deshan's stick" (the master Deshan Xuanjian taught with a blow of his staff instead).

---

**4/**
A student often arrived with a clever answer already loaded: a concept to defend, a position rehearsed, a quotation to deploy. The shout landed before any of it could be spent.

---

**5/**
The aim was never to frighten. It was to cut, to sever, for a single instant, the endless commentary running in a student's head, and drop him back into the bare present, the moment before thought.

---

**6/**
Worth being honest about the source: the Record of Linji (臨済録), where all of this comes from, reached its standard form in 1120, roughly 250 years after Linji's death, shaped by later hands. We're reading a distilled teaching, not a transcript.

---

**7/**
Here's the part that undoes the "just yell" reading. Linji himself sorted katsu into four kinds, and only one of them is loud.

---

**8/**
One like a vajra sword: cutting cleanly through confusion.

One like a golden-haired lion crouching low: pure poised presence, before any movement.

---

**9/**
Notice that only the first of those is anything like a raised voice. In Linji's hands a shout was a whole vocabulary, not a volume setting.

---

**10/**
Most of the "katsu" delivered in offices is the crude kind: louder feedback, a sharper tone, aimed at motivation. It mostly manufactures fear. Fear produces careful people, not awake ones.

---

**11/**
The real katsu is smaller and better aimed: one well-timed question that stops a colleague, or yourself, mid-rationalization. Delivered once, at the right moment, it does what an hour of raised voices cannot.

---

**12/**
Two of the four shouts are missing here, including the one that isn't a shout at all and matters most for anyone who leads. So is the actual question worth asking mid-rationalization, and the version you turn on yourself 👇
